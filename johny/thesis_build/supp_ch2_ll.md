## 2.5 The Living Lab in depth: definitions, typologies, case experience and scaling

Section 2.4 introduced the Living Lab and its combination with systems thinking at the level needed to state the gap. This section treats the concept more closely, because the study's central construct — the systemic Living Lab — inherits both the strengths and the pathologies of this literature.

### 2.5.1 Definitions across fields

The definition of a Living Lab varies with the field that uses it, and the variation is not cosmetic. Reviews of the literature find the concept applied in information and communication technology, product development, health, public administration, urban planning and, more recently, energy, with definitions differing in what they treat as primary: the space, the method, the network of actors, or the innovation outcome (Hossain et al., 2019; Mbatha and Musango, 2022). What the definitions share is a small set of invariants: innovation is developed with end-users in a real-life setting rather than tested on them afterwards; multiple kinds of organisation participate; and the activity is iterative, with cycles of design, use and revision rather than a single passage from prototype to release (Bergvall-Kåreborn and Ståhlbröst, 2009; Leminen et al., 2012).

Two definitional positions matter for the present argument. The first is the methodological definition offered by Dell'Era and Landoni, for whom the Living Lab is "a design research methodology aimed at co-creating innovation through the involvement of aware users in a real-life setting" (Dell'Era and Landoni, 2014). Its importance is the word aware: users are not passive sources of requirements but participants who know what they are doing, which is the position this study takes. The second is the characterisation of Living Labs as physical or virtual spaces in which stakeholders collaborate on societal challenges (Hossain et al., 2019). Its importance is the admission of virtual and mobile constitution, which matters for a lab that must operate where land, premises and services are informal.

For this study, a working definition is synthesised from the reviewed corpus rather than adopted from any single source: a Living Lab is an organised, iterative arrangement in which end-users and other stakeholders co-create and test innovations together in a real-life setting, with the explicit intention that what is learned changes both the innovation and the arrangement. The end-user-centred emphasis is deliberate: it names whose participation is constitutive of the method.

### 2.5.2 Typologies and classifications

The literature classifies Living Labs along several dimensions, and the classifications are consequential because they harden into defaults. The best-known classification is by the actor that drives the lab: a lab may be driven by the provider of infrastructure, the utiliser of results, the enabler who funds and convenes, or the users themselves (Leminen et al., 2012). The classification matters because the driver shapes what the lab optimises for: a provider-driven lab optimises for the deployment of the provider's technology, a utiliser-driven lab for the production of knowledge its sponsor can use, and a user-driven lab for the usefulness of the outcome to the participants. A second dimension is constitution: labs may be physically situated, wholly virtual, or mobile enough to follow the innovation (Hossain et al., 2019). A third is sector and purpose, in which the energy applications form a small and recent subset of a literature dominated by ICT and urban innovation (Mbatha and Musango, 2022).

### 2.5.3 Case experience across sectors

The reviewed corpus documents Living Lab implementations across many sectors and settings, and the synthesis of that documentation is instructive even where individual cases cannot be generalised. The systematic review by Hossain et al. (2019) finds a literature dominated by descriptions of single labs, with comparatively little comparison, longitudinal follow-up or evaluation against stated outcomes; the labs described are concentrated in European and other high-income contexts, and the energy domain is among the least represented. The review of energy-sector applications by Mbatha and Musango (2022) reaches a consistent conclusion for this domain: applications exist, their practices vary widely, and many were not designed with continuation, maintenance or scaling in view. Work on urban living labs in European sustainability transitions shows what labs can realistically change: through embedding in local institutions, through structured learning among participants, and through the networks the lab creates, a lab can influence its wider regime — but the influence is conditional on those connections having been built, and does not follow from the demonstration alone (von Wirth et al., 2019).

Three lessons for the present study follow. First, a documented case is the realistic assessment unit: the study can contribute a carefully documented, methodologically explicit case, and must not claim to establish that the model works everywhere. Second, the mechanisms by which a lab has effects beyond itself are institutional, not technical, which is why governance, ownership and exit are components of the framework in Chapter 4 rather than afterthoughts. Third, most documented weaknesses are designable: the fragmentation, the missing follow-up and the absent continuation planning are choices the framework can refuse to make.

### 2.5.4 Living Labs in the energy sector

Within the energy applications that do exist, two lines of work anchor the present study. The first is the design and operation of living labs for energy technology innovation in poor urban environments described by Mukama et al. (2022), which demonstrates the practical steps of assembling stakeholders, running co-design activities with household end-users, and developing an energy innovation against real conditions of use; the associated co-design toolkit supports the multi-stakeholder collaboration such labs require (Petrulaityte et al., 2022). The second is the modelling line of work showing how innovation and the security of energy services interact as a system in these environments (Batinge et al., 2022), together with the analysis frameworks that make household roles, burdens and knowledge visible in the design process (Anditi et al., 2022; Musango et al., 2020).

What the energy-sector literature still lacks, taken as a whole, is the combination this study is organised around: an explicit systems architecture in which the lab's boundary, feedback and evaluation are derived from theory; an end-user-centred participation design; justice criteria applied to the lab's own operation; and a continuation plan treated as a design component rather than a closing remark. The energy labs documented to date tend to be strong on engagement practice and weak on the systemic and institutional variables (Mbatha and Musango, 2022; von Wirth et al., 2019). These observations sharpen the gap statement of Section 2.9 rather than replacing it.

### 2.5.5 Real-world testing, evaluation and scaling

The living lab's defining promise is that innovations are tested in use, in the conditions where they must ultimately work. The reviewed literature suggests both sides of the promise. On the productive side, real-world testing surfaces the non-technical requirements — the water, the space, the labour, the tenure rules, the cost structure — that a laboratory setting hides, and it allows revision during use rather than after failure. On the demanding side, real-world testing makes the evaluation problem harder: outcomes are entangled with everything else that changes in a settlement, so a lab that cannot document its own interventions cannot attribute its results.

The scaling question is where the literature is weakest. Scaling is often written as though it followed from demonstrated success. The evidence reviewed here points the other way: labs scale, where they scale at all, through the mechanisms von Wirth et al. (2019) identify — embedding in institutions, learning transferred to the actors who will operate the next installation, and networks that carry the model — and each of these mechanisms has to be built. For the present study, the implication is stated in the framework itself: exit, durability and scaling are planned from the start as a component of the lab (Chapter 4), not as an epilogue to it. The design of the planned evaluation, and the criteria against which the lab will judge itself, are set out in Chapters 4 and 5.
