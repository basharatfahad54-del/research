# 9. Conclusion

## 9.1 What this study has argued

This document has set out a complete research proposal in thesis format. Its argument can be restated in one paragraph. Energy poverty in South African informal settlements persists not because households lack devices or opinions but because the socio-material configuration around energy fails them: the fuel, the infrastructure, the dwelling, the cost structure, the labour and the rules do not hold together, and the burdens of that failure fall unevenly on the household members who use the technology most. Existing methods of innovation treat the artefact as the object of design and the household as a source of attitudes, and they therefore reproduce the failure. The study proposes an alternative in which the configuration is the unit of design: a Systemic Living Lab for a Just Energy Transition (SETILL), in which end-users participate as co-designers in a real-world setting, the lab's own boundaries and feedback are derived from systems theory, and its operation is judged against energy justice criteria as well as technical performance. The proposal is developed through an end-user-centred approach — the household members who use, maintain, pay for and abandon the technology are the primary participants — with gender retained as an analytically necessary axis, following the established line of South African work on the security of energy services in poor urban environments (Musango et al., 2020; Batinge et al., 2022).

The proposal answers five research questions, stated verbatim in Chapter 1. The first establishes a conceptual frame that treats inequality, energy security, energy poverty, innovation and informal settlements as one configuration rather than five variables. The second and third determine the factors, cultural elements and mechanisms that shape end-user participation and acceptance. The fourth identifies mechanisms for improving both. The fifth tests whether a Systems Living Lab built through participatory design can address energy poverty and enhance the security of energy services in a real-world setting. None of these questions can be answered from the desk. All five require the fieldwork planned in Chapter 5, the co-design and pilot phases planned in Chapter 7, and the evaluation and writing phases that complete the 36-month plan.

## 9.2 The expected contribution, restated

Four contributions are claimed, each bounded and each assessable against the evidence the study will produce, as set out in Chapter 6. The theoretical contribution is a posthumanist account of acceptance as an emergent property of a socio-material configuration, with a derivation of the systemic character of a Living Lab from boundary critique rather than assertion. The methodological contribution is the documented combination of reflexive thematic analysis, situational mapping, causal loop diagramming and diffractive reading, together with the operationalisation of energy justice as indicators at project scale. The practical contribution is a design protocol that treats the configuration as the unit of intervention, and evidence on the conditions under which community-scale biogas is sustained. The policy contribution is a set of justice criteria with which municipal energy programmes and engagement processes can be assessed. The study does not claim to discover a new theory, to solve energy poverty, or to produce a technology that works everywhere. It claims a carefully documented case, a framework that can fail, and an honest account of what both produce.

## 9.3 Limitations

The limitations acknowledged in Chapter 5 are restated here because they discipline every claim in this document. The study is a proposal: no fieldwork has been conducted, no data exist, and every statement about the settlement, the participants and the outcomes is either an intention or a planning assumption. The design examines one settlement, one technology and a three-year window; it does not model the national system, evaluate the life-cycle impacts of biogas or estimate willingness to pay. It aims for analytical rather than statistical generalisation, in the sense defended in Chapter 1. The single-site design limits transferability, and the co-design process — the study's central method — makes the researcher part of the phenomenon studied, so that the reflexive and intervention records of the lab are not optional rigour but the condition on which any finding becomes interpretable. Within those limits, the design is adequate to the questions it poses; beyond them, its claims are deliberately silent.

## 9.4 The work that follows

The next steps are those of the plan already set out: the registration of the proposal with the faculty, the submission and review of the ethics application, the completion of the site selection memorandum against the criteria in Chapter 5, and the first phase of the descriptive study. The supervisor's research programme provides concepts, instruments and access, and the study is designed to test that programme's frameworks hard rather than merely apply them. The distance between the argument in this document and the evidence that would support it is the work of the next three years, and the plan in Chapter 7 is the schedule of that work.
