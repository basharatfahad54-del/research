## 4.8 SETILL in practice: definition, structure, stakeholders and operations

The preceding sections specified the framework as a set of components and relationships. This section turns the framework into an operating proposition: what the lab is called, how it is structured, which stakeholders it convenes, what each contributes, and how the whole is set in motion. The content is design, not description: the lab does not yet exist, and everything in this section is written as an intention to be tested by the study's execution.

### 4.8.1 A working definition of SETILL

SETILL — the Systemic Living Lab for a Just Energy Transition — is defined for this study as a physical, virtual or mobile research and design space in which energy innovations are co-created and tested through the involvement of end-users in a real-life setting, organised by systems thinking, and directed at the security of energy services in poor households. The definition is built deliberately on the definitional invariants established in the literature review (Section 2.5.1), with four commitments added that make it systemic in the sense argued in Chapter 3. The lab undertakes, through its operation: to understand the material cultures, cognitive norms and energy practices that shape end-users' adoption of energy innovations; to bring end-users' knowledge and participation into the design of energy technology innovation; to develop and test an energy innovation to pilot scale together with those end-users; and to strengthen the security of energy services for poor households while making the distribution of costs and benefits visible and contestable.

Within the end-user-centred approach, gender is retained as a necessary axis of analysis rather than discarded with the earlier gender-focused framing. The framework takes all household end-users as participants in the design, while making the different roles, burdens and knowledge of household members visible wherever the evidence requires it, using the analysis tools established in the research lineage the study extends (Musango et al., 2020; Anditi et al., 2022). The shift is one of primary framing: from a lab defined by who is underrepresented to a lab defined by who uses the technology — which, in practice, includes the same households.

The stakeholder column names the people-public-private partnership explicitly because stakeholder configurations in energy labs are usually under-specified (Mbatha and Musango, 2022); the management and vision rows state the continuation arrangement from the outset because documented labs rarely do so (Mbatha and Musango, 2022; von Wirth et al., 2019); and the research-approach row binds the lab to systems thinking as architecture rather than vocabulary, which is the study's central claim.

### 4.8.3 Stakeholders and their roles

Stakeholder collaboration is constitutive of the Living Lab method rather than incidental to it: labs are arrangements for collaboration across organisational boundaries, and their classifications are literally classifications of who drives the collaboration (Leminen et al., 2012). The energy-sector literature identifies stakeholder configuration and role clarity among the factors that decide whether an energy lab functions (Mbatha and Musango, 2022), and the participation literature supplies the warning that collaboration without attention to power becomes consultation in disguise (Arnstein, 1969). SETILL therefore specifies its stakeholder set and the role of each part of it, and the specification is treated as revisable: the boundary critique (C1) revisits the stakeholder map at each cycle, and new stakeholders may join or leave as the work develops.

Five categories are proposed, and each is described in the role the study intends for it.

The community and end-users are the primary participants. The households of the settlement are the addressees of the innovation and the holders of most of the knowledge about conditions of use; under the end-user-centred approach they participate as co-designers and co-researchers rather than as respondents to someone else's design (Sanders and Stappers, 2008). Their participation is made materially possible by the participation architecture (C3) — timing, venue, language, transport and childcare — because an invitation that the rhythms of the settlement cannot accommodate is not participation. Within this category the study keeps the gender-differentiated reading of roles, burdens and knowledge that the research lineage established (Musango et al., 2020; Anditi et al., 2022), while addressing all household end-users as participants. Community organisations and local leaders participate as stakeholders in their own right, with interests that may differ from those of individual households; the framework treats those differences as data (C2) rather than assuming a single community voice.

Government and policy-makers participate at municipal and, where relevant, provincial and national level. Their roles in the intended collaboration are the regulatory and enabling ones: the legality of installations, the alignment of the intervention with municipal planning, the potential extension of a working arrangement to other settlements, and the financing routes that a durable service requires. The framework treats government as a stakeholder with its own interests — among them service delivery obligations, budget discipline and reputational risk — rather than as a neutral arbiter, and records its positions in the same evidence register as all other contributions.

Technology developers and energy industry partners supply the technical capability that the community cannot be expected to hold: design engineering, components, construction supervision and the training of local operators. Their role is defined by the configuration principle of the framework (C4): the industry partner engineers the arrangement, not only the appliance, and the specification it works to is produced and agreed in the co-design process with end-users rather than handed down. Energy service providers are included in the same category where the service model involves them — the supply relationship, the tariff and the maintenance route are part of the design.

The researchers organise the process. Their role is facilitation, methodological discipline and knowledge integration: maintaining the evidence register, running the co-design and analysis cycles, holding the lab to its own evaluation criteria, and making the lab's own interventions visible and disputable (C9). The researchers hold no design authority over the community's decisions; their authority is procedural, and the boundary critique (C1) keeps even that under review. Where the researchers bring theory to the table — systems thinking, the justice criteria, the framework itself — they are accountable for its fit with the accounts of the settlement.

Funders and institutions provide the resources without which the design cannot be built: the host institution at the University of Johannesburg, external funders, and any industry partners contributing in kind. Their interests — financial and reputational — are stated at the outset and managed through the governance component (C5), so that the arrangement's ownership and its continuation are not quietly settled by whoever paid for it.

### 4.8.4 A rich picture of the problem situation

As a first structuring device, the study will construct a rich picture of the problem situation with the stakeholders named above. The rich picture is the soft systems convention for representing a situation as its actors, their interconnections, their concerns and the systems that shape them, drawn as a single annotated depiction rather than a diagram of causation (Checkland, 1981). Its purpose here is alignment: the picture will be drawn collaboratively during the scoping phase, so that the stakeholders' differing understandings of the situation — what the problem is, who is involved, what has been tried — are visible before the design activities begin, and so that disputes over the depiction can be productive rather than buried. The picture will be revised as the cycles proceed; like the boundary statement, it is a dated artefact whose revisions are part of the record.

### 4.8.5 Reading the framework as antecedents, attributes and consequences

For readers working from the intervention-evaluation tradition, the ten components of Section 4.4 can be read in a second organisation that complements the cluster logic already given (establish the frame, organise the intervention, assess and sustain). The reading groups the components as antecedents, attributes and consequences of the intervention, and it is offered as an interpretive aid rather than a change to the framework: every statement below restates what the components already require.

The antecedents are the conditions that must be in place for the intervention to be designed coherently: the boundary statement and stakeholder map (C1), which establish what is inside the system and whose accounts are needed; the situated diagnosis (C2), which establishes what the configuration currently is, from the positions of those who live it; and the participation architecture (C3), which establishes that the people who must be part of the design are able to take part on terms that suit their lives. These three are the state of the situation at the moment design begins, and the framework refuses to proceed without them — the dependency stated in Section 4.5, that configuration design depends on the diagnosis, is this reading's central claim.

The attributes are the design properties of the arrangement that the lab creates: the configuration specification covering the fuel, water, space, labour and rules around the technology (C4); the governance and ownership arrangement (C5); the traced distribution of costs (C6); and the capability left in local hands (C7). These are the properties the co-design phase negotiates and the specification documents, and they are the dimensions along which the intervention is deliberately different from a technology handover.

The consequences are what the lab will produce and must answer for: the justice profile across the four dimensions (C8); the reflexive account of what the lab itself changed (C9); and the durability and continuity of the arrangement after the research team withdraws (C10). The feedback relations of Section 4.5 survive this reading — the justice evaluation feeds back into design (C8 to C4), and the reflexive records guard against the lab mistaking its own effects for the settlement's — which is why the reading is an aid to exposition rather than a second framework.

### 4.8.6 Bringing the model into motion

The final question is how the structure becomes an operating lab. The study's implementation phases (Chapter 5 and the research plan) sequence the work from scoping and ethics through diagnosis, co-design, prototyping, evaluation and exit. In the conceptual vocabulary of the model, the cycles run from an opening conception of the lab and its purpose, through the design of the arrangement, its development and application in the settlement, its implementation as a working service, and a review that both evaluates the arrangement and revises the lab itself — a cycle that then repeats with the revised understanding. The phasing is the study's own operationalisation of the Living Lab's iterative character; the named activities at each phase are those of the Design Research Methodology adopted in Chapter 5, and the recovery of the cycle from a stated problem situation to a supported design outcome is what the rich picture, the boundary statement and the diagnostic account are for in practice.

The lab is designed to be mobile and, where premises cannot be secured, to operate virtually: the activities of the lab are workshops, walks, diaries, build sessions and reviews attached to people and places, not to a building. What makes it a lab is the organised cycle of co-creation, testing in real conditions, evaluation against the justice criteria and revision — not an address. This is stated here because it is one of the definitional invariants on which the model depends (Section 2.5.2), and because the pilot-scale demonstration the study commits to is a demonstration of an arrangement in use, not of a device on a bench.
