# 3. Theoretical Framework

## 3.1 Why a theoretical framework matters in doctoral research

A theoretical framework is a set of assumptions and concepts, connected by an argument, that determines what a study observes, how it interprets what it observes, and what it will accept as an explanation. It is not a summary of theories that seem relevant. It is the structure that makes the study coherent, and it does three kinds of work at once. It specifies an ontology, a view of what exists and what kinds of things can act. It specifies an epistemology, a view of how knowledge about those things can be produced. And it specifies an axiology, a view of what the research is for and whose interests it serves.

The literature on doctoral supervision is consistent about the function of the framework, even when it disagrees about the detail. Grant and Osanloo (2014) describe the theoretical framework as the blueprint of a dissertation: it determines the rooms that can be built and the connections between them. Ravitch and Riggan (2017) go further, arguing that a conceptual framework is not a diagram added at the end of a literature review but the researcher's reasoned account of why the study is designed as it is. Sandberg and Alvesson (2011) show that the construction of a research question is itself a theoretical act, because the question already assumes a way of dividing the world. Alvesson and Kärreman (2007) make a related point about theory development: empirical material becomes interesting when it resists the theory that was used to generate it, not when it confirms it.

Four functions follow from this. First, a framework prevents description from masquerading as analysis. A study that reports what people said, without a theory of why they said it, produces data rather than knowledge. Second, it makes assumptions contestable. If a study assumes that households are rational choosers, that assumption can be examined and, if necessary, rejected. If the assumption is left implicit, it cannot be examined. Third, a framework connects a study to a tradition. It allows the reader to see which conversation the study is joining, and therefore what counts as a contribution. Fourth, it disciplines interpretation. A researcher who has committed to a theory can be held to its implications; a researcher who has not can explain any finding after the fact.

The relationship between theory and empirical work runs in both directions. Theory shapes the questions, the design and the interpretation. Empirical material, in turn, can revise the theory. This is the logic of abduction, which Alvesson and Kärreman (2007) describe as a movement between a theoretical frame and a surprising observation. The surprise is not a nuisance. It is the point at which the study has something to say.

A theoretical framework is not the same as a conceptual framework, and the distinction matters for the structure of this proposal. The theoretical framework is the general body of theory that the study adopts, together with the justification for adopting it. It is abstract and portable. The conceptual framework is the researcher's own model of the specific problem, derived from theory, literature and context. It is concrete and local. Section 5 establishes the theory. Chapter 4 constructs the model. A common weakness in applied research is to collapse the two, so that the model is presented as if it were a theory and the theory is never tested against anything.

The consequences of a weak or absent framework are predictable. Variables are selected ad hoc. Claims are made that cannot be falsified. Findings cannot be generalised beyond the case, because there is no theory to say what they are a case of. Recommendations follow from the researcher's preferences rather than from the analysis. In engineering management, where the pressure to be useful is strong, this failure is common. A framework is sometimes treated as an academic ornament that delays the practical work. The argument of this section is the opposite: without a framework, the practical work has no way of knowing what it is looking at.

## 3.2 Theoretical frameworks in Engineering Management research

Engineering Management sits at the intersection of technical systems, organisational behaviour, economics, innovation studies and public policy. Its problems are rarely confined to one level. A decision about a technology is also a decision about skills, contracts, maintenance, regulation and trust. A project that is technically sound can fail because the organisation that was supposed to operate it did not have the capacity, or because the tariff structure made the service unaffordable, or because the community had learned from previous projects that promises are not kept.

The field has borrowed theories from several disciplines. From psychology and information systems it has taken the Technology Acceptance Model and its successors. From operations it has taken Lean Thinking and the resource-based view. From complexity science it has taken complexity theory and systems thinking. From sociology and innovation studies it has taken socio-technical systems theory and the multi-level perspective. Each of these has been productive in the settings for which it was developed. The difficulty arises when a theory developed for a firm, an individual or a market is applied to an infrastructural problem in a low-income settlement. The theory does not simply lose precision. It mis-specifies the object.

Three mis-specifications recur. The first is level confusion. A theory of individual intention is used to explain a collective outcome, or a theory of firm strategy is used to explain a household practice. The second is the black-boxing of technology. The artefact is treated as a fixed input whose properties are known, when in fact its properties are part of what has to be explained. The third is the treatment of context as background. Context is what the model controls for, rather than what the model is about. In a study of energy in an informal settlement, context is the substance: tenure, density, infrastructure, income, gender and municipal capacity are not variables to be held constant. They are the conditions that make the technology work or fail.

A framework adequate to this problem needs to be relational and multi-level. It needs to hold human and non-human elements in one analytical frame, without reducing one to the other. It needs to be normative, because the problem is about justice and not only about efficiency. And it needs to be empirically tractable, because the study has to produce findings that can be defended. These four requirements are the criteria against which the theory is selected in Section 3.12.

There is also a question of disciplinary fit. Engineering management values usefulness. A theory that cannot be used to make a decision will not survive in the field, however elegant it is. The framework developed here is therefore assessed not only for its philosophical coherence but for whether it changes what a design team would do on a Monday morning.

## 3.3 Philosophical foundations of critical posthumanism

### 3.3.1 Humanism and its limits

Humanism is less a single doctrine than a family of commitments. It treats the human being as the central subject of knowledge and value. It separates culture from nature, mind from body, and subject from object. It assumes that the human has a essence that is universal, and that this essence is the measure against which other beings are judged. These commitments made possible important achievements: universal rights, scientific inquiry, the critique of arbitrary authority. They also produced exclusions. The universal human was, in practice, a particular kind of human: male, European, propertied, rational and able-bodied. Those who did not fit were treated as less than fully human, or as nature rather than culture, or as objects of study rather than participants in knowledge.

Posthumanism begins from this diagnosis. It does not reject the achievements of humanism. It rejects the pretence that the human is a self-contained, universal subject, and it asks what becomes visible when that pretence is dropped. The question is not "what comes after the human?" but "how has the human been produced, and what else is involved in that production?"

### 3.3.2 Three formations that must not be conflated

The term posthumanism covers several projects, and the differences matter.

The first is posthumanism as a historical condition. The human is increasingly entangled with technologies, data, infrastructures and ecological systems. The boundaries between body and machine, nature and culture, are becoming harder to draw. This condition is described by Hayles (1999) and by Braidotti (2013), although they evaluate it differently.

The second is critical posthumanism. This is a normative and philosophical project. It draws on anti-humanism, feminism, postcolonial thought and science and technology studies. It affirms difference rather than universalism, and it treats knowledge as situated. Braidotti (2013, 2019) is its most systematic contemporary exponent. Wolfe (2010) distinguishes it carefully from the third formation.

The third is transhumanism. Transhumanism seeks to enhance human capacities through technology: longer life, greater intelligence, control over the body. It is often described as posthuman, but its values remain humanist. It celebrates perfectibility, mastery and transcendence. It treats the body as a limitation to be overcome rather than as a condition of knowledge. This study rejects transhumanism, and the rejection is not a matter of taste. Transhumanism would direct attention to the enhancement of individual capacities and away from the collective, material and institutional arrangements that are the subject of this study.

A fourth position, technological determinism, is also rejected. Determinism holds that technology drives social change according to its own logic. The alternative adopted here is not that society is autonomous, but that technology and society are co-constituted. A prepaid meter does not cause a particular relation between landlord and tenant. It materialises a relation that is already being negotiated, and in doing so it changes the terms of the negotiation.

### 3.3.3 Relational ontology

The central ontological claim of critical posthumanism is that entities do not precede their relations. This is stated most rigorously by Barad (2007). In agential realism, the primary unit is the phenomenon, not the object. A phenomenon is an entanglement of matter and meaning in which boundaries are enacted. The entities that appear within a phenomenon, which Barad calls relata, acquire their properties through intra-action. They do not exist first and then interact. Intra-action is the relation through which the relata emerge.

This is a difficult claim, and it is easy to repeat without understanding it. An example may help. A biogas digester is not a thing with fixed properties that is then introduced into a household. What the digester is, in practice, depends on the feedstock that is available, the water that can be spared, the space that is allocated, the stove that is used, the person who maintains it and the agreements that govern it. Change the water supply and the digester becomes a different object: it may become a burden rather than a service. The properties that matter are not intrinsic to the vessel. They are produced in the relation.

Haraway's (1988) epistemology is a companion to this ontology. Knowledge is always from somewhere. The idea of a "view from nowhere" is not a description of objectivity but a concealment of a particular position. Situated knowledge is not less objective; it is objective in a different way, because it can be located and therefore checked. For this study, the implication is direct: the knowledge of a resident about how a stove behaves in a small kitchen is not anecdote to be validated by an expert. It is knowledge produced at a particular site, and it is indispensable to a design that has to work at that site.

### 3.3.4 Distributed agency

If entities emerge through relations, then agency cannot be a property that belongs to humans alone. Barad (2007) describes agency as an enactment, not an attribute. Agency is distributed across the apparatus through which a phenomenon is observed and through the materials that participate in it. The agential cut is the boundary that is drawn, within a phenomenon, between subject and object, or between what is observed and what observes. The cut is not arbitrary, but it is a cut: it includes some things and excludes others, and it has consequences.

Latour's (2005) actor-network theory is adjacent to this position but not identical. Latour treats humans and non-humans symmetrically as actants, and he traces the networks through which action is assembled. His work is empirically useful because it shows how a technology enrols allies and how it can be undone. It is less useful for normative questions, because it is deliberately indifferent to the justice of the network it describes. This study uses Latour's sensibility without adopting his ontology wholesale.

Bennett's (2010) notion of thing-power is also relevant. Bennett argues that matter has a vitality of its own, that things can resist, surprise and exceed human intention. A digester that produces too little gas, a pipe that clogs, a stove that will not sit flat on a pot: these are not merely technical failures. They are moments in which the material world asserts its own agency and changes what the household can do.

The consequence for research is significant. If agency is distributed, then a study that asks only what people think has already excluded most of what is going on. It has excluded the meter, the fuel, the dwelling, the water supply and the maintenance arrangement. Those are not context. They are participants.

### 3.3.5 Materiality and technological agency

Posthumanism takes matter seriously. This is what distinguishes it from social constructionism, which can treat the material world as a product of discourse. Matter is not passive. It has properties that enable some actions and constrain others. A prepaid meter, for example, is not a neutral device for measuring consumption. It enacts a particular relation between the household, the utility and money. It requires a purchase before use, it can disconnect a household automatically, and it shifts the management of cash flow onto the household. Those are material effects that shape practice, and they are not captured by asking whether the meter is easy to use.

A biogas digester is another example. Its material requirements, which include water, feedstock, temperature and space, produce a set of social requirements: someone must collect the waste, someone must carry the water, someone must be available when the system needs attention, and someone must be able to buy a spare part. The technology distributes labour, and the distribution is usually gendered. This is not a social consequence that follows from a technical artefact. It is a property of the artefact-in-relation.

### 3.3.6 Ecological interdependence

Posthumanism also insists on the interdependence of human and ecological systems. The nature–culture distinction is not a description of the world but a way of organising it, and that organisation has costs. The Anthropocene is the name for a condition in which human activity has become a geological force. It is also a condition in which the consequences of that activity are unevenly distributed: those who contributed least to emissions are often most exposed to their effects.

For this study, ecological interdependence matters in two ways. First, energy interventions have environmental effects that are not externalities to be added at the end. A biogas system that consumes water in a water-scarce settlement is not environmentally benign simply because it is renewable. Second, environmental justice and social justice are not separate agendas. The same arrangements that expose residents to smoke and fire also expose them to floods, heat and pollution. A framework that treats the social as one domain and the environmental as another will miss the connection.

### 3.3.7 Ethics and power

Posthumanist ethics is relational and affirmative. Braidotti (2006, 2013) contrasts potestas, a power that negates and constrains, with potentia, a capacity that enables and creates. The distinction is useful because it avoids the assumption that power is always repressive. A community can have the capacity to organise, to learn and to build, even when it lacks formal authority. The task of a just intervention is to increase potentia without ignoring the potestas that constrains it.

Haraway (2016) offers the concept of response-ability: the capacity to respond to others, human and non-human, in a way that is accountable. Response-ability is not a rule. It is a practice of attention. Barad (2007) describes ethics as ethico-onto-epistemology, insisting that questions of being and knowing cannot be separated from questions of value. What we choose to observe, and how we choose to observe it, is already an ethical decision.

These ideas have practical implications for a Living Lab. Who is invited to the first meeting is an ethical decision. Which data are recorded and which are not is an ethical decision. Who is credited for an innovation, and who bears the risk of failure, are ethical decisions. A framework that treats ethics as a consent form to be signed at the start will miss most of them.

Braidotti supplies the normative horizon. Her critique of humanist universalism is the reason the study cannot treat "the user" as a generic category. Her account of affirmative ethics is the reason the framework is oriented towards capacity-building rather than deficit-repair. Her distinction between potestas and potentia, developed in Transpositions (2006) and used by Aboalez (2025), gives the study a way of talking about power that is not only negative. But Braidotti's work is not a method. It does not tell the researcher what to observe in a settlement, and it can encourage a style of writing that is more evocative than analytical. The study uses her concepts and resists her prose.

Haraway supplies the epistemology. Her argument that knowledge is situated is the strongest available alternative to the assumption that the designer knows best. Her concept of response-ability gives the study a way of describing the ethical relation between researchers and residents that is more demanding than informed consent. Her later work on sympoiesis, or making-with, is directly relevant to a Living Lab, because it describes collaboration as a condition of life rather than as a technique. The limitation is that Haraway is deliberately unsystematic. She offers figures and provocations, not procedures.

Barad supplies the ontology and the analytical unit. The concept of intra-action is the reason the study treats the phenomenon, rather than the user or the artefact, as the unit of analysis. The concept of the apparatus is the reason the Living Lab can be understood as producing the phenomenon it studies, rather than observing it from outside. Diffraction, the practice of reading insights through one another rather than against one another, is used in the analysis to hold the interview, observational and workshop material together. The risk is obscurity. Barad's vocabulary is drawn from quantum physics, and it can be used to impress rather than to explain. Every technical term in this proposal is therefore defined in plain English and illustrated with an energy example.

Hayles supplies the corrective to abstraction. Her account of embodiment insists that information is always instantiated in a body, a device and a material arrangement. Her concept of technogenesis, the idea that humans and technologies co-evolve, supports the study's refusal to treat the technology as an external object. Her work is less useful for questions of justice and participation, and it is not asked to carry that weight.

The four thinkers converge on a rejection of the autonomous humanist subject, on relationality, and on the critique of the nature–culture divide. They diverge on almost everything else. Braidotti is a vitalist monist; Barad is a realist about relations; Haraway is a situated pragmatist; Hayles is a media theorist of embodiment. The study does not present them as a single theory. It assembles a small number of concepts from each, states why each is needed, and accepts that the assembly is a construction rather than a discovery.

This is also the point at which the study connects to the supervisor's work. Aboalez (2025) draws primarily on Braidotti and Barad to argue for a posthumanist social approach to energy transition. The present study extends that argument from engineering education to settlement-level design, and adds Haraway's epistemology and Hayles's materialism to address questions that the earlier paper did not have to answer.

## 3.5 Posthumanism and socio-technical energy systems

An energy system is usually described as a chain: generation, transmission, distribution, metering, billing, appliances and end use. The description is accurate as far as it goes, but it separates the technical from the social and then treats the social as the place where the technical is received. A posthumanist account describes the same system as an assemblage: a configuration of people, materials, infrastructures, institutions, fuels, dwellings, meters, tariffs and practices whose relations produce the capacity to deliver an energy service.

The difference is not merely semantic. In the chain description, the household is the end point. In the assemblage description, the household is one element among many, and the service is produced by the relations between them. A cooking service, for example, is produced by the stove, the fuel, the pot, the kitchen, the person who cooks, the money that buys the fuel, the tariff that sets the price, the reliability of the supply and the norms that govern what counts as a proper meal. Change any one of these and the service changes.

This way of thinking has become more common in energy social science. Sovacool (2014), reviewing fifteen years of energy scholarship, calls for a social science agenda that takes infrastructure, practice and politics seriously. The multi-level perspective in transition studies describes how niches, regimes and landscapes interact over time (Geels, 2002, 2011). That literature is useful because it explains why transitions are slow and why a promising niche can fail to displace an incumbent regime. Its limitation, for present purposes, is that it operates at a high level of aggregation. It can explain the fate of a technology category. It is less good at explaining why one digester works and the one next door does not.

The value of posthumanism is that it operates at the scale of the configuration without losing the wider structures. It can hold the micro and the macro in one frame, because both are composed of relations. The tariff, the municipal by-law, the landlord's rule, the stove and the pot are all elements of the same assemblage, even though they operate at different scales.

### 3.5.1 A worked illustration: the biogas digester

Consider a community-scale biogas digester proposed for an informal settlement. A conventional engineering assessment would specify the feedstock, the hydraulic retention time, the digester volume, the gas yield and the capital cost. A TAM study would add measures of perceived usefulness and ease of use, and perhaps a question about whether residents would recommend the system to a neighbour. Both would produce useful information. Neither would explain what happens next.

A posthumanist analysis asks a different set of questions.

*Feedstock.* Who produces the organic waste, who collects it, and what else do they do with their time? If the waste is currently sold or fed to animals, the digester is competing with an existing use. If collection is done by women or children, the labour cost falls on them. If the supply is seasonal, the digester's output will be seasonal. The feedstock is not an input to the technology. It is a relation between the technology, the household economy and the division of labour.

*Water.* Digestion requires water, and the ratio of water to feedstock matters. In a settlement where water is shared, purchased or carried, the digester is competing with cooking, cleaning and drinking. A design that assumes a tap in the yard has already excluded the households that are most in need.

*Space.* A digester needs a footprint, a slope, access for maintenance and a safe distance from dwellings. In a dense settlement, space is not empty. It is claimed, used and contested. The siting decision is a decision about whose space is taken and who is compensated.

*Temperature.* Microbial activity depends on temperature. A system that performs well in a warm climate may underperform in winter or at altitude. The material property is not a detail to be handled by a safety factor. It shapes the service that can be promised.

*Cooking.* Gas burns differently from wood or paraffin. The flame is less visible, the heat is more concentrated, and the pot may need to be different. A technology that ignores the pot and the dish will be judged unusable by the person who cooks, whatever the gas yield.

*Maintenance.* A digester needs someone to check the feed, remove the digestate, clear a blockage and replace a valve. That someone needs time, tools, training and a reason to keep going. If the arrangement depends on a volunteer who moves away, the technology has a design fault, not a social problem.

*Gender.* The labour of feeding, cleaning and maintaining a household energy system is usually assigned to women. A system that increases that labour while reducing smoke may be a good intervention on one dimension and a bad one on another. The posthumanist frame makes the distribution visible because it treats labour as part of the assemblage rather than as a preference.

*Institutions.* The digester may require a permit, a waste collection agreement, a connection to the municipal service or a credit arrangement. These are not "enabling environment" factors that sit outside the project. They are elements whose presence or absence changes what the technology is.

None of this is invisible to a good engineer. Experienced practitioners know that feedstock, water, space and maintenance decide whether a system survives. The contribution of the theory is not the list of factors. It is the claim that the factors are constitutive of the technology rather than external to it, and that the unit of design is therefore the configuration. If that claim is accepted, the design process changes. It must prototype the arrangement, not only the artefact. It must include the meter, the tariff, the dwelling, the maintenance contract and the waste supply in the prototype. It must evaluate the arrangement, not only the device.

The TAM study would tell us that residents find the idea of biogas useful. The posthumanist study would tell us that the system will fail in the households where water is carried, succeed in the households where a market provides a steady waste stream, and produce a new burden for the women who are expected to feed it. The second account is less tidy, but it is more useful for design.

## 3.6 Posthumanism, social justice and just energy transition

The normative core of this study is not posthumanism alone. It is a conversation between posthumanism and energy justice. Each supplies something the other lacks.

Posthumanism adds four things to the justice literature. First, it extends the moral community beyond humans. Materials, ecologies and future generations are not merely resources to be allocated; they are participants in the arrangement whose flourishing matters. Second, it reframes justice as a property of composition rather than only of distribution. The question is not only how much of a benefit each household receives, but which actors and materials are admitted to the arrangement in the first place. Third, it makes visible forms of exclusion that a human-centred recognition analysis can miss. A dwelling that cannot accommodate a technology, or an infrastructure that excludes by design, is a form of injustice that does not appear in a survey of attitudes. Fourth, it supplies a critique of the technocratic view from nowhere. The designer who claims to be neutral is usually occupying a position that others do not have.

Posthumanism also has limits, and they should be stated plainly. It does not specify distributive criteria. It cannot tell a municipality how to allocate a budget between two settlements. It does not generate policy instruments. It has no theory of institutions, and it can be vague about how decisions are actually made. Applied without a human-centred justice vocabulary, it can obscure human suffering by dissolving it into ontology. A woman who cannot afford paraffin is not a "phenomenon". She is a person in a specific relation of deprivation, and the analysis must be able to say so.

Energy justice supplies the missing operational content. The four dimensions used in this study, distribution, procedure, recognition and restoration, provide criteria that can be assessed. Distribution asks who receives the service and who bears the cost. Procedure asks who participates and on what terms. Recognition asks whose knowledge and practices count. Restoration asks what past harm is repaired and what future capacity is built. These are not derived from posthumanism. They are derived from the justice literature (McCauley et al., 2013; Jenkins et al., 2016; Heffron and McCauley, 2017), and they are retained as a normative-operational complement.

Justice also has a scalar dimension. Fraser (2009) argues that the question of the frame, who is included in the "who" of justice, is itself a question of justice. In an energy project, the frame may be the household, the street, the settlement, the municipality or the planet. Each frame makes some claims visible and others invisible. A project that is just at the scale of the settlement may be unjust at the scale of the region, if it displaces pollution or extracts water. The study does not resolve this problem, but it names it and builds a scalar check into the evaluation.

The combination is therefore deliberate. Posthumanism provides the ontology and the critique of technocracy. Energy justice provides the criteria and the policy vocabulary. Systems thinking provides the tools for mapping relations and feedback. None of the three is sufficient alone, and the framework is designed so that each can be held accountable by the others.

One further point is necessary. The literature on just transition has been criticised for treating "the community" as a homogeneous beneficiary. The same criticism applies to a posthumanist framework if it is not careful. A settlement is stratified by tenure, income, gender, age, ethnicity and political affiliation. A project that benefits "the community" may benefit one group within it and burden another. The framework therefore requires disaggregated evidence. It does not assume that a community-level outcome is a household-level outcome.

## 3.7 Posthumanism and end-user participation

### 3.7.1 The user in humanist design

User-centred design begins from the needs and experience of the user. Participatory design goes further, involving users in the design process. Co-creation and co-research extend participation to the generation of ideas and the production of knowledge (Sanders and Stappers, 2008; von Hippel, 2005). These traditions have improved design practice, and they have given users a voice that they did not have before.

They also carry humanist assumptions. The user is treated as a centre of intention and experience. The artefact is treated as a tool that the user employs. Participation is treated as a process of extracting knowledge from users and transferring it into a design. The designer remains the one who decides what counts as knowledge and what counts as a good design. The user is consulted, but the frame of the consultation is set elsewhere.

These assumptions are not wrong in every context. They are inadequate for a technology whose performance depends on the arrangement around it. If the problem is the water supply, the tenure arrangement or the maintenance contract, then consulting users about their preferences for a stove will not solve it. Participation has to extend to the elements that the design tradition treats as context.

### 3.7.2 The end-user as a participant in an assemblage

A posthumanist reconstruction treats the end-user as a position within an assemblage rather than as a centre of intention. The end-user is the person whose practices, body, dwelling and relationships are enrolled in the technology. Their agency is real, but it is not the only agency at work. The artefact, the fuel, the infrastructure and the institution also act, and the outcome is produced by the relation.

This does not diminish the user. It makes the user's knowledge more important, not less. If the outcome depends on the relation, then the person who lives in the relation has access to knowledge that the designer does not. That knowledge is situated, embodied and practical. It is not always articulate, and it is not always consistent. It is nevertheless indispensable.

### 3.7.3 What kind of knowledge do end-users hold?

The literature often asserts that users hold valuable knowledge without saying what kind. Five types are relevant here.

*Tacit knowledge* is knowledge that is embedded in practice and difficult to articulate. A cook knows when a pot is at the right temperature by sound and feel, not by a thermometer. A household knows how much paraffin to buy in a given week without calculating it.

*Situated knowledge* is knowledge produced from a particular position (Haraway, 1988). A tenant knows the landlord's rules in a way that an owner does not. A woman knows the risks of cooking in a shared kitchen in a way that a male researcher may not.

*Embodied knowledge* is knowledge held in the body. It includes the physical effort of carrying water, the heat of a stove, the smoke in the eyes and the weight of a pot. Hayles (1999) insists that this knowledge cannot be reduced to information.

*Practice-based knowledge* is knowledge of how a task is actually done, as distinct from how it is supposed to be done. It includes workarounds, repairs, substitutions and the informal rules that make a system work.

*Cultural knowledge* is knowledge of what a technology means. It includes the appropriateness of a fuel for a dish, the symbolism of a flame, the social meaning of cooking for guests and the norms that govern who cooks and when.

A design process that recognises these types of knowledge will use different methods to elicit them. Interviews are good for articulate knowledge. Observation and transects are good for tacit and practice-based knowledge. Diaries and photovoice are good for embodied and situated knowledge. Workshops are good for cultural knowledge, if they are organised in a way that allows disagreement.

### 3.7.4 Culture as constitutive, not as a barrier

The earlier draft of this proposal treated culture as a barrier to acceptance. That framing is misleading. A cultural practice is not an obstacle to a technology; it is part of the standard against which the technology is judged. If a dish requires a visible flame, a technology that provides an invisible flame is not culturally neutral and technically adequate. It is inadequate for that dish. The question is not how to overcome the culture but how to design a technology that can be enrolled in it, or how to negotiate a change in practice that the household considers acceptable.

This does not mean that all practices should be accepted uncritically. Some practices are harmful, and some are constrained by poverty rather than by preference. The point is that the analysis must distinguish between a preference, a constraint and a compromise. A household that uses paraffin because it cannot afford electricity is not expressing a cultural preference. It is managing a constraint. A household that uses wood because the food tastes better is expressing a preference. The design response is different in each case.

### 3.7.5 Participation and its failure conditions

Participation is not automatically good. The literature on participation identifies several failure modes, and a framework that ignores them will romanticise the community it studies.

*Tokenism.* Residents are invited but not heard. Their presence is used to legitimise a decision that has already been made (Arnstein, 1969).

*Elite capture.* Those with status, education or political connections dominate the process and direct the benefits towards themselves.

*Expectation inflation.* The project promises more than it can deliver, and the disappointment damages trust in future projects.

*Consultation fatigue.* Communities are asked to participate repeatedly in projects that do not endure. The cost of participation falls on those with the least time.

*Framing power.* The agenda, the language and the technical vocabulary are set by the project. Residents can respond within the frame but cannot change it.

*Invisibility of labour.* Participation is counted in meetings attended, not in the time and effort that residents contribute. The labour of participation is often borne by women.

*Exit.* The project ends, the funding stops, and the arrangements that were built around it collapse. Participation without a sustainability plan is a temporary relationship.

A posthumanist analysis adds a further failure mode: *material exclusion*. A resident may be willing to participate but unable to attend because the meeting is held at a time when she is cooking, or in a place that she cannot reach, or in a language she does not speak comfortably. The barrier is not attitudinal. It is material and institutional, and it can be designed against.

### 3.7.6 Acceptance re-theorised

The study replaces the attitudinal construct of acceptance with a relational one. Acceptance is a pattern of sustained use that emerges when technology, infrastructure, household practice, cost and social norms align. It is not a decision made once. It is an achievement that has to be reproduced every day.

This definition has four implications. First, acceptance is observable in practice, not only in stated intention. A household that says it likes the system but does not use it has not accepted it. Second, acceptance is provisional. It can be lost when a part fails, a price rises or a relationship changes. Third, acceptance is distributed. It depends on the maintenance arrangement, the fuel supply and the institution as much as on the household. Fourth, acceptance is not the same as justice. A technology can be accepted because the household has no alternative, and that acceptance can coexist with exploitation.

The definition also makes acceptance measurable in a way that is useful for design. The study can observe use over time, record the points at which use declines, and trace the relations that changed. That is more demanding than a satisfaction survey, but it produces evidence that can be acted on.

## 3.8 Systems approaches: systems thinking, system dynamics and systems engineering

The framework's claim to be systemic is built from three related but distinct traditions: systems thinking as an epistemology, system dynamics as a modelling practice, and systems engineering as a design discipline. This section sets out what each contributes, because the terms are used loosely in the Living Lab literature and the looseness has consequences. A lab can use the vocabulary of all three and remain conventional in its structure; the framework requires their substance, and Chapter 4 specifies where each enters the design.

### 3.8.1 Systems thinking and the systems approach

Systems thinking is first of all a way of organising inquiry. Checkland's foundational argument is that a system is not an object lying in the world but a device for making sense of one: the analyst declares a boundary, names the elements inside it and the relationships among them, and accepts that another analyst could declare it differently (Checkland, 1981). The declaration is consequential. Whoever draws the boundary of "the project" decides whose costs appear in the accounts, whose knowledge counts as evidence and whose exclusion is invisible. Critical systems work makes that consequence explicit and asks, of any framing, whose interests it serves (Midgley, 2000).

The second resource is the behaviour of systems. Stocks accumulate, flows move material and information between them, feedback loops couple the parts, and delays separate causes from their effects; from these simple elements, systems produce behaviour — growth, oscillation, collapse, resistance to policy — that cannot be read off any single part (Meadows, 2008). For energy interventions in settlements this is not a metaphor. A digester that is abandoned when the water supply fails is a system behaving, not an attitude softening; a tariff that protects revenue and shifts arrears risk onto tenants is a feedback structure with a distributional signature.

Taken together, these resources discipline the study in two ways. The boundary critique is load-bearing rather than rhetorical: the framework in Chapter 4 makes the drawing of the boundary an explicit, recorded and revisable decision. And the attention to feedback is what distinguishes an architecture from a vocabulary: the lab is designed so that use changes design, evaluation changes governance, and the lab's own interventions are recorded as inputs to its analysis.

### 3.8.2 System dynamics and causal loop diagramming

System dynamics supplies the formal vocabulary in which the feedback claims are expressed. Its method represents a system as stocks and flows regulated by information feedback, and treats the structure as the generator of behaviour over time (Sterman, 2000). Within that tradition, the causal loop diagram — variables connected by signed links, closed into loops of reinforcing or balancing polarity — is the standard first formalisation, and it is deliberately qualitative: it disciplines the argument without demanding the data that a simulation would.

The study uses the qualitative instrument, not the simulation. The planned analysis includes the construction of causal loop diagrams with participants and stakeholders (Chapter 5), and the diagrams serve three purposes in the design. They force the specification of relationships that would otherwise remain anecdote; they expose to participants the structure the research team has inferred, so that the inference can be contested; and they provide the formalisation that connects the diagnosis to the components of the framework, several of which — cost incidence, participation, maintenance — are explicitly feedback phenomena.

One restriction is acknowledged at the outset. Causal loop diagrams are exchangeable representations, not validated models: a diagram drawn with participants is a record of a structured conversation, and its epistemic status is that of an organised account of how the stakeholders see the system operating. The methodological chapter treats them accordingly, and the reflection records of the lab (Chapter 4) keep the inference visible rather than letting harden into fact.

### 3.8.3 Systems engineering and the systems life cycle

Systems engineering contributes the designer's discipline. In its standard usage it names the methodology by which a complex system is defined, developed and deployed so that its parts — technical and organisational — work together across the whole life of the system: from the identification of the need, through requirements, design synthesis and verification, into operation, maintenance and eventual retirement. The discipline insists that interfaces be specified, that requirements be traceable to needs, and that the system be judged against its behaviour in operation rather than against the elegance of its design.

The study imports this discipline into the lab's intervention, with one deliberate inversion. In a conventional application the artefact is the system and the context is the environment; here the configuration — the arrangement of the technology with its fuel and water supply, its space, its labour and its governance — is the system to be engineered, and the digester is one component within it. The design specification that the co-design phase will produce (Chapter 5) is therefore written at the level of the arrangement, and the life-cycle discipline is applied to it: requirements traced to the diagnostic account, verification against conditions of use, a maintenance concept, and an explicit retirement-and-handover plan. The end-user-centred framing governs whose requirements count as the requirements.

### 3.8.4 Systems approaches in informal settlements

The three traditions meet in the specific conditions of an informal settlement, and the meeting is not frictionless. A settlement is a system in the strong sense — energy, water, waste, tenure and transport are interdependent, and interventions in one propagate to the others — yet it is also a system whose boundaries are politically loaded, whose stocks and flows are partly unrecorded, and whose design authority is dispersed among state, landlord, resident and project. Methodologically this means that the systems traditions must be used in their critical and participatory forms: system dynamics as a structured conversation rather than an expert model; systems engineering with requirements negotiated among the parties rather than derived from a single client; boundary critique as a permanent practice rather than a project-stage formality.

These commitments shape the operational framework in Chapter 4 at identifiable points: the boundary statement and stakeholder mapping (C1), the diagnosis that takes accounts from multiple positions as data (C2), the configuration specification (C4), and the reflexive records that treat the lab's own interventions as part of the system (C9). The next section turns to the synthesis proper: what the Living Lab becomes when its architecture is derived from this systems base together with posthumanist theory.

## 3.9 Posthumanism and the Systems Living Lab

A Living Lab is a real-world arrangement for co-creation. It brings users, researchers, firms and public bodies together in a setting where the innovation will be used, and it organises iterative cycles of design, prototyping and testing (Bergvall-Kåreborn and Ståhlbröst, 2009; Leminen et al., 2012). The literature distinguishes several types, including provider-driven, utilizer-driven, enabler-driven and user-driven labs. It also distinguishes labs that are primarily physical spaces from those that are primarily methods or networks (Mbatha and Musango, 2022).

Systems thinking adds three resources. The first is boundary critique. Checkland (1981) argues that the boundary of a system is a choice, and Midgley (2000) shows that the choice has political consequences. Who is inside the system, and who is in the environment, determines whose interests are considered. The second is feedback. Meadows (2008) shows that the behaviour of a system is produced by loops, delays and stocks, not by the intentions of its members. The third is emergence. Cilliers (1998) argues that complex systems have properties that cannot be predicted from their components, and that any model is a reduction.

A Living Lab becomes systemic, in this study, when three things are true. First, its boundary is chosen through an explicit critique rather than by convention. The lab does not assume that the project, the settlement or the municipality is the relevant system. It asks which boundary would include the relations that matter, and it records what is excluded. Second, its feedback architecture is designed. The lab collects data on the effects of its own interventions and uses them to revise the intervention. Third, its evaluation criteria are derived from the system's purpose, not from the project's outputs. It asks whether the arrangement became more just and more secure, not only whether the prototype worked.

There is a further, distinctly posthumanist point. A Living Lab is an apparatus in Barad's sense. It does not observe a phenomenon from outside; it participates in producing the phenomenon. The questions it asks, the instruments it uses, the people it includes and the data it records all enact a cut. The cut makes some things visible and others invisible. Reflexivity about the cut is therefore a condition of validity, not an optional virtue. A lab that presents its findings as neutral observations has misunderstood its own role.

This has practical consequences. It means that the lab must document its own decisions: why this site, why these participants, why this technology, why these indicators. It means that dissenting views must be recorded, not smoothed into consensus. It means that the lab must be accountable to the people it studies, not only to the funder. And it means that the evaluation must ask what the lab itself changed, including changes that were not intended.

The study derives six design principles for a systemic Living Lab from this argument. They are stated here and operationalised in Chapter 4.

1. *Boundary transparency.* The lab states whose interests are included and excluded, and revisits the boundary as the project develops.

2. *Distributed participation.* Participation is designed around the material conditions of residents' lives, including time, location, language, care responsibilities and safety.

3. *Configuration as prototype.* The lab prototypes the arrangement, including the artefact, the fuel supply, the maintenance arrangement, the tariff and the governance rules.

4. *Reflexive apparatus.* The lab records its own interventions and treats them as part of the data.

5. *Justice as an outcome and a process.* The lab evaluates distribution, procedure, recognition and restoration, and it applies the same criteria to its own operation.

6. *Exit and durability.* The lab plans for its own withdrawal from the start, including who will own, maintain and finance the arrangement after the project ends.

These principles are not a recipe. They are commitments that shape how the lab is organised, and they can be assessed.

It directs the design towards leverage points: the maintenance arrangement, the fuel supply, the tariff, the governance rule and the siting decision. It also insists that a mechanism is not just unless it can be shown to distribute benefits and burdens fairly.

For RQ5, the theory provides the criteria for evaluation. The lab is assessed not only on whether it produced a working prototype but on whether it changed the configuration in a way that increased justice and security. That is a higher bar, and it is the bar that the study sets.

## 3.12 Justification for selecting posthumanism

Six criteria are used to select the theory. They are explanatory power, ontological fit, capacity to handle human and non-human elements, normative adequacy, empirical tractability and institutional fit.

*Explanatory power.* Posthumanism explains why identical technologies produce different outcomes in different households and streets. It explains why participation can fail even when it is well organised. It explains why material and infrastructural conditions matter. It explains why justice is not an add-on but a property of the arrangement.

*Ontological fit.* The research questions are about relations, not about individuals or variables. Posthumanism is the only candidate that takes the relation as its primary unit. TAM takes the individual. Diffusion theory takes the population. Practice theory takes the practice. Each captures something, but none captures the full configuration.

*Human and non-human elements.* The study has to hold people, artefacts, infrastructures, fuels, institutions and ecologies in one frame. Posthumanism does this without reducing one to the other. Systems thinking can also do it, but it tends to treat the elements as components of a functional whole rather than as participants with unequal power.

*Normative adequacy.* The study is about justice. Posthumanism supplies a normative vocabulary that is not reducible to efficiency. It is complemented by energy justice, which supplies the criteria. The combination is stronger than either alone.

*Empirical tractability.* Posthumanism is often criticised for being abstract. This study addresses the criticism by operationalising the concepts as observable relations. Intra-action becomes the relation between the artefact, the household and the infrastructure. Distributed agency becomes the set of actors that must be enrolled for the system to work. Situated knowledge becomes the knowledge held by residents and elicited through specific methods. The test of the framework is whether it can be used in the field, not whether it is elegant.

*Institutional fit.* The theory was introduced by the supervisor and is supported by the research programme in which the study is located. That is a reason to take it seriously, but it is not a sufficient reason to adopt it. The argument of this section is that the theory is defensible on its merits. If it were not, the institutional fit would be a reason to negotiate rather than a reason to proceed.

Several alternatives were considered.

*TAM and UTAUT* were rejected for the reasons set out in Section 3.10. They are useful for measuring attitudes but not for explaining configurations.

*Diffusion of innovations* explains how an innovation spreads through a population. It is useful for understanding adoption curves and the role of early adopters. It is less useful for understanding why a technology that has spread can be abandoned, or why the benefits of diffusion are unevenly distributed.

*Social practice theory* is close to this study in its attention to routine, materiality and convention (Shove, 2003; Stephenson et al., 2010). It is a valuable complement, and it informs the analysis of cooking and fuel stacking. Its limitation is that it is stronger on reproduction than on intervention. It explains why practices persist; it says less about how a design team might change them.

*Actor-network theory* is the closest alternative. It shares the commitment to symmetry between humans and non-humans, and it provides a rich empirical vocabulary for tracing associations. It was not adopted as the primary theory because it is deliberately indifferent to normative questions. The study needs a theory that can say something about justice, not only about association.

*The capability approach* (Nussbaum, 2011) provides a normative framework for evaluating wellbeing and freedom. It is compatible with the justice dimension of this study and could be used to specify what people are able to do. It does not, by itself, provide an account of technology or of material agency. It is therefore a possible complement rather than a foundation.

*Political ecology* and *institutional theory* explain the political and organisational conditions of energy transitions. They are important, and they inform the analysis of municipal capacity and vested interests. They operate at a different level from the present study, which is concerned with the design of a local arrangement.

*Systems thinking alone* was rejected because it can describe a configuration without asking whether the configuration is just. It is retained as a methodological complement, not as the foundation.

The verdict is that posthumanism is retained as the primary foundation because it is the only candidate that supplies an ontology adequate to the human–technology–infrastructure relation at the heart of the study. It is not sufficient on its own. The supporting perspectives supply what it lacks: systems thinking supplies the tools, energy justice supplies the criteria, Living Lab theory supplies the method and socio-technical theory supplies the wider transition context.

The theory also has limits that should be stated. It explains individual motivation poorly. It does not produce measurable acceptance scores. It offers limited guidance on policy design. It can be difficult to write clearly. These limits are real, and the study manages them by using mixed qualitative methods and by keeping the empirical claims modest.

## 3.13 Synthesis: theoretical propositions

The framework can be stated as six propositions. They are not hypotheses in the statistical sense. They are claims that the empirical work will examine, qualify and, where necessary, revise.

*P1. Acceptance is an emergent property of a socio-material configuration.* It follows that interventions which change only the artefact will produce unstable acceptance. The observable implication is that acceptance will vary with water, space, fuel supply and maintenance arrangements, not only with attitudes.

*P2. End-user agency is distributed across people, artefacts and institutions.* It follows that participation mechanisms which address only human voice will fail to shift configurations. The observable implication is that projects which consult residents but do not change the material arrangement will not produce durable change.

*P3. Justice outcomes depend on which actors and materials are admitted to the intra-action.* It follows that the boundary of the project, who is in the room, what counts as evidence and which infrastructure is treated as fixed, is a justice decision. The observable implication is that projects with narrow boundaries will produce benefits that are captured by those inside the boundary.

*P4. A Living Lab is an apparatus that produces the phenomena it studies.* It follows that reflexivity about the lab's own cuts is a condition of validity. The observable implication is that a lab which does not document its own interventions will misattribute its findings to the community.

*P5. Acceptance and justice are related but not identical.* It follows that high acceptance can coexist with injustice. The observable implication is that a technology can be widely used and still distribute costs unfairly, for example when tenants pay more than owners or when women bear additional labour.

*P6. Participation has material conditions.* It follows that the failure of participation is often a failure of design rather than a lack of community interest. The observable implication is that attendance and engagement will vary with the time, place, language and care arrangements of the meeting.

## 3.14 Chapter summary

This section has argued that energy interventions in informal settlements fail for reasons that are theoretical as well as technical. The dominant models treat acceptance as an individual decision about a finished artefact, and in doing so they exclude the relations that determine whether a technology works. Critical posthumanism supplies an alternative ontology. It treats entities as emerging through relations, agency as distributed, matter as generative and knowledge as situated. It is complemented by energy justice, which supplies normative criteria, by systems thinking, which supplies tools for mapping relations and feedback, and by Living Lab theory, which supplies a method for organising collaboration.

The framework has limits. It does not specify distributive criteria, it does not produce policy instruments and it can be difficult to operationalise. The study manages these limits by treating posthumanism as the foundation rather than the whole, by using energy justice as an operational complement and by stating its empirical claims in a form that can be examined.

The framework yields six propositions about acceptance, agency, boundary, apparatus, justice and participation. These propositions are the basis for the conceptual framework in Chapter 4, which turns them into components, relationships and indicators. The distinction between the two sections is deliberate. Section 5 explains why the study thinks the way it does. Chapter 4 shows what the study will look at.
