# 5. Research Design and Methodology

## 5.1 Research paradigm

A paradigm is a set of assumptions about what can be known and how. The study works within a critical posthumanist paradigm, and this has four consequences for the design.

The first is ontological. Reality is treated as relational: entities, including technologies and households, acquire their properties through the relations in which they are enrolled. This does not mean that the digester is not real. It means that what the digester is, for the purpose of the study, includes the water, the feedstock, the space, the labour and the rules that sustain it.

The second is epistemological. Knowledge is treated as situated and partial. The researcher does not stand outside the settlement and observe it. The researcher is part of the arrangement, and the knowledge produced is knowledge from a particular position. This is not a weakness to be corrected by more rigorous detachment. It is a condition to be documented.

The third is methodological. If the object of study is a configuration, then the methods have to capture relations rather than variables. That points toward qualitative methods that can follow actors, materials and practices, and toward analytical tools that can represent feedback and interdependence.

The fourth is axiological. The study is not neutral about justice. It asks whether the arrangement improved or worsened the position of the people who live in it, and it reports the answer even when the answer is unflattering.

These commitments place the study within the interpretive and critical traditions rather than the positivist one. That placement has implications for how quality is judged, which are addressed in Section 5.7. It does not imply a rejection of technical evidence. The study records gas yields, water use, cost and maintenance events, and it treats them as data about the configuration rather than as background.

## 5.2 Research design

The study uses a single embedded case study, organised through the four stages of Design Research Methodology (DRM). The two are complementary rather than competing. DRM provides the internal logic of a design-oriented project: it specifies what has to be clarified, described, prescribed and evaluated. The case study provides the empirical logic: it specifies how evidence is collected from a bounded instance and how claims are generalised.

### 5.2.1 Design Research Methodology

DRM was developed by Blessing and Chakrabarti (2009) for research in engineering design, and it has been widely used in engineering management. It distinguishes four stages.

*Stage 1: Clarification of requirements.* The researcher establishes what the problem is, what the current situation looks like, what the desired situation would be, and what criteria would show that an intervention had succeeded. In this study, Stage 1 covers the literature review, the theoretical framework, the conceptual framework, site scoping and the ethics application. The output is a set of requirements for the SETILL framework and for the biogas configuration, together with the criteria against which the pilot will be evaluated.

*Stage 2: Descriptive study I.* The researcher examines the current situation in depth, to understand why it is as it is. In this study, Stage 2 covers the key-informant interviews, the resident focus groups, the transect walks, the household energy diaries and the photovoice work. The output is an evidence-based account of how energy services are currently produced in the settlement, who does the work, what fails and why.

*Stage 3: Prescriptive study.* The researcher develops and specifies an intervention that is intended to move the situation toward the desired state. In this study, Stage 3 covers the co-design workshops, the specification of the SETILL model in its site-specific form, the specification of the biogas configuration and the construction of the prototype. The output is a documented design with explicit assumptions and explicit requirements.

*Stage 4: Descriptive study II.* The researcher evaluates the intervention against the criteria established in Stage 1, and revises the understanding of the problem in the light of the results. In this study, Stage 4 covers the pilot evaluation, the justice assessment, the revision of the framework and the feedback of findings to the community.

The stages are not a waterfall. Stage 4 feeds back to Stage 2 and Stage 3, and a finding in Stage 2 can send the researcher back to Stage 1. The design allows for two full cycles and a partial third, subject to time and access.

### 5.2.2 Why a case study

A case study is appropriate when the research asks how or why a phenomenon works in its real-life context, when the researcher cannot manipulate behaviour, and when the boundaries between the phenomenon and its context are not clear (Yin, 2009). All three conditions hold here. The study asks how a configuration produces or undermines acceptance, it cannot control the conditions under which households use energy, and the context is not separable from the technology.

The case is embedded rather than holistic. Within the settlement there are four units of analysis: households, women's groups, the municipal service (waste, energy and planning functions) and the technology team (developers, artisans and researchers). The units are analysed separately and then brought together, because the configuration is produced at the intersection between them.

Flyvbjerg (2006) argues that the case study is undervalued in social science because of a mistaken belief that it cannot contribute to knowledge. His argument is that a well-chosen case can produce knowledge that is not available from statistical samples, particularly where the aim is to understand mechanisms rather than to estimate frequencies. That is the aim here. The study does not claim that its findings describe all South African informal settlements. It claims that the findings describe a configuration of a type that recurs, and that the framework can be tested elsewhere.

Analytical generalisation, in this sense, works through theory rather than through sampling. The reader asks whether the mechanism identified here would operate in another setting with similar conditions, and the study provides enough detail about the conditions for that judgement to be made.

### 5.5.1 Interviews

Semi-structured interviews will be used with key informants and with a subset of households. The interview guide is organised around the ten components of the framework but does not follow them mechanically. It begins with the person's own account of how energy is obtained and used, then moves to the points at which it fails, then to the arrangements that surround it, and finally to questions of decision-making and fairness.

Interviews will be conducted in the language the participant prefers, with interpretation where the researcher is not fluent. Interviews will be audio-recorded with consent, transcribed, and where necessary translated with the translation checked by a second speaker. Translation is an analytical act and will be documented as such.

### 5.5.2 Focus groups

Focus groups will be held separately for women, men, youth and tenants. The separation is deliberate. Mixed groups in this context tend to reproduce existing authority, with older men speaking first and at length. Separate groups allow other accounts to emerge. A final mixed session will be held to test whether the separate accounts survive when the groups are brought together.

The groups will be facilitated by a trained facilitator of the same gender where possible, with a note-taker. Discussions will be recorded with consent and transcribed.

### 5.5.3 Transect walks

A transect walk is a structured walk through the settlement with a resident guide, recording what is observed and what the guide says about it. The method produces spatial evidence that interviews miss: where the water points are, how far the wood is, which plots have space for a digester, where the waste accumulates, which paths flood, and how the settlement's edges are policed.

Walks will be conducted at different times of day, because a settlement at six in the morning is not the same place as at six in the evening. Notes and photographs will be recorded with consent, and locations will be described in a way that does not identify individuals.

### 5.5.4 Household energy diaries

Diaries capture what interviews miss: the ordinary sequence of a day. Participating households will be given a simple diary, in the local language, with pictograms for fuel types and a page for each day. A fieldworker will visit twice a week to support completion, because written diaries exclude participants with limited literacy.

The diary records what was cooked, on what, for how long, at what cost; when the electricity was off; what was used instead; who did the work; and what went wrong. The record is imperfect, and the analysis will treat it as an account produced with the researcher rather than as an objective log.

### 5.5.5 Photovoice

Photovoice asks participants to photograph what matters to them and to explain the images in a group discussion. It shifts the framing from the researcher's questions to the participant's attention. The method is used here for two reasons: it produces evidence about material conditions that residents consider important rather than what the project considers important, and it creates a record that can be shown to the municipality.

Ethical care is required. Photographs can identify people and dwellings. Participants will be trained in consent for photography, will be asked not to photograph identifiable individuals without permission, and will retain control over which images are used in publications.

### 5.5.6 Co-design workshops

Four workshops will be held in Stage 3. The first frames the problem and tests whether the diagnosis produced in Stage 2 is recognised by residents. The second develops the service model: who operates the system, who pays, who decides, what happens when it fails. The third specifies the technical configuration: size, siting, feedstock, water, stove, storage and spare parts. The fourth reviews the prototype and agrees the evaluation criteria.

The workshops will use visual and physical materials rather than text, because text-based workshops exclude participants who are not confident readers. Disagreement will be recorded rather than resolved by the facilitator, and minority positions will be carried into the analysis.

### 5.5.7 Documents and secondary data

The study will also collect documents: municipal Integrated Development Plans, waste and energy policies, project records from previous interventions, equipment specifications and maintenance logs. These are used to establish the institutional conditions and to check accounts given in interviews.

### 5.5.8 Internet research

A final collection approach is desk-based: the structured use of internet resources for the institutional and documentary layer of the descriptive study. This is listed as a method of the study because a part of the evidence the design requires — municipal documents, policy instruments, the utility's public data on supply and tariffs, regulatory filings, and the documented history of prior projects in the municipality — exists only in that form, and because the study's epistemology requires that all sources of evidence about the configuration be named and treated comparably rather than leaving the documentary layer implicit.

The planned practice is conventional but explicit. Sources will be identified against the boundary statement (C1), so that the documentary collection covers the actors and systems the boundary names rather than whatever is easiest to find. Each class of source — municipal and provincial documents, national policy and planning instruments, utility and regulatory publications, and prior project reports — will be logged with its provenance, date and access route in the same evidence register as the field-generated material. Where documents disagree, the disagreement is treated as data about the institutional field rather than as noise to be resolved.

Two quality matters are handled alongside the trustworthiness strategies set out below. The first is currency: official web resources change, so every document is stored with its access date and a copy, and figures that depend on administrative data are tied to the release from which they were taken. The second is standing: documents are read as the products of positions, in the same way that interviews are — a municipal annual report is an account produced by one stakeholder in the configuration, and the analysis treats it as such. The documentary layer is triangulated against the field-generated evidence rather than being used as its arbiter.

### 5.6.1 Reflexive thematic analysis

Thematic analysis is used in its reflexive form (Braun and Clarke, 2006, 2021). The approach treats themes as produced by the analyst's engagement with the data rather than as latent objects waiting to be found. Coding is both deductive, using the ten components, and inductive, allowing codes that the framework did not anticipate.

The six phases will be followed: familiarisation, initial coding, generating themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and writing. Coding will be conducted on the transcripts in the original language where possible, with translated extracts used for reporting. A second coder will code a subset of ten transcripts independently, and disagreements will be discussed and recorded. The purpose of the second coder is not to establish inter-rater reliability in a positivist sense but to test whether the coding frame is being applied consistently and to surface the analyst's assumptions.

Braun and Clarke (2021) insist that reflexivity is not a separate section appended to the method but a practice that runs through the analysis. The study will keep a reflexive journal, and extracts from it will be used in the methodology chapter to show how interpretations changed.

### 5.6.2 Situational mapping

Situational mapping, developed within grounded theory by Clarke (2005), produces three kinds of map: situational maps of the major human and non-human elements and their relations; social worlds and arena maps of collective actors; and positional maps of the discursive positions taken on contested issues.

The method suits the study because it can represent non-human elements without reducing them to context. A map can show the digester, the water point, the meter, the landfill and the ward committee in the same frame, with the relations between them. Positional maps are particularly useful for the disagreements that the study expects: on whether the settlement should be formalised, on whether women should manage the system, on whether the municipality can be trusted.

### 5.6.3 Causal loop diagramming

Causal loop diagrams represent feedback: how a change in one variable affects another, and how the effect returns. They will be used for two purposes. First, to represent the participants' own accounts of why systems succeed or fail, built with them in workshops. Second, to represent the researcher's synthesis of the evidence.

A diagram will be built for the settlement's current energy arrangements, and a second for the proposed configuration. The comparison is itself a finding: it shows which loops the intervention is intended to change and which it is likely to reinforce. A loop that reinforces exclusion, for example, may survive a technically successful installation.

The diagrams are not simulation models. They are qualitative representations, and they will be reported as such.

### 5.6.4 Diffractive reading

Diffraction, in Barad's (2007) sense, is a practice of reading insights through one another rather than against one another. In this study it is used to compare data sets that describe the same phenomenon from different positions: a household diary and an official's account of the same service; a woman's description of cooking and a designer's specification of the stove.

The procedure is simple in principle and demanding in practice. The two accounts are set side by side, and the analysis asks what each makes visible, what each obscures, and what difference the difference makes. The output is not a synthesis that resolves the disagreement but an account of how the phenomenon is produced differently in different apparatuses. That account is directly relevant to RQ3, because it shows that acceptance is not a single fact about a single object.

### 5.6.5 From analysis to framework revision

The analysis is not only a description of the case. It is a test of the framework. Three outcomes are possible for each component. The component may be confirmed as important and adequately specified. It may be confirmed as important but under-specified, in which case the specification is revised. Or it may turn out to be unimportant in this case, in which case the framework is revised to say so.

The revision will be documented in a table that shows the original component, the evidence, and the revised version. That table is one of the study's contributions, because it shows what a framework looks like after it has been used.

## 5.7 Trustworthiness

Qualitative research is judged by criteria that differ from those of statistical work. The study adopts the eight "big tent" criteria proposed by Tracy (2010) and the trustworthiness framework of Lincoln and Guba (1985), operationalised through the strategies in Table 5.5.

Three strategies deserve emphasis. The first is negative case analysis: the study will actively seek cases that contradict the emerging argument, and will report them. A household that uses the system successfully for reasons the framework did not anticipate is as important as one that abandons it.

The second is the audit trail. All coding decisions, sampling decisions and analytical memos will be retained and indexed, so that an examiner can trace how a claim was produced.

The third is the treatment of the researcher's position. The researcher is a doctoral candidate at a South African university, working with a supervisor whose work informs the framework, in a settlement where the researcher is an outsider. That position shapes access, interpretation and what participants are willing to say. It will be described in the thesis rather than concealed.

Three ethical issues deserve more than a line in a table.

The first is the ethics of a design study that builds something. A pilot that installs a system creates expectations and relationships that outlast the research. If the system fails after the project ends, the community bears the cost of the researcher's learning. The study manages this through C10: the exit plan is designed before the prototype is built, and the decision to build depends on whether a maintenance arrangement can be agreed.

The second is the ethics of representation. The settlement will appear in a thesis, in publications and possibly in policy discussions. The account will describe poverty, failure and conflict. Participants have a legitimate interest in how they are represented, and the study will share draft findings with them and record their responses.

The third is the ethics of the framework itself. A posthumanist framework that treats people as elements of an assemblage risks describing them in a way that diminishes their agency. The writing will guard against this by keeping human voices in the text and by avoiding a vocabulary that turns suffering into ontology.

## 5.10 Limitations of the design

The design has four limitations that should be stated in advance.

First, a single case cannot support claims about the prevalence of a configuration. The study will produce an account of how a mechanism worked in one place. Readers may judge whether it applies elsewhere, but the study will not make that judgement for them.

Second, the researcher's presence changes the configuration. A settlement that is being studied is not the same as one that is not. The reflexive record addresses this but does not eliminate it.

Third, the study depends on a technology that may not perform as intended. Biogas is sensitive to feedstock, water and temperature. If the system underperforms, the study will report that, and the analysis will treat the underperformance as evidence about the configuration rather than as a failed experiment.

Fourth, the timeframe is three years, and the durability of the arrangement can only be assessed within that window. A follow-up beyond the doctoral period would be valuable, and the exit plan will include arrangements for one.

## 5.11 Summary of the methodology

The study is a single embedded case study conducted through four DRM stages in an informal settlement in Greater Tzaneen. Data are collected through interviews, focus groups, transect walks, diaries, photovoice, co-design workshops and documents, and analysed through reflexive thematic analysis, situational mapping, causal loop diagramming and diffractive reading. Quality is addressed through the eight big-tent criteria and a documented audit trail. Ethics are addressed through procedural clearance and through a relational practice that includes an exit plan and shared findings. The next section describes the case site.

## 5.12 Context and case study: Tzaneen

### 5.12.1 Why Tzaneen

The choice of Greater Tzaneen is not arbitrary, and it is not a convenience sample in the ordinary sense. The municipality combines the conditions that the study needs: a large population with high levels of service connection and low levels of service quality; an economy built on agriculture and agro-processing, with the waste streams that follow; a climate that is warm enough for anaerobic digestion to be feasible; and a municipal administration that is under capacity but not absent.

It is also a place where the contradictions of the South African transition are visible in a small area. The town has a formal economy, a hospital, schools and a retail sector. Around it are settlements with different degrees of formality, different tenure arrangements and different access to services. The distance between them is a few kilometres and several decades of investment.

Finally, the municipality is in Limpopo, a province that receives less attention in the energy transition literature than Gauteng or the Western Cape, despite having a large population and significant renewable potential. A study located there adds to a thin evidence base.

### 5.12.2 The municipality

Greater Tzaneen Local Municipality (LIM333) is one of four local municipalities in the Mopani District. It covers approximately 2,896 square kilometres and had a population of 478,254 at the 2022 census, up from 389,623 in 2011, a growth rate of about two per cent a year (Statistics South Africa, 2023). There are roughly 129,600 households, with an average size of 3.7 people. About half are headed by women. Just over 16 per cent of adults have no schooling, and under nine per cent have a higher education qualification.

| Piped water inside dwelling | 27.1% | Water access is a constraint for digestion and for cooking |

The contrast between the electricity figure and the water, sanitation and refuse figures is the point of the table. Almost every household is connected for lighting. Fewer than a third have water inside the dwelling, and about a quarter have refuse collected. A household in this municipality is likely to be connected to the grid and to manage its own water, waste and cooking fuel. That combination is exactly the configuration the study is about.

Two caveats are necessary. The first concerns the dwelling classification. A figure of 97.1 per cent formal dwellings is difficult to reconcile with the visible presence of informal settlements in and around Tzaneen. The census category counts a structure as formal if it is a house or a flat on a formal erf, and backyard rooms and rented shacks on a formal plot may be counted within the formal dwelling. The statistic therefore understates the number of households living in informal or insecure arrangements. The study will not rely on it, and will establish tenure arrangements through its own scoping.

The second concerns the electricity figure. "Electricity for lighting" records whether a household uses electricity for lighting, not whether it has a legal connection, a reliable supply or enough capacity to cook. A household with an informal connection from a neighbour's meter is counted as using electricity. The indicator measures presence, not service.

### 5.12.3 Energy in the municipality

The municipality's energy profile is shaped by three factors.

The first is the national grid. Distribution in the area is carried out by Eskom rather than by a municipal distributor, which means that outages, tariff decisions and connection processes are administered at a distance. Municipal officials can advocate but cannot decide.

The second is the local economy. Tzaneen is an agricultural service town. Agro-processing generates organic waste: fruit waste, sawdust, poultry litter, abattoir waste and market waste. Some of it is already used, for animal feed or composting. Some is dumped. A digester would compete for a fraction of this stream, and the competition is part of what the study has to understand.

The third is the pattern of household fuel use. As in much of Limpopo, households combine electricity with wood, paraffin and LPG. Wood is often collected rather than bought, particularly in peri-urban areas where there is access to trees. Paraffin is bought in small quantities and is expensive per unit of energy. LPG is used where it is available and affordable. The combination changes with the season, the price and the reliability of supply.

The study will not attempt to quantify the municipal energy balance. It will document the household-level configuration and the institutional conditions that shape it.

### 5.12.4 Waste and water

Biogas depends on a feedstock stream and a water supply, and both are constrained in the municipality.

Refuse removal reaches about a quarter of households. The rest manage waste on site, burn it, bury it or dump it. Organic waste is therefore dispersed rather than concentrated, which is a problem for a digester that needs a steady supply. The design response is either to aggregate waste from a small number of sources (a market, a poultry operation, a group of households) or to size the digester to the supply that can be guaranteed. The choice between these is a design decision that the co-design workshops will address.

Water is supplied through a network that reaches most households, but inside connections are limited and supply is intermittent in some areas. A digester needs water in a ratio to feedstock, and the ratio competes with cooking, cleaning and drinking. In a settlement where water is carried, a technology that needs several hundred litres a week is a different proposition from one that needs fifty. The study will measure water use and will treat it as a design constraint rather than an operating detail.

### 5.12.5 The settlement

The specific settlement has not been selected. This is deliberate, and it should be read as a statement about the state of the work rather than as an omission.

The scoping phase in months one to six will assess candidate settlements against the criteria in Table 5.1. The assessment will include a site visit, conversations with community structures and a meeting with the municipality. The outcome will be documented in a short site selection memorandum, appended to the thesis, that states which criteria each candidate met and why the chosen site was preferred.

What can be said now is what the study expects to find, based on the literature and on preliminary conversations. The settlement is likely to be characterised by a mix of owner-occupied and rented dwellings, an electricity supply that is connected but unreliable and costly, a reliance on multiple cooking fuels, organic waste that is not collected, and water that is available at a shared point. It is likely to have at least one organised structure, whether a residents' committee, a women's group or a church-based organisation, and it is likely to have experience of previous projects that shapes how a new one is received. That last point matters. A settlement that has been promised services before will not take a new promise at face value, and the study should not expect it to.

### 5.12.6 Relevance to Engineering Management

The case is an Engineering Management case, not only an energy case, and the connection should be made explicit.

Engineering Management is concerned with the design, operation and improvement of systems that combine technical and organisational elements. The Tzaneen case is exactly such a system. A community-scale digester is a technical system with a supply chain, a maintenance regime, a cost structure, a governance arrangement and a set of users whose practices determine whether it works. The decisions that determine its fate are management decisions: siting, sizing, contracting, staffing, pricing, training, scheduling and risk allocation.

The study contributes to the field in three ways. It provides a method for specifying the non-technical requirements of a technical system in a low-income context. It provides an evaluation approach that treats justice as a system performance criterion rather than as an external constraint. And it provides evidence about the organisational conditions under which a decentralised energy system is sustained.

These contributions are relevant beyond biogas. The same questions arise for solar water heating, for container-based sanitation, for water ATMs and for small-scale PV. The framework is intended to be portable across these technologies, and the case is intended to show how.

### 5.12.7 Access, permissions and relationships

Access will be negotiated in three steps.

The first is institutional. The study will seek written permission from the municipality to conduct research in the area, and will engage the relevant departments (waste management, electricity, planning and local economic development) as participants rather than as gatekeepers. The engagement will include a commitment to share findings.

The second is community-level. The study will approach the recognised community structures, explain the purpose and the limits of the project, and ask for permission to work in the area. It will not offer benefits in exchange for access. Where a structure has concerns about the project, those concerns will be recorded and addressed before fieldwork begins.

The third is household-level. Participation will be sought individually, with a clear statement that refusal has no consequence for service entitlement. Participants will be able to withdraw at any point, and data already collected will be removed on request where that is practicable.

The study will also establish a small advisory group of residents, municipal officials and a technical expert, which will meet twice a year. The group is not a decision-making body, but it provides a channel for critique that does not depend on the researcher's judgement.

### 5.12.8 Summary of the case

Greater Tzaneen offers a setting in which nearly every household is connected to the grid and fewer than a third have water inside the dwelling, where refuse collection reaches a quarter of households and organic waste is dispersed, and where a large share of households are headed by women and depend on multiple fuels for cooking. Those conditions make the configuration, rather than the artefact, the central problem.

The settlement will be selected during scoping. The study will document the selection, describe the site in detail, and treat the description as part of the evidence rather than as background. Chapter 6 states what the study expects to contribute.
