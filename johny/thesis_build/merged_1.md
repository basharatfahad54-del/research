# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Background and context

### 1.1.1 A grid that is changing, unevenly

South Africa generates most of its electricity from coal, and the consequences of that dependence are now felt in three places at once: in the account that households pay, in the reliability of the supply they receive, and in the carbon intensity of the economy. The national utility, Eskom, supplies the greater part of the country's power from a fleet of ageing stations. Scheduled load-shedding reached record levels in 2023. It has since been suspended for long periods, but the system remains vulnerable to breakdowns, and the price of electricity has risen faster than household incomes. For a middle-income household, these are inconveniences and costs to be managed. For a household in an informal settlement, they determine whether there is a hot meal, whether a child can study after dark, and whether the money set aside for electricity runs out before the month does.

The aggregate picture looks better than the household picture. Around nine in ten South African households now have a grid connection (Statistics South Africa, 2023), a substantial achievement compared with the early 1990s. Yet connection is not the same as access. A connected household may still be unable to afford enough electricity, may receive an intermittent supply, may live in a dwelling where the wiring cannot carry a cooking load, or may buy electricity through a landlord's meter at a price that includes an informal markup. Energy poverty in South Africa is therefore less a problem of missing infrastructure than a problem of how infrastructure, income, tariffs, dwelling conditions and household practice fit together. That distinction matters for this study because it shifts attention from the presence of a technology to the arrangement in which the technology works, or fails to work.

Policy has begun to reflect this. The Presidential Climate Commission's just transition framework accepts that decarbonisation and social equity cannot be separated (Presidential Climate Commission, 2022), and the Integrated Resource Plan sets out a shift away from coal alongside a commitment to maintain security of supply (Department of Mineral Resources and Energy, 2019). The Just Energy Transition Partnership has brought international finance and, with it, expectations of a transition that is procedurally fair as well as technically rapid. Recent research on the barriers to the transition in South Africa shows, however, that the obstacles are as much institutional and social as they are financial or technical (Molepo et al., 2025; Mirzania et al., 2023). Municipalities carry distribution responsibilities without the capacity or revenue to fulfil them. Communities that host generation or transmission infrastructure have rarely been consulted in a meaningful way. And the households that are least able to pay are the least able to wait for the benefits of a transition that is planned at national scale.

### 1.1.2 Informal settlements as socio-material configurations

Informal settlements are not a single kind of place. They differ in age, size, location, tenure arrangements, access to services and internal organisation. Some are on well-located land close to work; others are peripheral. Some have been upgraded with water, sanitation and electricity; others rely on shared taps, communal toilets and informal connections. What they share is a combination of insecure tenure, self-built housing and incomplete municipal recognition. That combination shapes what is possible technologically, and it shapes who is able to make decisions about the household.

Three features are particularly relevant here. The first is density. Dwellings are close together, access routes are narrow, and space for equipment is limited. A technology that needs a dedicated yard, a water supply or a stable foundation may not fit. The second is the relationship between landlord and tenant. Many households rent a room or a shack on someone else's plot. The meter may be in the landlord's name, the rent may include electricity, and the tenant may have little say over wiring or appliances. The third is fuel stacking. Households rarely use one fuel for everything. Electricity may be used for lighting, television and phone charging, while paraffin, firewood, charcoal or LPG are used for cooking and space heating. The choice is not a matter of habit alone. It reflects cost per unit of useful energy, the reliability of the supply, the size of the pot, the weather, the availability of fuel and the taste of the food (Musango, 2022; Musango et al., 2020).

Fuel stacking has consequences that a simple access metric cannot capture. Indoor smoke from solid fuels remains a significant health risk, particularly for women and young children. Paraffin is a common cause of fires and of accidental ingestion. Collecting wood takes time, and the time is usually taken from women. Cooking on an electric hotplate during load-shedding is impossible, so a household that has "access" may still keep a paraffin stove for the evenings when the power is off. These are not peripheral details. They are the substance of what an energy service is, and they are the reason why a technology that performs well in a laboratory can perform poorly in a settlement.

### 1.1.3 Living Labs and decentralised energy technologies

The Living Lab has become a common way of organising innovation in real-world settings. The term covers a family of arrangements in which users, researchers, firms and public bodies co-create, prototype and test solutions in the context where those solutions will be used (Bergvall-Kåreborn and Ståhlbröst, 2009; Leminen et al., 2012). Living Labs have been applied in information and communication technology, health, urban planning and, more recently, energy. A systematic review of the energy literature found that the concept is used in several different ways: as a physical space, as a methodology, as an organisation and as a network (Mbatha and Musango, 2022). The same review notes that energy Living Labs in African informal settlements are few, and that the evidence on their outcomes is thin.

The South African research programme on gendered energy innovation is the most direct precedent for this study. Over several years, a group of researchers working in poor urban environments developed a gender analysis framework (Anditi et al., 2022), a systems model of energy service security (Batinge et al., 2022), a multi-stakeholder co-design toolkit (Petrulaityte et al., 2022) and a process for setting up energy Living Labs (Mukama et al., 2022). Their work established that gender is not an add-on to energy innovation and that the security of energy services depends on social as well as technical arrangements. It also established a practical vocabulary for working with communities. This study builds on that programme rather than claiming to start from nothing.

Biogas is one of several decentralised technologies that could be examined. It is selected here for three reasons. First, anaerobic digestion converts organic waste into a combustible gas that can be used for cooking, which is the energy service that electricity most often fails to provide in informal settlements. Second, the technology makes the socio-material arrangement visible. A digester needs feedstock, water, space, a stable temperature, regular attention and somewhere to put the digestate. Each of those requirements connects the artefact to the household, the neighbourhood, the municipality and the waste stream. Third, community-scale biogas has a mixed record in southern Africa. Some installations work for years; many fail within a short period because of maintenance, feedstock shortages, water constraints or a lack of agreement about who is responsible for the system. That mixed record is analytically useful. It provides the variation that a study of acceptance needs.

Biogas is not presented here as a universal solution. It is a test case for a framework. If the framework can explain why a biogas system is taken up in one part of a settlement and abandoned in another, it should be transferable to other decentralised technologies, including solar water heating, improved cookstoves and small-scale PV.

### 1.1.4 Why this is an Engineering Management problem

An engineering manager working on an energy project in an informal settlement has to make decisions that are technical, organisational and political at the same time. Which technology? At what scale? Who owns it? Who maintains it? How is it paid for? Who is trained? What happens when a part fails? These questions are not answered by a technical specification. They are answered through negotiations between households, municipal officials, suppliers, funders and community leaders, and they are answered under conditions of uncertainty.

The discipline of Engineering Management has tools for these questions: requirements analysis, systems modelling, project and lifecycle management, risk assessment, technology assessment and operations management. What it has lacked is a theoretical account of the relationship between the technology and the social arrangement into which it is introduced. Without such an account, the tools are applied to the artefact and the arrangement is left to chance. This study argues that the arrangement is the proper object of design, and that a theory is needed to describe it.

## 1.2 Historical development and conceptual foundations of this study

### 1.2.1 The path to this study

The study did not begin with a technology. It began with an observation that recurs across the South African literature on poor urban and peri-urban environments: households may be connected to the grid and still be unable to obtain the energy services they need, because connection is only one of several conditions. What the household experiences is the service — cooking that does not fill the kitchen with smoke, light after dark, refrigeration, a hot meal without a paraffin risk — and the service fails whenever any of its conditions fails. The concept of the security of energy services, developed for poor urban environments in this research lineage, names that gap and organises this study around it (Musango et al., 2020).

The immediate ancestry of the study lies in a programme of South African work that has examined how energy innovations can be developed for poor urban environments and what hinders their mainstreaming. A structured review of the factors obstructing the mainstreaming of gendered energy interventions identified organisational, market and knowledge barriers that recur across cases (Oosthuizen et al., 2020). A systems model formalised the relationship between innovation in these environments and the security of the services households obtain, showing that the two are linked through feedback rather than through a one-way pipeline from invention to adoption (Batinge et al., 2022). A gender analysis framework made visible the different roles, burdens and forms of knowledge that household members bring to energy innovation (Anditi et al., 2022). The development of living labs for energy technology innovation in poor urban environments, and a co-design toolkit supporting collaboration among the stakeholders such labs require, carried this line of work into practice (Mukama et al., 2022; Petrulaityte et al., 2022). A systematic review of the Living Lab concept in the energy sector mapped where the concept has been applied and found that many applications were not designed to continue beyond the project that created them (Mbatha and Musango, 2022).

This study extends that lineage in three ways. First, it adopts an end-user-centred approach: the household members who use, maintain, pay for and abandon energy technologies are treated as the primary participants in design, rather than as beneficiaries or subjects, with attention directed to those who carry the largest energy burden in the household. Gender remains an analytically necessary axis within that approach, because the burdens and responsibilities of energy use are unevenly distributed, and the framework retains the tools developed in the lineage above for making those distributions visible. Second, it binds the innovation process in systems thinking from the outset: boundaries, feedback and evaluation are designed into the lab rather than added to it, in the manner set out in Chapters 3 and 4. Third, it specifies a site and a technology: the study is located in the Greater Tzaneen Municipality in Limpopo Province, South Africa, and the technology focus is a household- and community-scale biogas system for cooking, developed and tested through the lab up to pilot scale.

The historical narrative also explains what this study is not. It is not a continuation of a completed project whose results are reported here. All empirical work — the diagnostic study, the co-design workshops, the prototype pilot and the evaluation — is planned and described in the future tense throughout this document. This is set out formally in Chapter 5 and in the ethical and risk chapters that follow it.

### 1.2.2 The Energy Cultures framework and energy behaviour

The diagnostic vocabulary of the study comes from the Energy Cultures framework. The framework holds that household energy behaviour is produced by the interlocking of three components: material culture, the physical objects and infrastructure available to the household; energy practices, the routines through which energy services are actually obtained; and cognitive norms, the expectations and beliefs that make one practice seem normal and another strange. No single component determines behaviour. Change in one component is constrained and shaped by the others, which is why interventions that change only the technology so often fail to change the outcome (Stephenson et al., 2010).

The framework matters to this study for two reasons. The first is diagnostic: it gives the study a way of describing a settlement's energy life that does not reduce to a list of attitudes. The second is designative: if behaviour is an outcome of an interlocking triad, then a technology intervention is incomplete unless the material conditions, the practices and the norms around the technology are specified together. This insight carries directly into the conceptual framework in Chapter 4, where the configuration — the arrangement of fuel, water, space, labour and rules around the technology — becomes the object of design, and into the planned methods in Chapter 5, which are chosen to capture practice and materiality rather than opinion alone.

### 1.2.3 Sustainable energy access and the systemic Living Lab

Two further concepts complete the foundations. The first is sustainable energy access, understood here in terms of services rather than supply: a household has secure and sustainable access when it can obtain the energy services it needs across normal and disrupted conditions, at a cost and risk that do not undermine the rest of household life. This is the household-level reading of security developed in the lineage above (Musango et al., 2020), and it makes the distribution of access an ethical question rather than merely an engineering one, because services can be secured for some households at the expense of others.

The second concept is the systemic Living Lab. Living Labs are real-world arrangements in which users, researchers, firms and public bodies co-create and test innovations under conditions of use. The configuration proposed in this study — the SETILL — is systematic in a stronger sense than the frequent use of the word in the literature suggests: its boundary, its stakeholder set, its feedback structure and its evaluation criteria are derived from systems theory and from the justice literature, not merely announced. The definitions, typologies and documented applications of Living Labs are reviewed in Chapter 2, the theoretical justification is given in Chapter 3, and the operational framework — including who the stakeholders are and what each contributes — is set out in Chapter 4.

## 1.3 Problem statement

Energy interventions in South African informal settlements tend to follow a similar pattern. A technology is selected, often for good technical reasons. A pilot is funded. The equipment is installed. Training is provided. For a period, the technology works. Then something changes: a part fails and cannot be sourced, the feedstock becomes too expensive, the person who maintained the system moves away, the landlord raises the rent, or the promised service does not arrive. Use declines, the equipment is abandoned, and the project is recorded as a technical success that did not achieve its social objectives.

There are three explanations for this pattern, and this study takes all three seriously. The first is analytical. The models that dominate applied energy research treat acceptance as an individual decision made about a finished artefact. The Technology Acceptance Model and its successors ask whether a person perceives a technology as useful and easy to use (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Those questions can be answered in a survey, but they cannot explain why the same technology is used in one street and abandoned in the next, or why a household that reports a positive attitude stops using a system after three months. The models abstract away the very things that matter: the cost of fuel, the reliability of the supply, the space available, the division of labour in the household, and the terms on which the dwelling is occupied.

The second explanation is methodological. Participation is now a standard requirement in energy projects, but it is often consultative rather than consequential. Residents are invited to a meeting, their views are recorded, and the design is finalised elsewhere. Participation of this kind can legitimise a decision that has already been taken. It can also raise expectations that the project cannot meet, and it can give an advantage to those who are already comfortable speaking in public. The literature on participation has described these failure modes for decades (Arnstein, 1969), yet they recur in energy projects because the incentives to consult are stronger than the incentives to share control.

The third explanation is evaluative. Projects are usually judged by outputs: the number of connections, the installed capacity, the number of people trained. These are legitimate measures, but they say little about whether the intervention was just. A project can connect a settlement and still leave the poorest households paying more per unit than wealthier ones. It can reduce smoke in the kitchen and transfer the burden of collecting feedstock to girls. It can create a community asset that is captured by a local elite. Without criteria that make these distributions visible, an evaluation can report success while the underlying injustice persists.

The problem, stated compactly, is this:

[NOTE] **Problem statement.** Energy technology interventions in South African informal settlements frequently fail to produce durable improvements in energy access because they are designed as transfers of finished artefacts rather than as reconfigurations of the socio-material arrangements through which energy services are produced. Engineering management lacks a systematic means of involving end-users as co-designers, of anticipating how materials, infrastructures, institutions and household practices will shape acceptance, and of judging whether the resulting arrangement is just. The consequence is a recurring pattern of misaligned technologies, abandoned pilots, unevenly distributed benefits and costs, and continued energy poverty.

## 1.4 Research aim and objectives

The aim of the study is to develop, apply and evaluate a Systemic Living Lab for Just Energy Transition (SETILL) that supports the co-design of responsive energy technology innovations in a South African informal settlement, and to assess whether the resulting arrangement improves energy access and advances energy justice.

The aim is pursued through five objectives. Each objective corresponds to one research question and to a stage of the research design.

1. To establish how Systems Living Lab concepts frame the relationship between inequality, energy security, energy poverty, energy technology innovation and informal settlements, and to derive a theoretical account of that relationship from critical posthumanism.

2. To determine the socio-material factors that shape the involvement and participation of end-users in decision-making, co-design, co-creation, co-research, prototyping and testing of energy technology innovations in South African informal settlements.

3. To examine the cultural elements, norms, practices and technological mechanisms that influence the acceptance of end-user energy innovations in informal settlements.

4. To develop and evaluate a Systems Living Lab, operationalised through a community-scale biogas pilot, that engages end-users and other stakeholders in participatory design in a real-world setting.

5. To evaluate whether the SETILL framework can function as a coherent theoretical framework and a practical tool for improving energy security, reducing energy poverty and advancing energy justice.

## 1.5 Research questions

The study is guided by five research questions. They follow the questions set out in the research brief, with minor editorial changes for clarity. The questions are deliberately open. They ask how and which, rather than whether a single variable has an effect, because the study is concerned with mechanisms and configurations rather than with the estimation of causal parameters across a population.

RQ1. How do Systems Living Lab concepts frame the relationship between inequality, energy security, energy poverty, energy technology innovations and informal settlements?

RQ2. Which factors determine the involvement and participation of end-users in decision-making, co-design, co-creation, co-research, prototyping and testing of energy technology innovations in informal settlements in South Africa?

RQ3. Which cultural elements, norms, practices and technological mechanisms influence the level of acceptance of end-user energy innovations in informal settlements in South Africa?

RQ4. Which mechanisms can be implemented to improve the acceptance of energy innovations and the participation and contribution of end-users to energy security services in poor informal settlements in South Africa?

RQ5. Can a Systems Living Lab that advances responsive energy technology innovations through participatory design engage end-users and other stakeholders in real-world settings in ways that address energy poverty and enhance security of and access to energy services?

## 1.6 Justification of the study

### 1.6.1 Academic justification

The study addresses a gap at the intersection of four literatures: energy justice, Living Lab methodology, socio-technical systems thinking and critical posthumanism. Each literature is well developed in its own field. Energy justice has produced a robust normative vocabulary but has said little about how an engineer should design a prototype. Living Lab research has produced detailed accounts of co-creation but has tended to evaluate outputs rather than justice. Systems thinking has supplied tools for mapping complexity but has often remained functionalist in its assumptions. Posthumanism has produced a sophisticated ontology of human–technology relations but has rarely been applied to energy infrastructure in the Global South.

The contribution is not the invention of a new theory. It is the construction of a defensible bridge between these bodies of work, tested against a case. That kind of bridge-building is a legitimate scholarly contribution, but it has to be done carefully. It requires that each tradition be represented on its own terms, that the points of incompatibility be stated rather than smoothed over, and that the resulting framework generate propositions that could fail.

### 1.6.2 Practical justification

For practitioners, the study offers three things. The first is a design protocol that treats the socio-material configuration, rather than the artefact, as the unit of intervention. The second is a set of evaluation criteria that make distributional and procedural questions visible alongside technical performance. The third is evidence about what happens when a community-scale biogas system is co-designed in an informal settlement, including the conditions under which it is likely to be maintained. These are modest outputs, but they are the kind that project managers and municipal officials can use.

### 1.6.3 Policy and sector justification

At the policy level, the study speaks to the implementation of the just transition framework at municipal scale. Municipalities are responsible for distribution, for integrated development planning and, in many cases, for the informal settlements that national policy treats as temporary. The study's attention to procedural justice, to the terms on which residents are included, and to the distribution of costs and benefits is directly relevant to the design of municipal energy programmes and to the evaluation of JETP-funded projects.

### 1.6.4 Methodological justification

The study contributes a methodological approach that combines reflexive thematic analysis, situational mapping, causal loop diagramming and diffractive reading. The combination is unusual. Each method is established, but they are rarely used together, and the epistemological work of combining them has not been done. The study will document how the combination was applied, what it produced that a single method would have missed, and where it created tensions. That documentation is itself a contribution to qualitative energy research.

## 1.7 Scope and delimitations

The study is deliberately bounded. It examines one informal settlement in Greater Tzaneen, Limpopo Province, over a period of three years. The empirical work focuses on household cooking and on a community-scale biogas system. It does not attempt to model the national electricity system, to evaluate the full life-cycle impacts of biogas, or to estimate willingness to pay across a representative sample. Those questions are important, but they require different designs and different resources.

The study is a proposal. No fieldwork has been conducted and no empirical data exist. The methods described in Chapter 5 are intended methods. They will be implemented only after ethical clearance and after agreements have been reached with the municipality, the community leadership and the households involved.

Four further delimitations should be stated. First, the study does not claim that biogas is the best technology for the site. It claims that biogas is a useful case through which to examine the framework. Second, the study does not aim for statistical generalisation. It aims for analytical generalisation to theory, in the sense described by Yin (2009) and defended by Flyvbjerg (2006). Third, the study does not evaluate the health impacts of indoor air pollution using clinical measures. It records reported symptoms and observed conditions, and it treats them as one dimension of wellbeing rather than as a medical endpoint. Fourth, the study does not treat "the community" as a unified actor. It deliberately samples for difference, including tenants, landlords, women's groups, youth and municipal officials.

## 1.8 Key terms

Several terms are used in this study in a specific sense. The definitions below are working definitions; they are refined in Chapters 4 and 5 of the thesis.

|---|---|

## 1.9 Structure of the thesis

The document follows the structure described in this chapter. Chapter 2 reviews the literature and identifies the gaps. Chapter 3 sets out the theoretical framework, beginning with the general function of theory in doctoral research and then developing critical posthumanism as the primary lens.
