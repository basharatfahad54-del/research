## 3.8 Systems approaches: systems thinking, system dynamics and systems engineering

The framework's claim to be systemic is built from three related but distinct traditions: systems thinking as an epistemology, system dynamics as a modelling practice, and systems engineering as a design discipline. This section sets out what each contributes, because the terms are used loosely in the Living Lab literature and the looseness has consequences. A lab can use the vocabulary of all three and remain conventional in its structure; the framework requires their substance, and Chapter 4 specifies where each enters the design.

### 3.8.1 Systems thinking and the systems approach

Systems thinking is first of all a way of organising inquiry. Checkland's foundational argument is that a system is not an object lying in the world but a device for making sense of one: the analyst declares a boundary, names the elements inside it and the relationships among them, and accepts that another analyst could declare it differently (Checkland, 1981). The declaration is consequential. Whoever draws the boundary of "the project" decides whose costs appear in the accounts, whose knowledge counts as evidence and whose exclusion is invisible. Critical systems work makes that consequence explicit and asks, of any framing, whose interests it serves (Midgley, 2000).

The second resource is the behaviour of systems. Stocks accumulate, flows move material and information between them, feedback loops couple the parts, and delays separate causes from their effects; from these simple elements, systems produce behaviour — growth, oscillation, collapse, resistance to policy — that cannot be read off any single part (Meadows, 2008). For energy interventions in settlements this is not a metaphor. A digester that is abandoned when the water supply fails is a system behaving, not an attitude softening; a tariff that protects revenue and shifts arrears risk onto tenants is a feedback structure with a distributional signature.

Taken together, these resources discipline the study in two ways. The boundary critique is load-bearing rather than rhetorical: the framework in Chapter 4 makes the drawing of the boundary an explicit, recorded and revisable decision. And the attention to feedback is what distinguishes an architecture from a vocabulary: the lab is designed so that use changes design, evaluation changes governance, and the lab's own interventions are recorded as inputs to its analysis.

### 3.8.2 System dynamics and causal loop diagramming

System dynamics supplies the formal vocabulary in which the feedback claims are expressed. Its method represents a system as stocks and flows regulated by information feedback, and treats the structure as the generator of behaviour over time (Sterman, 2000). Within that tradition, the causal loop diagram — variables connected by signed links, closed into loops of reinforcing or balancing polarity — is the standard first formalisation, and it is deliberately qualitative: it disciplines the argument without demanding the data that a simulation would.

The study uses the qualitative instrument, not the simulation. The planned analysis includes the construction of causal loop diagrams with participants and stakeholders (Chapter 5), and the diagrams serve three purposes in the design. They force the specification of relationships that would otherwise remain anecdote; they expose to participants the structure the research team has inferred, so that the inference can be contested; and they provide the formalisation that connects the diagnosis to the components of the framework, several of which — cost incidence, participation, maintenance — are explicitly feedback phenomena.

One restriction is acknowledged at the outset. Causal loop diagrams are exchangeable representations, not validated models: a diagram drawn with participants is a record of a structured conversation, and its epistemic status is that of an organised account of how the stakeholders see the system operating. The methodological chapter treats them accordingly, and the reflection records of the lab (Chapter 4) keep the inference visible rather than letting harden into fact.

### 3.8.3 Systems engineering and the systems life cycle

Systems engineering contributes the designer's discipline. In its standard usage it names the methodology by which a complex system is defined, developed and deployed so that its parts — technical and organisational — work together across the whole life of the system: from the identification of the need, through requirements, design synthesis and verification, into operation, maintenance and eventual retirement. The discipline insists that interfaces be specified, that requirements be traceable to needs, and that the system be judged against its behaviour in operation rather than against the elegance of its design.

The study imports this discipline into the lab's intervention, with one deliberate inversion. In a conventional application the artefact is the system and the context is the environment; here the configuration — the arrangement of the technology with its fuel and water supply, its space, its labour and its governance — is the system to be engineered, and the digester is one component within it. The design specification that the co-design phase will produce (Chapter 5) is therefore written at the level of the arrangement, and the life-cycle discipline is applied to it: requirements traced to the diagnostic account, verification against conditions of use, a maintenance concept, and an explicit retirement-and-handover plan. The end-user-centred framing governs whose requirements count as the requirements.

### 3.8.4 Systems approaches in informal settlements

The three traditions meet in the specific conditions of an informal settlement, and the meeting is not frictionless. A settlement is a system in the strong sense — energy, water, waste, tenure and transport are interdependent, and interventions in one propagate to the others — yet it is also a system whose boundaries are politically loaded, whose stocks and flows are partly unrecorded, and whose design authority is dispersed among state, landlord, resident and project. Methodologically this means that the systems traditions must be used in their critical and participatory forms: system dynamics as a structured conversation rather than an expert model; systems engineering with requirements negotiated among the parties rather than derived from a single client; boundary critique as a permanent practice rather than a project-stage formality.

These commitments shape the operational framework in Chapter 4 at identifiable points: the boundary statement and stakeholder mapping (C1), the diagnosis that takes accounts from multiple positions as data (C2), the configuration specification (C4), and the reflexive records that treat the lab's own interventions as part of the system (C9). The next section turns to the synthesis proper: what the Living Lab becomes when its architecture is derived from this systems base together with posthumanist theory.
