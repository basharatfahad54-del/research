# 2. Literature Review

## 2.1 Review approach

This is an integrative review rather than a systematic review. The distinction is deliberate. A systematic review answers a narrow question by exhausting a defined body of evidence. An integrative review synthesises literature from several traditions in order to frame a problem and identify a gap (Torraco, 2016). This study needs the second, because the question sits between fields that do not share a vocabulary. Energy engineering, energy justice, Living Lab research and posthumanist philosophy each describe part of the problem, and none describes the whole of it.

The review follows a documented search strategy, set out in Table 2.1. It does not claim to be exhaustive, and it does not use the language of PRISMA. The earlier draft of this proposal described the review as a "stand-alone qualitative systematic literature review (realist review)" but reported no protocol, search strings, screening counts or synthesis method. That claim was not defensible. It is replaced here by an accurate description of what was done, together with a search log that can be inspected. If the examiners require a systematic review at a later stage, a protocol can be registered and a second review conducted.

## 2.2 Energy poverty and energy security in South African informal settlements

Energy poverty is usually defined as the inability to obtain the energy services needed for a decent life. The definition is deliberately broader than "lack of electricity". It includes the quantity and quality of energy, its affordability, the reliability of supply, the safety of its use and the time required to obtain it. A household that has a connection but cannot afford to use it is energy poor. So is a household that cooks with paraffin because the electricity supply fails in the evening, or one that spends hours collecting wood because the local vendor has run out of gas.

South Africa's energy poverty is historically produced. Apartheid spatial planning located black households far from work and services, and the electricity system was built to serve industry and a minority of households. The post-1994 electrification programme was one of the largest in the world, and it raised the connection rate from around a third of households to around nine in ten (Statistics South Africa, 2023). That achievement is real. But it has not eliminated energy poverty, because connection was only one of several conditions. Tariffs have risen sharply, dwellings are often poorly insulated and inadequately wired, and supply interruptions have become routine. The result is a pattern in which formal access coexists with informal practice: households are connected and still stack fuels, still borrow electricity, still cook on paraffin when the power fails.

The literature on South African informal settlements has documented this pattern in detail. Musango (2022) uses a quantitative storytelling approach in the Groenheuwel settlement to show how gender, income and dwelling characteristics interact in household energy transitions. Musango et al. (2020) argue that the security of energy services, rather than the security of supply, is the appropriate organising concept for poor urban environments, because households experience insecurity through the services they fail to obtain rather than through the national balance of generation and demand. Batinge et al. (2022) formalise this insight in a systems model, showing how gendered innovation and service security are linked through feedback. Anditi et al. (2022) provide a gender analysis framework that makes visible the different roles, burdens and knowledge of women and men in energy innovation. Oosthuizen et al. (2020), in a structured review, identify the factors that have hindered the mainstreaming of gendered energy interventions in poor urban environments.

Three limitations recur. First, households are often treated as homogeneous units. The household is not a single decision-maker with a single preference. It is a set of relationships in which age, gender, tenure and income shape who decides, who cooks, who pays and who bears the risk. A technology that is acceptable to the household head may be unacceptable to the person who has to clean it. Second, the urban informal settlement is sometimes conflated with the rural village. The two share poverty and state neglect, but they differ in density, land markets, tenure, infrastructure and proximity to services. A framework built for rural energy poverty does not travel unchanged to an urban settlement. Third, the literature is stronger on description than on design. It explains why energy poverty persists, but it offers limited guidance on what an engineer should change in a prototype, a tariff or a service model.

Energy security, in this literature, is best understood as a household-level accomplishment. A household is secure when it can obtain the services it needs across normal and disrupted conditions, without unacceptable cost or risk. That definition connects the national conversation about generation capacity to the daily conversation about cooking. It also makes the distribution of security an ethical question, because security for one household can be purchased at the expense of another: a landlord who installs a prepaid meter secures his revenue and transfers the risk of arrears to the tenant.

## 2.3 Just transition and energy justice

The idea of a just transition began in the labour and environmental movements of the 1980s and 1990s, where it named the claim that workers in polluting industries should not bear the cost of environmental protection. It has since expanded. In its contemporary form, a just transition is one that is fair in its outcomes, open in its procedures and respectful of the identities and knowledge of affected communities (Newell and Mulvaney, 2013; Swilling and Annecke, 2012). Winkler (2020) gives the concept a neo-Gramscian reading, arguing that transitions are shaped by the balance of social forces and that a transition that does not shift that balance will reproduce existing inequalities under a greener label.

In South Africa, the just transition has become official policy. The Presidential Climate Commission's framework sets out principles for distribution, procedure and restoration, and the Just Energy Transition Partnership has attached international finance to the coal phase-down (Presidential Climate Commission, 2022). Research on implementation has been less optimistic. Mirzania et al. (2023) identify structural and institutional barriers to powering past coal, including municipal capacity, vested interests and the social dependency of coal-dependent towns. Molepo et al. (2025) interview industry experts and find that the barriers are distributed across policy, finance, skills and governance, and that they interact. The transition is not blocked by a single missing factor that could be supplied by a single intervention.

Energy justice supplies the normative vocabulary for evaluating these processes. The most widely used formulation identifies three tenets: distributive justice, which concerns the distribution of benefits and burdens; procedural justice, which concerns who participates and on what terms; and recognition justice, which concerns whose knowledge, identities and practices are acknowledged (McCauley et al., 2013; Jenkins et al., 2016). Sovacool and Dworkin (2015) add a set of principles, including availability, affordability, due process and intergenerational equity. Heffron and McCauley (2017) add restorative justice, the repair of past harm. Walker and Day (2012) make a related argument in the context of fuel poverty: distribution, recognition and procedure are not separate dimensions to be traded off, because a failure of recognition is itself a form of distributive injustice.

The literature has two weaknesses for present purposes. The first is scale. Energy justice is usually applied to policy, siting conflicts and large infrastructure. It has relatively little to say about the design of a household or community-scale technology. Sovacool and Dworkin's principles do not tell an engineer which material to specify, how to organise maintenance, or how to decide who sits on a management committee. The second is operationalisation. Recognition is often reduced to "stakeholder engagement", and procedure to "consultation". Both reductions lose the critical content of the concepts. A consultation can be procedurally correct and still be a mechanism for legitimising a decision that was taken elsewhere.

What the study takes from this literature is a set of evaluative questions, not a ready-made design method. The questions are: who receives what, through which procedure, with whose knowledge recognised, and with what repair for past harm? The framework in Chapter 4 turns these questions into indicators. The theoretical framework in Chapter 3 explains why they cannot be answered by a purely technical assessment.

## 2.4 Living Labs and systems thinking

A Living Lab is a real-world arrangement for innovation in which users, researchers, firms and public bodies collaborate over time. Definitions vary, but most agree on four elements: a real-life setting, multiple stakeholders, co-creation with users, and iterative experimentation (Bergvall-Kåreborn and Ståhlbröst, 2009; Leminen et al., 2012). Living Labs have been classified by who drives them (provider, utilizer, enabler or user), by their sector, and by the degree to which they are physically or virtually constituted. Hossain et al. (2019), in a systematic review, find that the literature is fragmented and that many studies describe individual labs without comparing them. Dell'Era and Landoni (2014) position the Living Lab between user-centred design and participatory design, which is useful because it makes clear that a Living Lab is not simply a consultation method. It is an organisational form.

The energy Living Lab literature is smaller. Mbatha and Musango (2022) review the application of the concept in the energy sector and find that it is used inconsistently: sometimes as a space, sometimes as a method, sometimes as a network. They propose a six-phase process for setting up an energy Living Lab in an urban informal settlement, from scoping and stakeholder identification to evaluation and scaling. Mukama et al. (2022) describe the development of Living Labs for gendered energy technology innovation in poor urban environments, and Petrulaityte et al. (2022) present the GENS co-design toolkit that supports multi-stakeholder collaboration. Von Wirth et al. (2019), writing on urban Living Labs in Europe, show that labs can influence sustainability transitions through mechanisms such as embedding, learning and network formation, but that their impact depends on how the lab is connected to the wider regime.

Systems thinking supplies a different set of resources. Checkland (1981) argues that systems are not objects in the world but ways of organising inquiry; the boundary of a system is a choice, and the choice has political consequences. Meadows (2008) shows how feedback, stocks and flows produce behaviour that cannot be inferred from the parts. Midgley (2000) develops a critical systems approach in which boundary critique is central: the analyst asks who is included and excluded by a given framing, and what would change if the boundary were drawn differently. Sterman (2000) provides the modelling vocabulary of causal loops and delays. Cilliers (1998) cautions that complex systems cannot be fully represented, so any model is a reduction that must be used with care.

The combination of Living Lab and systems thinking is often asserted rather than derived. A "systemic Living Lab" is usually a lab that uses systems tools, not a lab whose boundaries, stakeholder set, feedback architecture and evaluation criteria are derived from systems theory. That distinction is the subject of Section 3.9. It matters because a lab that is systemic only in its vocabulary will reproduce the conventional boundary of the project: funders, implementers and selected community representatives, with the wider settlement treated as context.

The empirical evidence on energy Living Labs in African informal settlements is thin. The South African work is recent and mostly descriptive. There are few longitudinal studies, few comparative studies and few evaluations that ask whether the lab produced outcomes that would not have occurred without it. This is not a criticism of the researchers, who are working in difficult conditions. It is a statement about the state of the evidence, and it shapes the claims that this study can make. The study can contribute a carefully documented case. It cannot claim to establish general effectiveness.

## 2.5 The Living Lab in depth: definitions, typologies, case experience and scaling

Section 2.4 introduced the Living Lab and its combination with systems thinking at the level needed to state the gap. This section treats the concept more closely, because the study's central construct — the systemic Living Lab — inherits both the strengths and the pathologies of this literature.

### 2.5.1 Definitions across fields

The definition of a Living Lab varies with the field that uses it, and the variation is not cosmetic. Reviews of the literature find the concept applied in information and communication technology, product development, health, public administration, urban planning and, more recently, energy, with definitions differing in what they treat as primary: the space, the method, the network of actors, or the innovation outcome (Hossain et al., 2019; Mbatha and Musango, 2022). What the definitions share is a small set of invariants: innovation is developed with end-users in a real-life setting rather than tested on them afterwards; multiple kinds of organisation participate; and the activity is iterative, with cycles of design, use and revision rather than a single passage from prototype to release (Bergvall-Kåreborn and Ståhlbröst, 2009; Leminen et al., 2012).

Two definitional positions matter for the present argument. The first is the methodological definition offered by Dell'Era and Landoni, for whom the Living Lab is "a design research methodology aimed at co-creating innovation through the involvement of aware users in a real-life setting" (Dell'Era and Landoni, 2014). Its importance is the word aware: users are not passive sources of requirements but participants who know what they are doing, which is the position this study takes. The second is the characterisation of Living Labs as physical or virtual spaces in which stakeholders collaborate on societal challenges (Hossain et al., 2019). Its importance is the admission of virtual and mobile constitution, which matters for a lab that must operate where land, premises and services are informal.

For this study, a working definition is synthesised from the reviewed corpus rather than adopted from any single source: a Living Lab is an organised, iterative arrangement in which end-users and other stakeholders co-create and test innovations together in a real-life setting, with the explicit intention that what is learned changes both the innovation and the arrangement. The end-user-centred emphasis is deliberate: it names whose participation is constitutive of the method.

### 2.5.2 Typologies and classifications

The literature classifies Living Labs along several dimensions, and the classifications are consequential because they harden into defaults. The best-known classification is by the actor that drives the lab: a lab may be driven by the provider of infrastructure, the utiliser of results, the enabler who funds and convenes, or the users themselves (Leminen et al., 2012). The classification matters because the driver shapes what the lab optimises for: a provider-driven lab optimises for the deployment of the provider's technology, a utiliser-driven lab for the production of knowledge its sponsor can use, and a user-driven lab for the usefulness of the outcome to the participants. A second dimension is constitution: labs may be physically situated, wholly virtual, or mobile enough to follow the innovation (Hossain et al., 2019). A third is sector and purpose, in which the energy applications form a small and recent subset of a literature dominated by ICT and urban innovation (Mbatha and Musango, 2022).

### 2.5.3 Case experience across sectors

The reviewed corpus documents Living Lab implementations across many sectors and settings, and the synthesis of that documentation is instructive even where individual cases cannot be generalised. The systematic review by Hossain et al. (2019) finds a literature dominated by descriptions of single labs, with comparatively little comparison, longitudinal follow-up or evaluation against stated outcomes; the labs described are concentrated in European and other high-income contexts, and the energy domain is among the least represented. The review of energy-sector applications by Mbatha and Musango (2022) reaches a consistent conclusion for this domain: applications exist, their practices vary widely, and many were not designed with continuation, maintenance or scaling in view. Work on urban living labs in European sustainability transitions shows what labs can realistically change: through embedding in local institutions, through structured learning among participants, and through the networks the lab creates, a lab can influence its wider regime — but the influence is conditional on those connections having been built, and does not follow from the demonstration alone (von Wirth et al., 2019).

Three lessons for the present study follow. First, a documented case is the realistic assessment unit: the study can contribute a carefully documented, methodologically explicit case, and must not claim to establish that the model works everywhere. Second, the mechanisms by which a lab has effects beyond itself are institutional, not technical, which is why governance, ownership and exit are components of the framework in Chapter 4 rather than afterthoughts. Third, most documented weaknesses are designable: the fragmentation, the missing follow-up and the absent continuation planning are choices the framework can refuse to make.

### 2.5.4 Living Labs in the energy sector

Within the energy applications that do exist, two lines of work anchor the present study. The first is the design and operation of living labs for energy technology innovation in poor urban environments described by Mukama et al. (2022), which demonstrates the practical steps of assembling stakeholders, running co-design activities with household end-users, and developing an energy innovation against real conditions of use; the associated co-design toolkit supports the multi-stakeholder collaboration such labs require (Petrulaityte et al., 2022). The second is the modelling line of work showing how innovation and the security of energy services interact as a system in these environments (Batinge et al., 2022), together with the analysis frameworks that make household roles, burdens and knowledge visible in the design process (Anditi et al., 2022; Musango et al., 2020).

What the energy-sector literature still lacks, taken as a whole, is the combination this study is organised around: an explicit systems architecture in which the lab's boundary, feedback and evaluation are derived from theory; an end-user-centred participation design; justice criteria applied to the lab's own operation; and a continuation plan treated as a design component rather than a closing remark. The energy labs documented to date tend to be strong on engagement practice and weak on the systemic and institutional variables (Mbatha and Musango, 2022; von Wirth et al., 2019). These observations sharpen the gap statement of Section 2.9 rather than replacing it.

### 2.5.5 Real-world testing, evaluation and scaling

The living lab's defining promise is that innovations are tested in use, in the conditions where they must ultimately work. The reviewed literature suggests both sides of the promise. On the productive side, real-world testing surfaces the non-technical requirements — the water, the space, the labour, the tenure rules, the cost structure — that a laboratory setting hides, and it allows revision during use rather than after failure. On the demanding side, real-world testing makes the evaluation problem harder: outcomes are entangled with everything else that changes in a settlement, so a lab that cannot document its own interventions cannot attribute its results.

The scaling question is where the literature is weakest. Scaling is often written as though it followed from demonstrated success. The evidence reviewed here points the other way: labs scale, where they scale at all, through the mechanisms von Wirth et al. (2019) identify — embedding in institutions, learning transferred to the actors who will operate the next installation, and networks that carry the model — and each of these mechanisms has to be built. For the present study, the implication is stated in the framework itself: exit, durability and scaling are planned from the start as a component of the lab (Chapter 4), not as an epilogue to it. The design of the planned evaluation, and the criteria against which the lab will judge itself, are set out in Chapters 4 and 5.

## 2.6 End-user participation and acceptance

Participation has a long and sceptical literature. Arnstein's (1969) ladder distinguishes manipulation and therapy from consultation, partnership and citizen control. The ladder is more than fifty years old, but its central insight remains: participation is not a single activity, and the difference between consultation and control is the difference between legitimising a decision and sharing it. Sanders and Stappers (2008) describe the shift from designing for users to designing with them, and von Hippel (2005) shows that users can be important sources of innovation, particularly when their needs are unusual and their knowledge is tacit.

Energy research has adopted participation as a norm but has been slower to examine its conditions. Wüstenhagen et al. (2007) distinguish social acceptance at three levels: socio-political, community and market. The distinction is useful because it shows that a technology can be acceptable in principle and resisted in a particular place. But the model remains attitudinal. It asks what people think about a technology, not what the technology, the infrastructure and the household practice do together.

The dominant model of acceptance in engineering and information systems is the Technology Acceptance Model. TAM holds that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use predict intention to use, which in turn predicts use (Davis, 1989). UTAUT extends the model with social influence and facilitating conditions (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Both models have been enormously productive. They are also built on assumptions that do not fit the present problem. They assume an individual chooser, a finished artefact, a measurable intention and a context that can be treated as background. They have little to say about the cost of fuel, the reliability of supply, the division of labour in a household, the terms of tenure, or the material properties of a dwelling. A TAM study can tell us that residents find a biogas system useful. It cannot tell us why the system is abandoned when the water supply fails.

Practice theory and the energy cultures framework offer an alternative. Shove (2003) argues that consumption is shaped by conventions of comfort, cleanliness and convenience, which are collective and material rather than individual. Stephenson et al. (2010) propose that energy behaviour is shaped by the interaction of norms, practices and material culture. These approaches are closer to the present study because they treat technology and practice as co-constitutive. But they are stronger on explanation than on design. They describe how practices persist; they say less about how a design team might intervene.

Participation also has failure modes that the literature documents but projects often ignore. Consultation can be tokenistic. Meetings can be dominated by those with time, confidence and status. Expectations can be raised and then disappointed. Benefits can be captured by local elites. Participation can create fatigue, particularly when communities are asked to participate repeatedly in projects that do not endure. These are not arguments against participation. They are arguments for designing participation with the same care as the technology, and for evaluating it rather than assuming its virtue.

## 2.7 Decentralised energy and biogas

Anaerobic digestion is a mature technology. Bacteria break down organic matter in the absence of oxygen, producing a gas that is roughly 50 to 70 per cent methane and a residue that can be used as a soil amendment. The process requires a consistent feedstock, water, a temperature range that supports microbial activity, and a retention time that allows digestion to complete. In practice, the design of a digester is a design of a supply chain and a maintenance arrangement as much as a design of a vessel.

Biogas has been promoted across Africa for decades, with mixed results. Successful installations tend to share several features: a reliable feedstock supply, a user who understands the system, a maintenance arrangement with access to spare parts, and a use for the gas that fits existing cooking practices. Failures tend to share the opposite features. Feedstock runs out, water is too far away, the digester is too small for the household, the gas pressure is too low, the stove does not fit the pot, or the person who was trained leaves. The technical literature often treats these as "non-technical barriers". A posthumanist reading would treat them as part of the technology: the digester is not the vessel alone, but the vessel plus the feedstock, the water, the labour, the stove, the spare parts and the agreement about who maintains it.

In South African informal settlements, the conditions for biogas are both promising and difficult. Organic waste is plentiful in some areas, particularly where there is a market or food trade. Water is often shared and sometimes costly. Space is limited, and tenure is insecure. Cooking practices vary, and gas may not be acceptable for all dishes. Municipal waste services are uneven. These conditions do not make biogas impossible. They make the configuration the central design problem, which is precisely why biogas is a useful case for this study.

### 2.7.1 Biogas technology: process and system types

Biogas is produced by anaerobic digestion, in which micro-organisms decompose organic matter in the absence of oxygen. The feedstocks are the organic streams that households and settlements already produce — kitchen waste, animal manure, sewage and crop residues — and the outputs are a methane-rich gas and a stabilised digestate. The gas can be used directly for cooking and heating and, where the system includes appropriate generation equipment, for electricity; the digestate is valued as a soil amendment. For a household or a small community, the technology is therefore not only an energy source but a waste-management intervention, which is why it is of particular interest in settlements where both waste services and clean cooking fuels are unreliable.

The technical literature distinguishes system classes by scale and duty: small household digesters of fixed-dome or floating-drum construction, serving a single dwelling or a small cluster; community-scale installations serving shared cooking or institutional loads; and larger industrial installations operated for municipal or commercial feedstocks. The household and community classes are the relevant ones for this study, because the service the study is organised around is cooking at household scale, with the possibility of an institutional annex — for example a school kitchen — where the settlement's feedstock and demand support it.

The choice of biogas as the technology focus of this study (Chapter 1) follows from that service logic rather than from enthusiasm for a single device. A biogas system couples the cooking service to waste handling and, potentially, to agricultural use of the digestate; it can therefore be designed, governed and evaluated as an arrangement rather than as an appliance, which is exactly what the conceptual framework in Chapter 4 requires. Its known implementation difficulties — the capital and maintenance demands of the installation, the need for reliable operation and local skills, and the social conditions of adoption — are precisely the configuration variables the framework is built to manage, and are treated as design inputs rather than background risks.

### 2.7.2 Biogas in African settlements: implementation experience and lessons

The African implementation record for biogas is mixed, and the present study treats that record as evidence about configurations rather than as a verdict on the technology. Where community-scale biogas services have worked in low-income contexts, the documented conditions of success recur: the people who depend on the system participate in its design and adapt it to local conditions; the biogas service is integrated with other services the settlement needs, particularly waste handling; and there is a capacity-building and funding arrangement that allows operation and maintenance to continue after the initial project ends. Where installations have failed or fallen into disuse, the recurrent causes are the mirror image: capital and skills were provided without a maintenance route; the design was imported without adaptation; and the arrangement depended on a project team that later withdrew.

Two cautions discipline the use of this experience in the present study. First, much of the documented African experience is reported without the longitudinal and comparative evidence that would support general claims, which is one more reason the study's claims are bounded to a documented case (Section 2.4). Second, the case inventory available in the study's earlier concept documents — including named implementations elsewhere in South Africa and East Africa — could not be verified at source during the preparation of this proposal, and is therefore not cited here; a targeted verification of that literature is recorded as an outstanding task in the study's quality record. The design implications drawn above rest on the general implementation pattern, not on any single named case.

## 2.8 Posthumanism in energy and engineering

Posthumanism has been applied extensively in education, geography, feminist technoscience and design, but rarely in energy engineering. Forlano (2017) argues that design should take non-human agency seriously, and that this changes what counts as a design problem. Ulmer (2017) sets out posthumanism as a research methodology, with implications for how data are produced and interpreted. Aboalez (2025), writing in engineering education, makes the case for a posthumanist social approach to energy transition. That paper is the closest precedent for the present study and is discussed in Chapter 3.4.

The gap is not that posthumanism has never been mentioned in energy research. It is that there is no developed account of how distributed agency shapes the design, acceptance and use of energy technologies in informal settlements. The literature on energy justice tells us what justice requires. The literature on Living Labs tells us how to organise collaboration. The literature on posthumanism tells us how to think about human–technology relations. What is missing is a framework that connects the three at the scale of a settlement project.

That positioning has a cost: the study cannot claim to advance energy economics, posthumanist philosophy or Living Lab methodology as such. It can claim to show how a small number of concepts from those fields can be assembled into a framework that is useful for a particular class of problem, and to test that framework against a case. Whether the claim holds is an empirical question, and the design in Chapter 5 is intended to answer it.

Three claims that appeared in the earlier draft are withdrawn or reformulated. The draft stated that no study had ever combined Living Lab and systems approaches to address energy poverty in South Africa. That claim is too strong to defend and is replaced by the narrower observation that the reviewed literature contains few such accounts and none that combines them with a posthumanist ontology. The draft also implied that participation produces acceptance and that acceptance produces justice. Both implications are treated here as hypotheses rather than premises. Finally, the draft described the review as systematic without providing a protocol. That description has been corrected.
