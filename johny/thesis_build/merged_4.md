# 4. Conceptual Framework

## 4.1 From theory to model

Chapter 3 ended with six propositions about acceptance, agency, boundary, apparatus, justice and participation. Those propositions are abstract. They say that acceptance is emergent, that agency is distributed, that boundaries have justice consequences, that the lab produces what it studies, that acceptance and justice are not the same thing, and that participation has material conditions. None of them tells a researcher what to do on the first day of fieldwork.

This section converts the propositions into a working model. The model is called SETILL: a Systemic Living Lab for Just Energy Transition. It has ten components, each of which names something the lab has to organise, decide or measure. The components are not stages. They run in parallel and they feed one another. The numbering is a convenience for reference, not a sequence.

The distinction between Chapter 3 and this section is worth restating, because it is the distinction between a theoretical framework and a conceptual framework. Chapter 3 justifies the ontology. This section builds the model that the ontology implies, with enough specificity that another researcher could apply it and enough openness that the case can surprise it. A model that cannot be surprised is not a research instrument; it is a description of the author's expectations.

## 4.2 What the framework is and is not

The framework is a structured account of the elements that have to be aligned for a community-scale energy innovation to be taken up, sustained and judged fair in an informal settlement. It is intended to be used in three ways: as a design heuristic for the project team, as an analytical frame for the data, and as an evaluation instrument.

It is not a theory of everything. It does not explain national energy politics, it does not model the electricity market, and it does not predict adoption rates. It is deliberately local. Its claim is that the local configuration is where the fate of a decentralised technology is decided, and that the configuration can be described systematically without pretending to capture the whole system.

It is also not a checklist to be completed mechanically. Each component raises a question that has to be answered in the specific case. The answers will differ between a settlement with a market and one without, between a site with yard space and one without, between a community with an organised women's group and one where the only organised structure is the ward committee. The framework's contribution is the set of questions, not a set of answers.

Three design commitments follow from Chapter 3 and shape every component. The first is that the unit of analysis is the configuration, not the artefact and not the individual. The second is that justice is assessed throughout, not added at the end. The third is that the lab is part of what it studies, so its own operation has to be documented.

### 4.4.1 C1 Boundary critique and stakeholder mapping

Every project draws a boundary. The boundary decides who is consulted, whose costs are counted and which infrastructure is treated as fixed. In most projects the boundary is drawn by convention: the funder defines the project area, the implementing organisation lists its partners, and the settlement appears as a beneficiary group. The boundary is rarely stated, and because it is not stated it cannot be questioned.

This component makes the boundary an explicit decision. The lab produces a boundary statement that names who is included, who is excluded and why. It includes a note on the actors who could affect the outcome but are not in the room: the landlord who controls the meter, the ward councillor who controls access to the site, the municipal department that owns the waste collection route, the supplier who stocks the spare part. The statement is revisited at each cycle, because the boundary that made sense at the start may not make sense once the fieldwork begins.

The component is derived from Checkland's (1981) argument that the system is a way of organising inquiry rather than an object in the world, and from Midgley's (2000) boundary critique, which asks whose interests are served by a given framing. It is also informed by Arnstein (1969), because the question of who is inside the boundary is inseparable from the question of who has power within it.

The practical test is simple. Can the research team produce a dated statement of the boundary, with reasons, and show that it changed at least once in response to something learned in the field? If not, the boundary was probably inherited rather than chosen.

### 4.4.2 C2 Situated diagnosis

A diagnosis is an account of what the problem is. In energy projects the diagnosis is usually technical: the settlement lacks a reliable cooking fuel, the waste is not collected, the grid connection is inadequate. These statements may be true, but they are made from a position. A woman who cooks for eight people, a tenant who pays a fixed rent that includes electricity, a landlord who collects the rent, an artisan who repairs stoves and a municipal official who manages a landfill will describe the same settlement differently.

This component treats those differences as data. The lab collects accounts from multiple positions and uses them together, rather than selecting one account as the accurate description and treating the rest as perceptions. The methods are those that can capture practice and embodiment as well as opinion: transect walks, household energy diaries, photovoice, observation in kitchens and key-informant interviews.

The component draws on Haraway's (1988) argument that knowledge is always situated and that the view from nowhere conceals a position. It also draws on the South African literature on gendered energy research, which has shown that household energy practices are organised by gender and that a diagnosis which ignores this will mis-specify the problem (Musango et al., 2020; Anditi et al., 2022; Musango, 2022).

The test is whether the diagnosis contains accounts that differ by position, and whether those differences changed something in the design. A diagnosis that produces a single coherent story is suspicious. Settlements are not coherent.

### 4.4.3 C3 Participation architecture

Participation is often organised as an event: a meeting, a workshop, a feedback session. The event is announced, people are invited, some attend, and the attendance is recorded as evidence of participation. This approach measures presence, not influence, and it systematically excludes those whose time is not their own.

This component treats participation as a design problem. It asks what would have to be true for a tenant who works during the day, a woman with small children, a person with a disability or an elderly resident to take part on equal terms. The answers are practical: meetings at times that fit the working day, venues within walking distance, childcare, translation into the languages people actually use, separate sessions where mixed groups would silence some voices, and payment for time where the project can afford it.

The component is derived from Arnstein's (1969) ladder, which distinguishes consultation from partnership and citizen control, and from the co-design literature, which insists that users are participants in the generation of ideas rather than sources of requirements (Sanders and Stappers, 2008). It also reflects the Living Lab literature's attention to the organisational form of collaboration (Leminen et al., 2012).

The test is whether attendance is broad and stable across the cycles, and whether the people who attend are able to change decisions. A participation architecture that produces good attendance and no change has failed.

### 4.4.4 C4 Configuration design

The fourth component is the one that most directly distinguishes SETILL from a conventional Living Lab. In a conventional lab the artefact is the object of design and the context is the setting in which it is tested. Here the configuration is the object of design. The artefact is one element, and the design process has to specify the others: the fuel supply, the water source, the space, the maintenance arrangement, the spare-parts route, the skills, the tariff and the rules that govern use.

For a biogas system this means that the design specification includes the feedstock supply chain, the water budget, the siting decision, the gas storage, the stove type, the digestate disposal route, the maintenance schedule and the person responsible for each task. A specification that covers only the digester volume and the gas yield is incomplete, however technically sound it is.

The component rests on Barad's (2007) argument that properties emerge through intra-action, and on Bennett's (2010) account of thing-power, which insists that materials resist and surprise. It is also informed by practice theory, which shows that technologies are enrolled in routines that already exist (Shove, 2003; Stephenson et al., 2010).

The test is whether the specification names the non-technical requirements explicitly, and whether the prototype that is built is a prototype of the arrangement or only of the vessel.

### 4.4.5 C5 Governance and ownership

A community-scale system needs a decision-making structure. Who sits on the management committee? How are members chosen, and can they be removed? Who owns the asset if the funder withdraws? Who authorises a repair, and who pays for it? Who resolves a dispute between two households over access?

These questions are often left to the end of a project, when they are answered by default: the asset is registered to a community organisation that may or may not be functional, the committee is composed of whoever was present at the final meeting, and the dispute route is a phone number that stops working when the project officer leaves.

This component makes those decisions explicit and early. It produces a written agreement, in the local language, that names the owner, the managing body, the decision rules, the tariff-setting principle and the dispute route. The agreement is not a legal contract in most cases, but it is a document that residents can describe and hold each other to.

The component draws on the commons literature on institutional design, particularly the principles that boundaries, rules and conflict-resolution mechanisms should be defined by the users themselves. It is also informed by von Wirth et al. (2019), who show that the durability of an urban Living Lab depends on its embedding in local institutions.

The test is whether residents can describe, in their own words, who owns the system and what happens when it breaks. If the answer is "the project", the component has not been completed.

### 4.4.6 C6 Cost incidence and financing

Every intervention distributes cost. Some of the cost is financial: capital, operating expenses, fuel, spare parts. Some is in labour: the time spent collecting feedstock, carrying water, cleaning the digester, attending meetings. Some is in risk: the possibility that the system fails and the household has to fall back on a more expensive option.

This component traces the distribution. It asks who pays, in what form, at which point in the service chain, and with what consequence for the household budget. It disaggregates by tenure, income and gender, because the same nominal cost can be trivial for an owner and decisive for a tenant.

The component draws on the energy justice literature's attention to affordability and to the distribution of burdens (Sovacool and Dworkin, 2015; Walker and Day, 2012), and on the argument that unpaid labour is a cost even when no money changes hands.

The test is a cost profile by household type that includes unpaid labour. A project that reports a low cost per household without disaggregating has not answered the question.

### 4.4.7 C7 Capability and local enterprise

A Living Lab that leaves behind a working system but no capacity to maintain it has not succeeded. This component treats the development of local capability as an outcome in its own right: skills in construction, operation, maintenance and repair; a local supplier of spare parts; a small enterprise that can service other installations; and a set of people who can train others.

The component is informed by Braidotti's (2013) distinction between potestas and potentia, which frames capacity-building as an increase in the community's power to act rather than as compensation for a deficit. It is also compatible with the capability approach, which evaluates arrangements by what people are able to do and be (Nussbaum, 2011).

The test is whether the skills persist after the project team leaves. A training workshop that produces a certificate but no practice has not built capability.

### 4.4.8 C8 Justice evaluation

This component is the operational heart of the framework's normative claim. It takes the four dimensions of energy justice and turns each into a set of indicators with a data source and a point of comparison. The indicators are set out in Table 4.4 and discussed in Section 4.6.

The component is not a scoring exercise. It produces a profile that reports where the arrangement performs well and where it does not. The profile is shared with participants before it is finalised, and disagreements about it are recorded.

### 4.4.9 C9 Reflexive monitoring

A Living Lab is an apparatus in Barad's (2007) sense: it does not observe a phenomenon from outside but participates in producing it. The questions it asks, the people it includes and the data it records all enact a cut. This component makes the cut visible.

In practice the lab keeps three records. The first is a decision log: what was decided, by whom, on what basis, and what alternatives were rejected. The second is an intervention register: every action the project took that could have affected the outcome, from the installation of the digester to the choice of a meeting venue. The third is a reflection record: the research team's notes on its own assumptions, surprises and discomforts.

These records serve two purposes. They allow the analysis to distinguish project effects from other changes in the settlement, and they allow the reader to assess the lab's own role in what it found. Without them, a finding about residents' preferences may be a finding about the questions the project asked.

### 4.4.10 C10 Exit, durability and scaling

The final component plans for the end from the beginning. It asks what will still be working two years after the project closes, who will be maintaining it, how it will be financed, and what will happen to the relationships that were built.

An exit plan is not an abandonment plan. It specifies the handover: the asset, the records, the spare parts, the training materials and the contacts. It specifies the finance: a maintenance fund, a tariff, a municipal budget line or a combination. And it specifies the review: a date at which the arrangement will be assessed by the people who depend on it.

The component draws on von Wirth et al. (2019), who show that the impact of an urban Living Lab depends on how it is embedded in wider structures, and on Mbatha and Musango's (2022) observation that many energy Living Labs in Africa are not designed for continuation.

The test is a named owner, a budget line and a maintenance route that do not depend on the research team.

## 4.5 The relationships between the components

The components are not independent. Four relationships matter for the analysis.

The first is a dependency: C4 depends on C2. A configuration cannot be designed without a diagnosis of what the configuration currently is. This seems obvious, but projects routinely design a technology before diagnosing the arrangement, and then discover during implementation that the water, space or maintenance conditions were never assessed.

The second is a tension: C3 and C10 pull against each other. Broad participation takes time, and the later cycles of a project are under pressure to close. The framework does not resolve the tension, but it requires the lab to record how it was managed.

The third is a feedback: C8 feeds C4. The justice evaluation is not only a final assessment; it produces findings that should change the design. If the distributional analysis shows that tenants bear a disproportionate cost, the design of the tariff or the siting has to respond.

The fourth is a precondition: C1 and C5 make the other components possible. A lab without a boundary statement does not know what it is assessing, and a lab without a governance agreement cannot hand anything over.

Two features of Table 4.4 deserve comment.

First, the indicators are mostly about relations, not about perceptions. "Decisions changed as a result of resident input" is a relational indicator: it requires a record of a decision and a record of the input. "Satisfaction with participation" is a perception indicator, and it can be high in a process that changed nothing. The framework prefers the former, although it collects the latter as well.

Second, the indicators can produce a mixed profile, and that is the point. A project might show good distributional outcomes, weak procedural influence and strong recognition. The report would say so. A framework that produces a single number invites the reader to ignore the dimensions that the number conceals.

The framework also applies the justice criteria to the lab itself. The lab consumes time, attention and space. It makes decisions about who is included. It distributes the benefits of participation unevenly, because those who attend more often get more of the project's attention. C9 exists to make this visible, and the justice profile in Chapter 8 includes a short assessment of the lab's own performance.

## 4.7 How the framework will be used in the analysis

The framework does three jobs in the analysis, and it is worth being explicit about them because the risk of using a framework loosely is that it becomes a set of headings under which anything can be filed.

The first job is deductive. The ten components provide an initial coding frame for the interview and observational data. A passage about the timing of meetings is coded to C3. A passage about who owns the digester is coded to C5. This gives the analysis a starting structure and makes it comparable across data sources.

The second job is abductive. The framework is expected to be incomplete. Where the data do not fit a component, or where a component turns out to be unimportant in this case, that is a finding. The analysis records the misfits and uses them to revise the model. This is the moment at which the study can contribute to theory rather than merely apply it. Alvesson and Kärreman (2007) describe this as the construction of mystery: the empirical material becomes interesting when it resists the framework used to generate it.

The third job is evaluative. The justice criteria in Table 4.4 are applied at baseline and at follow-up, and the resulting profile is one of the study's main outputs. The profile is not a verdict on the community or on the project team. It is a description of how a particular arrangement distributed benefits, voice, recognition and repair at a particular time.

There is a risk in the third job that should be named. An evaluation conducted by the project team, about the project team's work, is vulnerable to self-serving interpretation. The study manages this in three ways: the criteria are specified in advance, the data include records that the team did not generate (diaries, observation by a research assistant not involved in delivery), and the draft profile is discussed with participants before it is finalised.

## 4.8 SETILL in practice: definition, structure, stakeholders and operations

The preceding sections specified the framework as a set of components and relationships. This section turns the framework into an operating proposition: what the lab is called, how it is structured, which stakeholders it convenes, what each contributes, and how the whole is set in motion. The content is design, not description: the lab does not yet exist, and everything in this section is written as an intention to be tested by the study's execution.

### 4.8.1 A working definition of SETILL

SETILL — the Systemic Living Lab for a Just Energy Transition — is defined for this study as a physical, virtual or mobile research and design space in which energy innovations are co-created and tested through the involvement of end-users in a real-life setting, organised by systems thinking, and directed at the security of energy services in poor households. The definition is built deliberately on the definitional invariants established in the literature review (Section 2.5.1), with four commitments added that make it systemic in the sense argued in Chapter 3. The lab undertakes, through its operation: to understand the material cultures, cognitive norms and energy practices that shape end-users' adoption of energy innovations; to bring end-users' knowledge and participation into the design of energy technology innovation; to develop and test an energy innovation to pilot scale together with those end-users; and to strengthen the security of energy services for poor households while making the distribution of costs and benefits visible and contestable.

Within the end-user-centred approach, gender is retained as a necessary axis of analysis rather than discarded with the earlier gender-focused framing. The framework takes all household end-users as participants in the design, while making the different roles, burdens and knowledge of household members visible wherever the evidence requires it, using the analysis tools established in the research lineage the study extends (Musango et al., 2020; Anditi et al., 2022). The shift is one of primary framing: from a lab defined by who is underrepresented to a lab defined by who uses the technology — which, in practice, includes the same households.

### 4.8.3 Stakeholders and their roles

Stakeholder collaboration is constitutive of the Living Lab method rather than incidental to it: labs are arrangements for collaboration across organisational boundaries, and their classifications are literally classifications of who drives the collaboration (Leminen et al., 2012). The energy-sector literature identifies stakeholder configuration and role clarity among the factors that decide whether an energy lab functions (Mbatha and Musango, 2022), and the participation literature supplies the warning that collaboration without attention to power becomes consultation in disguise (Arnstein, 1969). SETILL therefore specifies its stakeholder set and the role of each part of it, and the specification is treated as revisable: the boundary critique (C1) revisits the stakeholder map at each cycle, and new stakeholders may join or leave as the work develops.

Five categories are proposed, and each is described in the role the study intends for it.

The community and end-users are the primary participants. The households of the settlement are the addressees of the innovation and the holders of most of the knowledge about conditions of use; under the end-user-centred approach they participate as co-designers and co-researchers rather than as respondents to someone else's design (Sanders and Stappers, 2008). Their participation is made materially possible by the participation architecture (C3) — timing, venue, language, transport and childcare — because an invitation that the rhythms of the settlement cannot accommodate is not participation. Within this category the study keeps the gender-differentiated reading of roles, burdens and knowledge that the research lineage established (Musango et al., 2020; Anditi et al., 2022), while addressing all household end-users as participants. Community organisations and local leaders participate as stakeholders in their own right, with interests that may differ from those of individual households; the framework treats those differences as data (C2) rather than assuming a single community voice.

Government and policy-makers participate at municipal and, where relevant, provincial and national level. Their roles in the intended collaboration are the regulatory and enabling ones: the legality of installations, the alignment of the intervention with municipal planning, the potential extension of a working arrangement to other settlements, and the financing routes that a durable service requires. The framework treats government as a stakeholder with its own interests — among them service delivery obligations, budget discipline and reputational risk — rather than as a neutral arbiter, and records its positions in the same evidence register as all other contributions.

Technology developers and energy industry partners supply the technical capability that the community cannot be expected to hold: design engineering, components, construction supervision and the training of local operators. Their role is defined by the configuration principle of the framework (C4): the industry partner engineers the arrangement, not only the appliance, and the specification it works to is produced and agreed in the co-design process with end-users rather than handed down. Energy service providers are included in the same category where the service model involves them — the supply relationship, the tariff and the maintenance route are part of the design.

The researchers organise the process. Their role is facilitation, methodological discipline and knowledge integration: maintaining the evidence register, running the co-design and analysis cycles, holding the lab to its own evaluation criteria, and making the lab's own interventions visible and disputable (C9). The researchers hold no design authority over the community's decisions; their authority is procedural, and the boundary critique (C1) keeps even that under review. Where the researchers bring theory to the table — systems thinking, the justice criteria, the framework itself — they are accountable for its fit with the accounts of the settlement.

Funders and institutions provide the resources without which the design cannot be built: the host institution at the University of Johannesburg, external funders, and any industry partners contributing in kind. Their interests — financial and reputational — are stated at the outset and managed through the governance component (C5), so that the arrangement's ownership and its continuation are not quietly settled by whoever paid for it.

### 4.8.4 A rich picture of the problem situation

As a first structuring device, the study will construct a rich picture of the problem situation with the stakeholders named above. The rich picture is the soft systems convention for representing a situation as its actors, their interconnections, their concerns and the systems that shape them, drawn as a single annotated depiction rather than a diagram of causation (Checkland, 1981). Its purpose here is alignment: the picture will be drawn collaboratively during the scoping phase, so that the stakeholders' differing understandings of the situation — what the problem is, who is involved, what has been tried — are visible before the design activities begin, and so that disputes over the depiction can be productive rather than buried. The picture will be revised as the cycles proceed; like the boundary statement, it is a dated artefact whose revisions are part of the record.

### 4.8.5 Reading the framework as antecedents, attributes and consequences

For readers working from the intervention-evaluation tradition, the ten components of Section 4.4 can be read in a second organisation that complements the cluster logic already given (establish the frame, organise the intervention, assess and sustain). The reading groups the components as antecedents, attributes and consequences of the intervention, and it is offered as an interpretive aid rather than a change to the framework: every statement below restates what the components already require.

The antecedents are the conditions that must be in place for the intervention to be designed coherently: the boundary statement and stakeholder map (C1), which establish what is inside the system and whose accounts are needed; the situated diagnosis (C2), which establishes what the configuration currently is, from the positions of those who live it; and the participation architecture (C3), which establishes that the people who must be part of the design are able to take part on terms that suit their lives. These three are the state of the situation at the moment design begins, and the framework refuses to proceed without them — the dependency stated in Section 4.5, that configuration design depends on the diagnosis, is this reading's central claim.

The attributes are the design properties of the arrangement that the lab creates: the configuration specification covering the fuel, water, space, labour and rules around the technology (C4); the governance and ownership arrangement (C5); the traced distribution of costs (C6); and the capability left in local hands (C7). These are the properties the co-design phase negotiates and the specification documents, and they are the dimensions along which the intervention is deliberately different from a technology handover.

The consequences are what the lab will produce and must answer for: the justice profile across the four dimensions (C8); the reflexive account of what the lab itself changed (C9); and the durability and continuity of the arrangement after the research team withdraws (C10). The feedback relations of Section 4.5 survive this reading — the justice evaluation feeds back into design (C8 to C4), and the reflexive records guard against the lab mistaking its own effects for the settlement's — which is why the reading is an aid to exposition rather than a second framework.

### 4.8.6 Bringing the model into motion

The final question is how the structure becomes an operating lab. The study's implementation phases (Chapter 5 and the research plan) sequence the work from scoping and ethics through diagnosis, co-design, prototyping, evaluation and exit. In the conceptual vocabulary of the model, the cycles run from an opening conception of the lab and its purpose, through the design of the arrangement, its development and application in the settlement, its implementation as a working service, and a review that both evaluates the arrangement and revises the lab itself — a cycle that then repeats with the revised understanding. The phasing is the study's own operationalisation of the Living Lab's iterative character; the named activities at each phase are those of the Design Research Methodology adopted in Chapter 5, and the recovery of the cycle from a stated problem situation to a supported design outcome is what the rich picture, the boundary statement and the diagnostic account are for in practice.

The lab is designed to be mobile and, where premises cannot be secured, to operate virtually: the activities of the lab are workshops, walks, diaries, build sessions and reviews attached to people and places, not to a building. What makes it a lab is the organised cycle of co-creation, testing in real conditions, evaluation against the justice criteria and revision — not an address. This is stated here because it is one of the definitional invariants on which the model depends (Section 2.5.2), and because the pilot-scale demonstration the study commits to is a demonstration of an arrangement in use, not of a device on a bench.

## 4.9 Boundaries of the framework

The framework has four limits that should be stated.

First, it is designed for a community-scale, decentralised technology in a low-income urban settlement. It may not transfer to household-scale devices, to networked infrastructure or to rural settings without modification. The components would remain relevant, but their relative weight would change.

Second, it assumes a research team with a presence in the field over an extended period. A three-month consultancy could use the framework as a checklist, but it could not complete the cycles or observe the durability of the arrangement.

Third, it depends on access. A lab that cannot reach tenants, or that is confined to the households the municipality is willing to identify, will produce a partial picture. The boundary statement should record this.

Fourth, it is a framework for design and evaluation, not for causal inference. It can show that an arrangement changed and describe how, but it cannot isolate the contribution of the lab from other changes in the settlement. The design in Section 7 addresses this through the case study logic and through the reflexive record, not through statistical control.

## 4.10 Chapter summary

This section has converted the theoretical propositions of Chapter 3 into a ten-component model. The components cover the frame (boundary, diagnosis, participation), the intervention (configuration, governance, cost, capability) and the assessment (justice, reflexivity, exit). Each component has a core question, a set of activities and a test of whether it is working. The justice dimension is operationalised in twelve indicators with data sources.

The framework is presented as an instrument that can be used, criticised and revised. Its value will be judged by whether it produces findings that a conventional technology-transfer approach would have missed, and by whether those findings are useful to the people who live in the settlement. That judgement cannot be made in a proposal. It can only be made after the fieldwork, and the design in Section 7 is intended to make it possible.
