1.2 The overview of the historical development of the research study 
Energy poverty conditions are mostly prevalent in poor rural and urban African informal 
settlement households, most informal settlement households make use of localised energy 
sources but still lack access to clean energy sources and modern energy services (Okoye, 2020). 
Therefore, ‘energy insecurity’ which can also be called ‘energy poverty’ in the context of this 
research study, is challenging and the issues have been generally supported in literature and 
policies in the public domain (Balmer, 2007; McCollum et al, 2017; Sadath and Acharya, 
2017). The highest rate of energy insecurity is in Africa with more than 80% of fuel 
consumption being paraffin (high carbon emitting fuel), biomass, and municipal industrial and 
animal wastes (UNEP, 2017). In developing countries, billions of individuals are unable to 
access clean cooking sources and electricity (Sokona, Mulugetta and Gujba, 2012), and the 
United Nations (2014) indicated that by 2030, over 2.5 million individuals could be unable to 
access clean modern sources of energy for cooking. Therefore, this research study looked in 
the direction of developing a modern energy source for cooking with women as the end-users 
with the Living Lab through a bottom-up approach which will be validated and tested.

2.2.2 Historical context and current status of gender inequality in energy access 
Prominent gender inequality in energy access has historical political and social causes that can 
be traced to South Africa and many other African countries. The culture of male dominance 
that has been evident in many African societies tends to influence gender equality or the lack 
of it in many sectors like energy (Johnson, Gerber and Muhoza, 2019). Traditionally, women 
in African societies have been confined to the home, socially and economically powerless, and 
have had little scope or opportunity for education, business, and other ventures. This division 
of labour based on gender has greatly influenced energy access since women are responsible 
for supplies of energy for home use, which include cooking, water heating, and space heating, 
services that mainly depend on traditional sources of energy such as firewood and charcoal 
(Jagoe et al., 2020). 
In South Africa, the apartheid policy system aggravates gender and racial disparities and 
provides a background to the country’s current socio-economic system that gives differentiated 


=== PAGE BREAK ===

Page | 28 
 
access to the mineral-energy complex. There have been challenges in the South African energy 
landscape since the definite post-apartheid progressive upturns; today, millions of people in 
the country, including those who are rurally and informally housed, remain inadequately 
electrified (Mario, Brincat and Moe, 2024). Women suffer grave consequences in these 
communities due to their social and economic status and roles based on gender (Heise et al., 
2019). 
The currently available data shows that there has not been much progress in addressing the 
gender gap in energy access. Estimates by the International Energy Agency (IEA) show that in 
sub-Saharan Africa alone, more than 600 million people have no access to electricity, and 
women endure the worst impacts of energy poverty (Ferdous, 2021). Although there are 
improvements in the electrification rates in South Africa, this does not necessarily mean that 
everyone can access modern energy services or have reliable energy for several activities, 
especially for rural women. 
Patriarchal norms shape access, use, and control of energy resources in African societies to a 
large extent. This research study also examines how the energy sector has remained a male-
dominated industry in the past, where very few women were employed, let alone given 
decision-making authority. It is also examining how human factors affect energy security. It is 
also established that men dominate the formal employment sector, whereby they are the major 
participants in generating, distributing, and vending energy (Oyiana, 2021). At the same time, 
women are either ceremonially involved in the same processes or are part-time employees 
involved in the informal vending of energy in their homes. The next section explains gendered 
energy technology innovation leading to energy access.

2.2.3 Energy technology innovation for achieving gender equality in energy services 
As explained above, ensuring reliable energy services continues to pose a significant challenge 
in impoverished rural areas across Africa. A considerable number of households face a deficit 
in appropriate energy technologies to meet their modern energy demands. Women bear the 
brunt of this issue due to entrenched gender roles within households. Despite the evident gender 
disparities in energy services, the incorporation of gender perspectives in the innovation of 
energy products remains largely unaddressed. This research study delved into the intersection 
of gendered innovation and energy innovation, investigating how this combination can 
alleviate issues surrounding the security and accessibility of energy services in households 


=== PAGE BREAK ===

Page | 29 
 
within impoverished rural settings and went ahead to develop a biogas technology to a pilot 
scale with gendered perspective. 
Innovation has long been a cornerstone of human progress, driving pivotal shifts in agriculture, 
science, and industry throughout history. Today, in our modern, technology-driven societies 
powered by digital advancements, energy innovation plays a pivotal role in facilitating access 
to a wide array of services (batinge et al., 2022). Energy services encompass a spectrum of 
benefits facilitated by energy carriers, fuel sources, and technologies, (Sovacool, 2011). These 
benefits extend to various outcomes of energy utilisation (Modi et al., 2005), ranging from 
preserved food and cooked meals to illuminated spaces, chilled beverages, rapid internet 
connectivity, and cosy sleeping arrangements (Sovacool, 2011).  
The conventional centralised approach to innovation is swiftly losing relevance 
(MacCormack et al., 2007). Top-down innovation typically follows a sequence of idea 
generation, screening, market research, product development, commercialization, and 
implementation (Gaynor, 2013). However, the limitations of these traditional top-down 
models have been evident in academic discourse for approximately four decades, these 
limitations encompass neglect of alternative actors (Sabatier, 1986), limited user ownership, 
prolonged innovation readiness periods, and potential market evolution by the time of 
product availability. Furthermore, even in cases where the market remains static, there's a 
considerable risk that the innovation may not align with market needs due to flaws in market 
research or misperceptions regarding innovation diffusion (Batinge 2022). The process by 
which innovations are adopted within a society (innovation diffusion) is a dynamic and 
evolving phenomenon (Bale et al., 2013). The concept of how innovations spread is well-
established in the study of household energy usage (Wilson & Dowlatabadi, 2007). Studies 
across various disciplines have examined how technologies and ideas spread through 
networks (Valente, 2005). An infection paradigm may also be used here to indicate the 
process of network diffusion (Akbarpour & Jackson, 2018; Pastor-Satorras et al., 2015; Raj et 
al., 2012), as well as an epidemic with the very beginning and the potential development of 
the spread of a disease and its further consequences. This analysis would cannot be complete 
without the mention of the Bass model which was proposed in 1969 and is a clear example of 
a diffusion model. 
The Bass model developed by Frank Bass defines the process of new products adoption via the 
communication between current and potential users. Although several models of diffusion 


=== PAGE BREAK ===

Page | 30 
 
focus on social aspects, which in the model of Bass are in the form of word-of mouth influence 
(Bale et al., 2013), the decision making process of the adoption of a new product depends on 
this. Such elements can include the likelihood of its purchase, compatibility of the new product 
with other products, appropriateness of the technology in view of the individual characteristics, 
and the social-cultural factors. Those decisions are usually taken based on such relative factors 
as ‘intrinsic advantages for the intended adoption’ and the technology ‘compatibility with the 
existing technology environment’, as well as ‘simplicity in the usage of a given technology’, 
the possibility of ‘trial run’, and the ‘clearliness of results’ (Vagnani & Volpe, 2017). Perceived 
benefits refer to the extent to which an innovation is believed to be more beneficial than the 
concept which it replaces. Adoption by others is the degree to which an innovation matches up 
to current practices, past experiences and needs of other users. IPO means how difficult or easy 
it is considered to understand and implement an innovation. Trial option can be defined as 
potential in which an innovation can be first tried on a limited level. Observability of its impact 
is the level of measurability in terms of how others are aware of the presence of an innovation. 
On this note, it is in most models explaining the diffusion of innovations that an innovation's 
ability to fulfill users' needs is critical towards the adoption decision. For these reasons, this 
study suggested that the innovation process takes into consideration the context in which the 
user will eventually apply the innovation while developing the innovation based on the specific 
attributes identified in Vagnani and Volpe, (2017) for the innovation to serve exactly according 
to the needs of end consumers. In low-income households, where females are the majority, 
their energy use is significantly higher—about 80% more overall, with a notable 54% increase 
in electricity consumption compared to males (Elnakat et al., 2016). These communities, as 
highlighted by Elnakat et al. (2016), have a higher energy consumption per unit of living area, 
attributed to the prevalence of female-led households and the inefficiency of their homes. The 
role of women is pivotal in achieving satisfaction with household energy services in 
economically disadvantaged areas, given their dominance in such households (Kovacic et al., 
2019) and their higher usage of energy services. 
Concerning energy access, there was a reveal that gender disparities were apparent in many 
African countries, including South Africa (Ngarava et al., 2022). Women, and especially those 
living in the irregular structures have limited access to the energy resources. They experience 
these barriers in their day to day life and in their opportunities to work, to earn an income and 
thus be included in society. Mukumba, Makaka, and Mamphweli (2016) further note that

women in these communities are most affected by energy poverty because they are the ones

These negative effects are not only health wise but also economically as limited energy access

have very little role in the decision making that impacts their lives through policies and projects. 
As a consequence, energy solutions fail to meet the female demand and choice and therefore, 
have low usage and sometimes undesirable impacts. For example, energy projects may target 
technologies that are in some way detrimental to the usability of the female population, thus 
reducing the utility of those projects. 
To address gender inequality in the access to energy we need to effectively implement gender-
aiding policies, programs, and technologies in the provision of energy. Musango et al., (2020) 
emphasise that women should be included in the energy planning processes and the decision-
making process in practices and activities in order that their specific needs and perspectives are 
recognized. To ensure better solutions of energy in various aspects, it is time we involve women 
as active stakeholders in that process. For instance, engaging women in the design and 
implementation of solar energy solutions can promote the creation of unique solutions 
according to women’s usual schedules and dangers involved with electricity systems.

Bergvall-Kåreborn et al. (2009:1) defined the Living Lab as “an environment in which people 
and technology are gathered and in which the everyday context and user need stimulate and 
challenge both research and developments since authorities and citizens take active part in the 
innovation process.” Bergvall-Kåreborn et al. (2009) definition seems to highlight aspects 
similar to the focus of this proposed research, although the definition was not specific to the 
end-users. For this research study, the end-users will include women.

energy designs, innovations, services, and new processes are developed and tested with the 
involvement of the end-users (women) and researchers. Therefore, the Living Lab, as defined

products, and societal infrastructures.” Similarly, Santoro and Conte (2016) definition have 
elements similar to the research focus.

Living Lab is an active and effective concept of research and development where the actual 
user of the product is involved in the process of development (Choi and Kwon, 2021). Being 
open innovation platforms focused on the users, Living Labs offers a systematic approach of 
co-creation that involves the combination of research and innovation activities in the real

solutions that are being developed workable because they serve a developmental function, but

enable interdisciplinary cooperation (Leal Filho et al., 2022). Since Living Labs involve people 
from different disciplines like technology, social sciences and policy, problem solving can be 
viewed and tackled from various perspectives at the same time as opposed to traditional 
perspectives where a problem is solved strictly from a single angle (Tercanli and Jongbloed, 
2022). This is a relevant approach since issues like energy poverty are social problems, which 
also have a technical and economic component.

Utilizer-driven Living Labs 
This type of Living Lab is usually short-lived or in the form of a project-based because they 
usually contend for speedy results and supports research context and co-design with end-users 
(Schuurman et al., 2015). The focus here is to produce and test new products and services 
because they are launched to develop businesses. However, the utilizers make use of the Living 
Lab as a planned tool to investigate their product and services on the users to be in a central 
position before the products are developed (Schuurman et al., 2015). 
Therefore, this type of Living Lab seems to resemble the focus of co-designing, which requires 
end-users to take part in the decision-making activities before the development of the products, 
and this is in line with the research study. 
User-driven Living Labs 
Unlike the utilizer-driven Living Lab, the “user-driven Living Labs” are long-lived since they 
are developed in or around the end-users environments (Schuurman et al., 2015), and this kind 
of Living Labs are quite uncommon because it might be a result of the enabler-driven Living 
Lab (which is discussed below), where the end-users are required and propelled to participate 
in the innovation activities, although it could be challenging to start this type of Living Lab 
from scratch by the community end-users themselves  (Leminen et al., 2012). 
This type of Living Lab benefits the users more and benefits the stakeholders indirectly because 
the focus is co-created for the end-users mainly and established by end-users with a focus on 
solving everyday environmental life problems (Schuurman et al., 2015). This type of Living 
Lab shows promises of how the SETI Living Lab would look like. 
Enabler-driven Living Labs 
The enabler-driven Living Lab is also a long-lived one that involves the public sector actors, 
non-governmental sectors, towns, and municipalities whose aim is to carry out projects to 
produce social innovations or environmental developments (Schuurman et al., 2015). From 
table 2.4 above, the observation is that the “enabler-driven Living Labs” are similar to the 
knowledge sharing and collaboration activities which indicates that the central part here is the 
creation and dissemination of information and knowledge through the stakeholders and end-
users involved in the system (Schuurman et al., 2015). 


=== PAGE BREAK ===

Page | 70 
 
Therefore, the SETI Living Lab also intends to create and disseminate knowledge gained from 
the participation of the end-users and those involved in the energy technology innovation 
processes and not mainly from the stakeholders. 
Provider-driven Living Labs 
The “provider-driven Living Labs” aims at improving the lives of the users by allowing all 
participants in the system to gain from the outcome of the innovation, which most of the 
innovation looks at producing candid knowledge and information for all involved in the system 
(Schuurman et al., 2015). These providers can be those in public and private organisations who 
participate in the “Living Lab” to co-design new products, services, and bring solutions to the 
needs of the industry. 
Some “provider-driven Living Labs” are established as long-lived platforms for innovation and 
aiming to advance research development of theory, expanding the creation of knowledge and 
bringing about specific solutions to problems (Schuurman et al., 2015). Observing table 2.4 
above, the provider-driven Living Labs falls into the Original American type of Living Lab, 
which uses a lab infrastructure for the study of end-users in an environment, like the urban poor 
environment. The testbed Living Lab can also fall into the Provider-driven Living Labs, where 
the “technical testbed infrastructure” is used to develop and test “innovative ICT” services 
(Schuurman et al., 2015). 
However, Schuurman et al. (2015, p.5-6) state that Leminen et al. (2012) argue that “Living 
Labs are usually either utilizer-driven or provider-driven because both emphasise efficiency 
with regards to the firm's investments, our observation complements this statement, as Living 
Labs can be a provider- and utilizer-driven, depending on the focus of the analysis (case-based 
or infrastructure-based).” This type of Living Lab is inversely related to the proposed SETI 
Living Lab. 
Therefore, the typologies of the Living Labs have been identified, however, most of them lack 
the aspect of gender. The argument remains that the existing Living Lab typologies are gender-
neutral, which means they focus on users without incorporating the gender aspect. They also 
do not incorporate the systems approach into the development processes. Therefore, this 
research study will contribute by developing the SETI Living Lab typology, which focuses on 
users incorporating the gender aspect with the use of systems approach.

2.3.5 Living 
Lab 
in

Living Labs have become an indispensable tool for advancing innovations in the energy field, 
as they can serve as an experimental model for developing and implementing energy solutions 
within real-life contexts (Mbatha and Musango, 2022). This approach ensures that the 
developed technologies are not only technologically feasible, but it is also realistically possible 
and would focus on the end-users. Thus, when addressing energy needs throughout different 
contexts including rural informal settlements, energy Living Labs result in responsible 
innovation that has been grounded on the principles of inclusivity, reflexivity and 
sustainability. 
In the energy sector, Living Labs can be defined as a facility, venues where stakeholders such 
as end-users, researchers, from the industry, policymakers and community organisations work 
in cooperation to create and with the help of experiments, test energy solutions (Tercanli and 
Jongbloed, 2022). This participatory approach is essential because the innovations would in 
real sense come with realities for the targeted users, especially individuals in the underserved 
markets. Living Labs include people directly in the innovation process, capturing vital ideas 
and input that traditional research settings may miss (Campos & Marín-González, 2023). 
The energy efficiency projects implemented in Living Labs can have many benefits, one of 
them is pre-implementation of activities in real-life conditions (von Geibler et al., 2014). This 
implies that energy technologies can be subjected to usage conditions before requiring 
complete use with the real world, making the tests more realistic, practical and revealing 
dependability, practicality and benefits. For example, new technologies in renewable energy 
like solar energy or wind energy devices can be installed in societies in order to assess the 
adaptability of the technology in offering permanent and sustainable energy. In the following 
deployments of these technologies, information collected from the real world can then be used 
to enhance them and adapt them to suit the needs of the users (Fortiş & Petcu, 2022). 
In the context of energy Living Labs, Campos and Marín-González (2023) emphasise the 
necessity of being responsible for the innovation processes that are implemented. It implies a 
situation whereby all parties can contribute their quota towards the generation, development, 
and marketing of the innovation, preferably, women. This is of particular concern whenever 


=== PAGE BREAK ===

Page | 80 
 
energy poverty also comes into the picture, such as when women are mainly responsible for 
managing energy, fuel, and lighting at home but are rarely involved in decision-making about 
energy sources (Botha et al., 2012). In this Case, Living Labs could involve more Women in 
co-creation as well as testing of energy technologies to ensure products with satisfactory 
solutions to their problems are designed. In the Energy Living Labs area, reflexivity is 
understood as the dynamic self-reflectiveness of the innovation processes (von Wirth et al., 
2018). This means that as in any innovation process the ongoing evaluation of the energy 
solutions and of the process of innovation has to be done, in order to fine tune the energy 
solutions and the process to fit the user needs. This cyclical action of thinking and modifying 
guarantees that the changes remain appropriate and efficient throughout the period (Herselman 
et al., 2010). 
Another principle is sustainability where energy Living Labs aim to develop solutions that can 
be managed in such a way as to protect the environment and preserve resources for future 
generations (Callaghan and Herselman, 2015). This relates not only to the creation but also to 
the long-term effectiveness of innovation solutions based on environmentally friendly energy 
technologies. Sustainable innovation in energy Living Labs aims at maintaining economic, 
social, and environmental aspects while seeking to meet overarching sustainability objectives 
of energy production and use like reduction of emission levels and more equality in energy 
access (Ersoy & Van Bueren, 2020). The figure 2.7 below extracted from Veeckman et al 
(2013) highlights the Living Lab triangle that sought to illustrate the proximity relations 
between the users, the technology developers and the researchers within the Living Lab 
context.

Introducing Living Labs also enables the coordination of technical, social, and policy aspects 
of innovations in the field of energy (Campos and Marín-González, 2023). For instance, the 
adoption of the Living Labs as an environment enables the implementation of smart grids that 
can accommodate integrated energy management systems that will help in minimising energy 
wastage. These systems can also be coupled with renewable energy, thus allowing for improved 
energy efficiency and optimal use of energy resources. Through the integration of 
policymakers, these innovations can be attached to the specific guidelines and policies to reach 
all members of the society (Mbatha & Musango, 2022). With regards to South Africa, where 
the majority of the population does not have access to clean, efficient, and affordable energy 
sources, Living Labs may indeed be a more appropriate solution to the challenges posed by 
energy poverty. By adopting the spirit of participation, self-interpretation, and co-design, 
Energy Living Labs can create solutions to cater for these communities. This approach 
enhances not only the energy extended to these communities and security, but is also perceived 
as an innovation platform where the community stands to benefit directly. In addition, Energy 
Living Labs positively responded to Sustainable Development Goal 5 (SDG 5) on gender 
equality for women and girls (Giannetti et al., 2020). Since the energy idea and its practical 
application is co-designed and trialled with the target beneficiaries, Living Labs can help make

Brief examples from South Africa and African context 
In South Africa, it becomes evident that several living labs are implementing energy efficiency 
and the desired change. Several Living Labs in South Africa testify to the success of the 
implementation. Perhaps the most prominent example is the Stellenbosch Smart City program, 
which focuses on implementing new smart energy solutions through the Living Lab approach 
(Mukama et al., 2022). The participants involved in this project include the local authorities, 
institutions of higher learning, private organisations, and the general population. The subject is 
based on the promotion of using wind power and other sources of cleanly generated electrical 
power, improving the efficiency of energy usage, and developing eco-friendly technology in 
urban planning. With the Living Lab methodology, the project has experimented with several 
sustainable technologies like solar PV power, energy-efficient lighting, and smart advanced 
metres (Molinari et al., 2023). 
Another successful example is the iShack project that has been implemented at Stellenbosch 
and aims to advance access to energy in the context of Community-Based Management 
(Bloem, Swilling and Koranteng, 2021). It employs a Living Lab project cycle incorporating 
residents in the sustainable energy solution development. The iShack Project touches on energy 
and social issues by incorporating solar power and housing improvements. Living Lab has 
helped identify and offer solutions to numerous clients by delivering cheap and dependable 
power to several inhabitants and proving that Living Lab is well equipped to solve complicated 
socio-economic problems (Sphokazi Phelokazi, 2022). 
From a regional perspective, Energy Living Lab in Kenya is among the few that have 
successfully adopted this concept in Africa (Mbatha and Musango, 2022). The following is an 
initiative to employ decentralised renewable energy systems to enhance access to electricity in 
rural areas. It focuses on the users since the local communities participate in the innovation 
process to develop optimum solutions to the problems in their daily routine activities. The 
project has introduced several technologies in the categories of renewable energy, such as solar 
microgrids and biogas systems, which have greatly enhanced energy provision and availability 
to most of the targeted communities (Valentyna Loboichenko et al., 2024).

2.3.6   Conceptualising systems Living Lab: Living Lab, energy Living and systems approach 
The systems energy technology innovation Living Lab (SETILL) will be developed in chapter

The SETILL is the link between the Living Lab, energy Living Lab and systems approach (SD

Systems Living Labs can be characterised as a new paradigm of innovation and problem 
solving, especially useful for use in modern society, where problems are often closely 
connected. Systems Living Labs differ from the previous ones as the latter are applied to single 
technologies or solutions for complex issues within complex and constantly changing 
environments (Hossain, Leminen, & Westerlund, 2019). Thus, the systems Living Lab will be 
developed in this study as a Living Lab used to solve energy challenges with the use of a 
systems approach. This approach accepts that problems that exist in real life occur within the 
context of connected webs of multiple associates that are interdependent, such that actions 
taken in one part of the web influence and affect the rest of the web (von Wirth et al., 2018).

end-users’ quality of life. 
The iterative development process is an important part of Systems Living Lab methodology, 
in which a few iterations of development that are based on the same logical framework are 
made (Leal Filho et al., 2022). This process encompasses a cycle of prototyping the new 
communication technology, testing it with users, gathering feedback, and improving on it. It

the design and operation of the energy technology that was reviewed in the previous step.

and users. The identification of stakeholders and end-users as participants in the development 
process is indispensable to the success of energy innovations. In the case of Botha et al. (2012), 
the authors underscore that adopting the co-design and co-testing of the solutions makes it 
possible not only to arrive at technically feasible solutions but also to evaluate the social 
acceptability of the solutions.

hardships of the community; for instance, they have to work with limited resources and

complexity through conceptual modelling enhances the understanding of reinforcing 
behaviours and feedback mechanisms at various levels. This process, in turn, deepens 
comprehension of the system and solidifies concepts and patterns. The system's objective is 
achieved by unravelling complexity and understanding, predicting, and adjusting feedback 
mechanisms to yield effective outcomes. Within these key processes, new processes can be 
added or removed to improve the understanding of system behaviour. These processes create 
a behaviour pattern within the system that can be either 'reinforcing' or 'dynamic' in nature.

2.4.2 Biogas 
technology 
Biogas technology is a relatively new way of harnessing waste to produce renewable energy 
that is referred to as biogas, which is made of methane and carbon dioxide in large proportions 
(Luostarinen & Normak, 2011). This conversion is referred to as anaerobic digestion, and it 
entails the decomposition of organic inputs like agricultural waste, manure, municipal waste, 
plant biomass, sewage, and food waste using microorganisms that do not require oxygen 
(Luostarinen & Normak, 2011). The outcome is biogas, which can be used as fuel for cooking, 
heating, and the generation of electricity, thus acting as a sustainable and renewable source of 
energy. In the study area, Uganda, biogas is an ideal technology since it solves waste disposal 
problems in most areas with poor waste management systems in the informal settlements as 
well as serves the purpose of an energy source (Sphokazi Phelokazi, 2022). Due to its 
versatility, this technology can be applied more frequently to independents and communities, 
which are often prevalent in squatter camps. Since biogas technology manages to convert waste 
into energy, we can minimise the amount of waste and the risks associated with pollution. 
Mukumba, Makaka, and Mamphweli (2016) argue that biogas technology is another promising 
technology able to help South Africa overcome the shortage of energy sources and record the 
significance of waste management. However, they stress the need to establish adequate policies 
and a conducive environment that will encourage the rapid deployment of this technology. 
Secondly, they encouraged that communities should embrace the projects and be informed, as 
this will increase the chances of the successful advancement of biogas projects. 
Biogas digesters operate based on providing a specific environment where anaerobic 
microorganisms will break down organic matter (Achinas and Euverink, 2020). The process 
involves several stages: First, the process involves hydrolysis; second, it involves acid 
oogenesis; third, it involves acetogenesis, followed by methanogenesis. Various complex 
organic compounds get resolved or split into soluble molecules in hydrolysis. The process, 
often called acidogenesis, transforms these molecules into volatile fatty acids (Ramos-Suarez 
et al., 2021). In acetogenesis, these acids are further degraded to acetic acid, hydrogen, and 
carbon dioxide. Lastly, the methanogenesis of the products creates methane and carbon-dioxide 
to form biogas. The direct utilisation of biogas is applicable as fuel for cooking and heating or 
can be upgraded to electricity. This is in line with this research study. 
These biogas plants or systems vary depending on the kind of system and circumstances 
involved. For small-scale producers, family-sized digesters include fixed-dome or floating-


=== PAGE BREAK ===

Page | 105 
 
drum systems installed in rural houses on average (Rasimphi et al., 2022). These systems are 
cheap & appropriate for handling organic waste from homes and animal manure. Small-scale 
digesters are suitable for single farm holdings or even small communities, while larger-scale 
digesters are suitable for larger volumes of organic waste (Huber, 2019). Industrial-scale 
digesters are used in industries, municipalities, and large farming operations. Large-scale 
digesters are planned for extensive production, waste management, and farming communities 
(Budiman, 2021). These systems are usually more comprehensive and may incorporate other 
sub-systems for purification and storage of the biogas, thus making them ideal for large-scale 
energy generation.

There are therefore multiple advantages to the adoption of biogas technology, especially in

retrofitting aspect of it. They note that biogas technology holds potential for reducing green

release green gases into the atmosphere when decomposing.

Energy needs and challenges in the informal settlements: current energy access and usage 
patterns 
Energy access and use or emerge as a factor deeply unequal in diagrams and largely 
dependent on traditional energy resources in the investigated IHs (Oswald et al., 2020). Most 
of the inhabitants living in these settlements rely on biomass energy which include firewood 
and charcoal and kerosene among others. These sources are commonly used for cooking, 
heating and lighting since these are cheaper and more easily accessible to people compared to 
today’s energy forms such as electricity and liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) (Puzzolo et al.,

2019). Biomass fuels, however, come with a number of difficulties; they are not efficient and 
pose health issues from indoor pollution. 
Apparently, the energy utilization profiles in the IECs tend to be associated with the 
demographics and income standards of the households and existing energy supplies. Usually, 
the energy use is low given that only a small proportion of the population has access to 
modern energy services and owing to the high energy–income ratio (Jeuland, Biggs, & 
White, 2021). Majority of households only use energy for cooking and limited lighting hence 
leaving little power for other uses which are refrigeration, communication and entertainment 
(Aliero et al., 2021). This limited energy consumption limits economic growth and 
development and) nullifies quality of lives, keeping the community stuck in poverty. 
Some disparities in energy access become very noticeable in the informal settlements 
especially because development infrastructure in such areas is usually limited compared to the 
developed urban and rural areas. The absence of legal electricity relationships and secure 
energy contributes to making many families poor (Adom et al., 2021). On this regard, this gap 
is worsened by the fact most of these settlements are informal and rarely included in the 
national energy planning and policies (Kovacic et al., 2020). Prospects remain bleak for 
residents to attain overall energy security and end-users are forced to use costly and unreliable 
means of power in their homes. 
Socioeconomic 
and

impacts 
A priority that is linked with energy poverty in induced informal settlements has far reaching 
socioeconomic implication. Socio economically, energy affects household usage of income 
whereby a lot of money is utilized to cater for energy thus reducing the income available to pay 
for education health and other basic facilities. Dependence on kerosene and charcoal as source 
of energy is very expensive and since they depend on market forces their prices are also subject 
to change hence the economic vulnerability of the households (Ang’u, 2023). Also, very few 
people can afford a reliable source of energy and energy prices are very high which derails 
investment in income-generating activities hence slows the economic growth and poverty 
eradication. 
Energy poverty is a major issue having crippled health implications as well as adverse effects 
on the environment. The burning of biomass fuels to cook and heat causes emissions and fumes 
that endanger people’s indoor health by giving them diseases related to the respiratory system.

Firewood and charcoal are an environmental nuisance because of the provision of raw materials

Thus, the effects of energy poverty vary across genders; this is especially the case with informal

with substantial exposure to IAP are more pronounced among women, thereby making them 
even more vulnerable. 
As for gender, it is important to understand that women living in informal settlements have 
better yet different energy access and use prospects (Pearl-Martinez, 2020). It establishes them 
as responsible for providing energy to their homes, thereby making them over-reliant on

firewood or buying the fuel hinders them from their opportunity to get an education or earn an 
income. Also, women lack proper access to funds, credit, and decision-making, so they have

modern energy solutions. This exclusion from the energy sector influences the entrenched 
gender inequalities while at the same time retarding efforts at attaining energy security. 
However, there are opportunities for women in energy solutions in the informal settlements, 
especially since the field has numerous challenges, as mentioned. Enabling and supporting 
women with a specific focus through energy programs will likely bring about a drastic change 
in the accessibility and utilisation of energy besides the female status. For instance, it is 
conceivable that engaging women in the conception, planning, and deployment of energy 
projects increases the relevance of the solutions delivered (Chirambo, 2020). Women can be

1.3 The Living Lab concepts as applied to the SETILL model 
The energy efficiency market is controlled by an innovative techno-centric model, which 
indicates that users are the logical decision-makers who will effectively accept and use the 
energy technologies once they are informed of the gain the system has to the economic and 
environmental aspects (Caird and Roy, 2008). The Living Lab concepts have been discussed 
in Chapter 2 of this research study and will be briefly discussed in this chapter, leading to the 
SETILL model.  
Therefore, it has been noticed in this research study that the definitions of the Living Labs as a 
concept are peculiarly dependent on the field it is used for. Generally, the Living Lab involves 
studying and developing innovations in a real-world environment like the communities 
(Mukama et al., 2022). This allows for the visibility and installation of the technology 
innovations in the communities, thereby impacting them (Ballon, Pierson and Delaere, 2005; 
Hossain, Leminen and Westerlund, 2019) and leading to the adoption and acceptance of the 
innovations. Furthermore, due to the participation of end-users in co-creating technology 
innovations in the Living Labs and the real-world environment, Dell’Era and Landoni (2014) 
argued that the definitions of the Living Lab research lacked the inclusion of producing novel 
innovations in the Living Lab, which is why Living Lab was defined as “a design research 
methodology aimed at co-creating innovation through the involvement of aware users in a real-
life setting” Dell’Era and Landoni (2014:139). 
Furthermore, during the study carried out by Hossain, Leminen and Westerlund (2019:2), the 
Living Lab was defined as “a physical or virtual space in which to solve societal challenges, 
especially for urban areas by bringing together various stakeholders for collaboration and 
collective ideation”. These definitions identified above are all in agreement with this research 
study, however, this study disagrees with the Living Lab being specifically for the urban 
environment, but the Living Lab should be developed anywhere (rural and urban) for the 
benefit of the communities or stakeholders involved (Almirall, Lee and Wareham, 2012). The 
Living Lab as a resilient methodology is fully focused on the ideas or insights obtained from 
the end-users (Almirall, Lee and Wareham, 2012), to achieve a specific objective (Ahmadi et 
al., 2020), which allows for various designs in the Living Lab suited for the technology 
innovations to be developed (Mukama et al., 2022). Therefore, in the case of the women in the 
informal settlement who are not empowered, are marginalised and do not have the 
opportunities to co-design the innovations, the Living Lab will allow them to contribute and 


=== PAGE BREAK ===

Page | 123 
 
co-design the energy innovations in the communities. This is referred to as the Living Lab as 
a ‘safe space’ (Ahmadi et al., 2020). 
The Living Lab as a methodology is classified into the following according to Almirall, Lee 
and Wareham (2012): User-Centric Design (UCD), design-driven, and participatory-driven. 
The UCD has been introduced in Chapter 1 of this study and this is the process where the main 
passive collaborators are the end-users. The UCD is introduced in this research study as the 
research study intends to develop a new technology innovation to be tested and to analyse how 
technologies are affected by human factors (Mukama et al., 2022). This is because, in a 
research area, the Living Lab was defined by (Schaer, Schaible and Müller, 2020) as “a user-
centric study methodology for researchers to evaluate the performance of retrieval systems 
within real-world applications”. This implies that the end-users are to be involved in the 
research as co-researchers to achieve a common goal. Therefore, the definitions above and the 
Living Lab concepts as applied to SETILL can be suitable to the phenomena being studied 
because it involves the ‘end-users’ and the ‘real-world testing environment’. These major

1.4 Structuring and adapting the Living Lab leading to SETILL model 
The Living Labs and the gender studies have been discussed in Chapter 2 of this study. The 
Living Labs are seen as methodologies, real-world scenarios and landscapes where various 
approaches, techniques and concepts are used by several stakeholders for a common goal 
(Hossain, Leminen and Westerlund, 2019). However, the successful development of Living 
Labs and their results are mostly affected by several challenges like the maintenance of the 
innovation, and the engagement of the end-users and stakeholders. There are several Living 
Labs in the world with a lens of systems thinking in them. Such as the Sustainable Urban 
Neighbourhood (SUN) research projects which took place in Europe, the Living Lab for Smart 
Mobility situated in Eindhoven Netherlands, the Systems Living Lab situated in Oulu Finland 
and at the University of Trento in Italy.  However, at the moment it is not confirmed if these 
Living Labs still exist, but the point is that they used the systems thinking lens to develop and 
test products in collaboration with different stakeholders, but, with no gender lens. 
Furthermore, in South Africa, although there are several Innovation hubs in the form of the 
Living Lab focused on energy technology innovation, most of them lacked the focus on gender 
and systems thinking perspectives. Generally, these innovation hubs may not focus on gender 
design in energy technology innovation in South Africa, but they work to improve gender 
equality in other sectors. There are also research studies that have used the soft systems 
methodology lens towards a Living Lab methodology for smart cities, innovation management, 
concept designs, transitioning smart campus to cities, to develop a community Living Lab, 
framework, forming future IT to show the Living Lab processes for the involvement of users, 
for user-centric co-creation, and for the evaluation of Living Labs to innovation (Mirijamdotter 
et al., 2006; Kong and Li, 2007; Ståhlbröst, 2008; Bergvall-Kåreborn, Holst and Ståhlbröst,

The government is one of the stakeholders in the Living Lab and its connection with the

1.6 Conceptualising the SETILL theoretical model 
The SETI Living Lab model as conceptualised will be implemented as part of any hub of 
energy technology innovation with gender design and systems thinking approach in poor 
informal settlements. The SETILL model could be a virtual place if there is no space due to 
social distancing. This means that it could be done virtually by bringing together those involved 
online, and it can also be mobile, which means that it can be moved to any convenient space 
regardless of where the energy innovation is to be developed. Also, the SETILL model is not 
complete if there is no energy technology innovation developed to benefit a particular end-
users chosen by the researcher in collaboration with other stakeholders. 
Therefore, considering the project of the proposed SETILL context, the Living Lab is defined 
as physical or virtual and mobile research and systems thinking approach space for design 
methodology focused on co-creating and testing of energy innovations through the involvement 
of end-users in a real-life scenario towards mitigating energy poverty for the informal 
settlements by collaborating with various stakeholders to (1) understand the material cultures, 
cognitive norms and energy practices or mechanisms influencing the level of adoption of 
energy innovations, (2) to investigate the influence of end-users contributions on energy 
technology innovation in a systems Living Lab, (3) develop and test an energy technology

Page | 137 
 
innovation up to a pilot scale through the involvement of end-users, (4) promote energy 
security, gender equality and women empowerment.

knowledge contribution of the innovative design. The users would be involved in the co-
creation of the energy services solutions, co-design of energy technology innovations, 
prototyping, and testing. Although men are also part of the users, for the context of this research 
study, the focus is on women. A different study that wants to use the SETILL model can decide 
to choose a different user if possible.
