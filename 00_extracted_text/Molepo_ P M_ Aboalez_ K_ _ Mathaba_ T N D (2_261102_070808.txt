A B S T R A C T
Renewable energy sources hold immense potential for meeting future energy demands while significantly
reducing greenhouse gas emissions. Despite South Africa's abundant wind and solar resources, the country's
transition to renewable energy has remained stagnant. Several barriers hinder the integration of these tech-
nologies into the energy system. This study applied the AKTESP theoretical framework to analyse the key
challenges impeding wind and solar energy adoption in South Africa. Fifteen semi-structured interviews with
industry experts were conducted to gather insights into these barriers. The analysis revealed that agreement,
social, and technological categories are the most critical barriers obstructing South Africa’s energy transition.
Among the most significant barriers are the lack of grid connection, the lack of policy continuity (both mentioned
by 100% of participants), and limited public awareness (mentioned by 87% of participants). In contrast, political,
market, economic, and knowledge-related barriers were found to be less significant. South Africa has well-
developed policies and knowledgeable stakeholders capable of supporting renewable energy projects and
facilitating their integration into the national grid.
Introduction
South Africa (SA)'s power supply challenges began in late 2007 due
to insufficient generation capacity (Mtukushe, 2021). However, in 2023,
the country experienced increasingly severe and frequent power out-
ages, with blackouts lasting several hours daily (Wiese & van der
Westhuizen, 2024). These persistent outages highlighted a deepening
power generation crisis. The state utility, Eskom, is struggling with a
deteriorating power generation system that is overwhelmed by rising
energy demand (Akinbami et al., 2021). The country generates more
than 80% of its electricity from fossil fuels (Fitzgerald et al., 2024),
contributing significantly to environmental and climate challenges. The
extensive use of coal for electricity generation has positioned South
Africa among the top ten global contributors of greenhouse gas (GHG)
emissions (Akinbami et al., 2021).
With growing energy demand and Eskom’s inability to maintain a
stable power supply, developing clean energy is essential to address this
crisis and reduce greenhouse gas emissions from coal burning (Akinbami
et al., 2021; Asante et al., 2020). South Africa is rich in renewable re-
sources, including abundant solar energy, high wind potential, and
substantial biomass reserves (Akinbami et al., 2021; Amir & Zubair,
2022). South Africa’s annual 24-hour global solar radiation average is
220 W/m2, compared to 150 W/m2 for the United States and 100 W/m2
for Europe and the United Kingdom (Smarte Anekwe et al., 2024). The
annual average wind speed is over 4 m/s (Akinbami et al., 2021)
compared to 3 m/s for Europe (European Union, 2024) and 6 m/s for the
United States (Roy & MacPhee, 2018). However, wind power generation
is not directly proportional to the mean wind speed, due to the nonlinear
relationship between wind speed and power output (Pandit & Kolios,
2020). Additionally, factors such as the standard deviation of wind
speed (which reflects variability) (Lee et al., 2020), power density,
turbine type, hub height, and site-specific characteristics (Landwehr
et al., 2024) all play a critical role in determining actual energy
production.
By the end of Quarter 3 of 2024, renewable energy (RE) accounted
for approximately 12.6% of South Africa’s electricity supply. This in-
cludes contributions of 4.2% from solar photovoltaic (PV), 0.9% from
solar thermal, 6.4% from wind power, 0.3% from hybrid systems (solar
PV and battery storage), and 1.1% from hydropower (Eskom, 2024;
Fitzgerald et al., 2024). In comparison, by the end of 2024, solar energy
contributed around 40% of total electricity capacity in Europe and 30%
in the United States, while wind power accounted for 32% and 25%,

Africa lacks the urgency and consistency needed to prioritize renewable
energy development. The penetration of renewables into the grid re-

due to existential barriers (Walwyn, 2021).
South Africa's transition to a just energy system remains unstable,
with abundant solar and wind resources largely untapped due to these
existential barriers (Todd & Mccauley, 2021). These barriers are dy-
namic and evolving as renewable energy development progresses. While
some previously identified obstacles have been addressed, new chal-
lenges emerge (Nouri et al., 2022). Despite the country’s ongoing elec-
tricity crisis and delays in renewable energy development, limited
academic research focuses on IPPs' challenges in developing and inte-
grating renewable energy into South Africa’s power system. The land-
scape of renewable energy barriers is constantly shifting, shaped by
technological advancements, political uncertainty, and other factors
(Mercer et al., 2017). In order to support the development and inte-
gration of renewable energies into the national energy system, it is
necessary to continuously and quickly assess these obstacles.
This research aims to answer the following question: What are the
renewable energy barriers faced by Independent Power Producers in
South Africa? A growing number of studies have explored the barriers to
renewable energy development and integration in South Africa. The
study by (Todd & Mccauley, 2021) identified key policy barriers
impeding energy transition in South Africa and solutions to mitigate
their effect. Research conducted by (Pegels, 2010) identified barriers to
renewable energy investment in South Africa and formulated policy
recommendations for these barriers. A study by (Mirzania et al., 2023)
assessed the constraints impacting South Africa’s energy transition
ambitions. Although these studies have assessed renewable energy
barriers, none have applied a theoretical framework for barrier identi-
fication and analysis. This study aims to address this gap by making the
following key contributions: First, it identifies renewable energy barriers
and contextualises them to the South African market through expert
interviews. Second, it refines the theoretical framework used for barrier
analysis by introducing a two-level structure, enabling a more compre-
hensive understanding of the underlying causes. Finally, the study offers
practical policy recommendations to effectively address these barriers.
The structure of the study is as follows: Section 2 provides a literature
review on the current state of renewable energy in South Africa. Section
3 outlines the selection of the theoretical framework. Section 4 describes
the data collection methodology, while Section 5 presents the study’s
findings. Section 6 discusses the findings in the South African context
and Section 7 concludes the study, discusses its limitations, and provides
policy implications and recommendations for future research.
Literature review
South Africa’s current electricity status
As shown in Fig. 1, at the end of the third quarter (Q3) of 2024, South
Africa's electricity generation capacity was distributed as follows: 39
000 MW from coal, 1 900 MW from nuclear, 3 400 MW from gas and
diesel, 6 230 MW from renewable sources (including photovoltaic,
concentrated solar power (CSP), and wind), and 3 550 MW from hydro,
pumped storage, and hybrid systems (Eskom, 2024; Fitzgerald et al.,
2024). These energy outputs are produced by Eskom and IPPs partici-
pating in the REIPPPP and exclude renewable energy power generated
and connected to the grid by IPPs for private offtake. Coal remains
dominant in the electricity mix as the most significant contributor of
installed generation capacity.

South Africa by 2030. It envisions a transformed energy sector that
supports economic growth, promotes social equity, and ensures envi-
ronmental sustainability (South Africa National Planning Commission,
2012). To achieve this vision, in 2019, the Department of Mineral

South Africa’s renewable energy procurement
South Africa strives to achieve a diversified energy mix that reduces
its reliance on coal as the dominant energy source. Renewable energy
offers a significant opportunity to diversify the energy landscape while
reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. In addition to being more
environmentally sustainable, renewable energy sources are increasingly
cost-effective than traditional conventional energy, making them a
viable solution for the country’s energy transition (Müller & Claar,
2021).
South Africa’s energy transition journey began in 2003 and has
steadily gained momentum over the years (Müller & Claar, 2021). The
diversification of the country’s energy mix through the integration of
renewable energy technologies is primarily driven by the Renewable
Energy Independent Power Producer Procurement Programme. Estab-
lished in 2011 by the Department of Mineral Resources and Energy,
REIPPPP is a competitive tender process designed to attract private in-
vestment for utility-scale, grid-connected renewable energy projects
(Eberhard & Naude, 2016). South Africa was one of the first African
countries to implement this type of procurement program for utility-
scale renewable energy based on auctioning for IPP (Kitzing et al.,
2022). The program is managed by the Independent Power Producer
Office (IPPO) on behalf of the DMRE and its associates (Eberhard &
Naude, 2016). In 2014, electricity from renewable energy projects under
REIPPPP was fed into the national grid for the first time (Wlokas, 2017),
marking a significant milestone in South Africa's transition to cleaner
energy sources.
As of March 2025, South Africa has completed nine renewable en-
ergy procurement rounds, known as Bid Windows (BW), under the
REIPPPP, with one round failing to reach commercial close. In each Bid
Window, IPPs compete to supply energy using various technologies,
including solar PV, concentrated solar power, wind, biomass, biogas,
and landfill gas. Successful bidders are required to generate and sell the
electricity to the state utility, Eskom, under a standard, non-negotiable
20-year Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) (Ayamolowo et al., 2022).

REIPPPP has been widely regarded as a well-designed, well-
managed, and transparent procurement process (Kitzing et al., 2022). It
has played a crucial role in unlocking South Africa's renewable energy
potential and increasing access to cleaner and more affordable energy
(Müller & Claar, 2021). Beyond its contribution to energy supply secu-
rity, REIPPPP is also structured to achieve socio-economic development
goals, including job creation, social upliftment, skills development, and
the growth of local industries (Eberhard & Naude, 2016). However,
delays in signing Power Purchase Agreements and releasing new bid
windows have created instability in the programme. This has hindered
the smooth integration of renewable energy into South Africa’s energy
mix and led to uneven investment by IPPs (Esser & Champion, 2021). To
ensure the long-term success of grid-connected renewable energy in-
vestments in South Africa through 2030 and beyond, it is crucial to
evaluate and refine the current procurement model. Addressing these
challenges will be vital for fostering a more stable and effective energy
transition.
Connection to the theoretical framework
The theoretical framework is one of the most compelling aspects of
the research process (Grant & Osanloo, 2014). It provides the foundation
for the research study, enhancing its credibility (Adom et al., 2018). It
serves as a basis for the literature review and analysis methods, mapping
a clear vision of the research structure (Grant & Osanloo, 2014). It is
derived from established and validated theories and relates to the
research study's hypothesis (Adom et al., 2018).
There are various theoretical frameworks within the sustainability
transition literature (Sovacool et al., 2021). This research is grounded in
the literature on energy transition, diffusion, and innovation to evaluate
the barriers that South Africa faces in its transition to the widespread
adoption of renewable energy technologies. The study adopts the
AKTESP framework to analyse the barriers hindering the renewable
energy transition in South Africa. AKTESP is an acronym for Agreement
Knowledge Technological Economic Social Political. It is a theoretical
model developed by Trudgill in 1990 to understand the nature and
impact of barriers, enabling the prioritization of those that need to be
addressed first (Richards et al., 2012). According to Trudgill, achieving
environmental solutions may be impossible if a barrier exists within any
of these AKTESP categories. Each category contains multiple aspects,
which can overlap, and the barriers need not occur in a sequential or
orderly manner, as depicted in Fig. 4 (Mercer et al., 2017). In analysing
these barriers, the framework evaluates the extent to which each
category of the AKTESP impedes progress toward the solution (Chin
Haw et al., 2008).
Richards et al., (Richards et al., 2012) applied the AKTESP frame-
work to assess the barriers to wind energy development, and in their
findings, they assert that the barriers to developing renewable energy
are complex and multi-dimensional. Similarly, Mercer et al., in their
study conducted in Canada using the AKTESP framework, emphasized
that no single barrier exists; instead, multiple challenges interact and
overlap (Mercer et al., 2017). This highlights the need for a multi-
dimensional assessment of these barriers to identify the most signifi-
cant underlying factors and to understand their interrelationships and
influences on one another (Richards et al., 2012).
Given its focus on assessing barriers from a multi-dimensional
perspective, Trudgill’s AKTESP framework has been selected as the
most suitable framework for this research. Barriers to renewable energy
are complex and multifaceted. As such, understanding their root causes
is essential for generating valuable insights that can guide the prioriti-
sation and development of effective strategies to address them (Richards
et al., 2012). However, Trudgill’s framework does not account for the
analysis of barriers across multiple levels. To address this limitation, this
study modifies the original framework by introducing a two-level
structure: barrier category based on the AKTESP classification and
sub-barrier category, with an explanation of each sub-barrier category.
The framework is further modified by adding additional barriers that do
not form part of the AKTESP category and classifying them as “Others”.
This approach enables a clearer and more in-depth exploration of the
multidimensional barriers and their underlying causes. Lastly, the
framework allows for policy interventions to be formulated targeting
each barrier category.
Application of AKTESP Framework
The application of AKTESP has proven effective in explaining various
environmental challenges and suggesting potential solutions (Mercer
et al., 2017; Richards et al., 2012). Many researchers have applied
Trudgill’s theory across various research fields to investigate barriers to
achieving a better environment (Abdullah et al., 2021). For example,
Richards et al., applied the AKTESP theoretical framework to assess
stakeholders’ perceptions of barriers to wind energy development in
Canada. Their results revealed that the most significant renewable en-
ergy barriers were primarily related to technological and political fac-
tors. The study concluded that renewable energy barriers cannot be
understood through a single factor (such as social, economic, or

emphasized that the cost of renewable energy technology is the most

Mercer et al., applied Trudgill’s AKTESP framework to comprehen-

and Labrador provinces of Canada. Interviews were structured accord-
ing to the six categories of the AKTESP framework and conducted with
industry experts. Their study identified political and economic barriers
as the most significant. Technology and social barriers were found to be
the least significant. The study concluded that many renewable energy
barriers extend beyond the technical category, emphasizing the impor-
tance of addressing political, economic, and other factors in the transi-
tion to renewable energy (Mercer et al., 2017).
Based on these studies, there is no single barrier to renewable energy
development. The challenges to RE development extend beyond tech-
nical issues, encompassing a range of social, political, and economic
factors. Therefore, assessing these barriers requires a multi-disciplinary
approach that incorporates broader social science perspectives, ensuring
a more comprehensive understanding of the factors hindering the
transition to renewable energy (Mercer et al., 2017).
Barriers to renewable energy adapted to the AKTESP framework
The barriers presented in Table 1 were identified through a sys-
tematic literature review (SLR) of recent and relevant studies sourced
from reputable academic databases, specifically Scopus and Science-
Direct. The barriers were categorized using the modified AKTESP
theoretical framework at two levels and include a description of each
sub-barrier. In addition to the AKTESP category, two other barrier cat-
egories were identified and categorized as “Others.” These categories
include Market, and Geographical and Environmental. The barriers and
their descriptions are also presented in Table 1. It is important to note
that the classification of barriers and sub-barriers into these categories is
not fixed, and some barriers or sub-barriers may be relevant to more
than one category (Painuly, 2001).
Methodology
Data collection and sampling
This study employs semi-structured interviews to gather data from
South African renewable energy industry experts. The interview ques-
tions were developed based on the categories of the AKTESP theoretical
framework and included both closed and open-ended questions. The
study targeted a sample size of 15 experts, sampled using non-
probability purposive expert sampling and recruited from the private
and public sectors through the public social media platform LinkedIn
and the researcher’s professional network. The selection criteria
included a minimum of three years of working experience in the wind or
solar energy sector, ensuring that participants have substantial expertise
in the field. The interviews were conducted via Microsoft Teams, digi-
tally recorded, and concurrently transcribed manually by the researcher.
According to (Hennink & Kaiser, 2022), the adequacy of sample size
is best evaluated by determining saturation. Data saturation is the
theoretical guiding principle used to assess if sufficient data has been
collected in qualitative research (Guest et al., 2020). According to the
research conducted by Guest et al., a sample of 12 interviews is sufficient
to reach saturation (Guest et al., 2020). While Hennink and Kaiser
concluded that saturation is typically achieved by conducting interviews
with 9 to 17 participants (Hennink & Kaiser, 2022). A study by (Mercer
et al., 2017) examined barriers to wind energy development in Canada;

Data analysis method
The data was analysed using the content analysis method, a widely
applied research technique across various fields for identifying patterns
in written communication (Lock & Seele, 2017). Content analysis can be
qualitative, focusing on interpreting and understanding words, or
quantitative, emphasizing counting and measuring words (Lock & Seele,
2017). For this study, to analyse the closed-ended questions, the quan-
titative content analysis method, based on frequency, was chosen for its
robustness, ability to minimize bias, and efficiency in data handling.
Microsoft Excel was used as the analysis tool to quantify how often
participants identified specific barriers relevant to South Africa’s
renewable energy industry. To examine responses to the open-ended
questions, qualitative content analysis was employed, using NVIVO
software to analyse, organise, manage, and code the data. The research
design methodology employed in this study is depicted in Fig. 5.
Reliability and validity analysis
To ensure the reliability and consistency of the data, the interview
protocol was standardized, with a uniform set of questions posed to all
participants, informed by the modified theoretical framework. To
enhance the credibility of the findings, interview results were triangu-
lated with insights from the existing literature, allowing for a compari-

Findings
Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 15 industry experts
to gain a deeper understanding of the barriers hindering renewable
energy adoption, focusing on the wind and solar PV sectors in South
Africa. Ten respondents consented to being digitally recorded, while five
declined due to company policies. The interviews were conducted be-
tween September and November 2024. Data from these interviews were
analysed using quantitative and qualitative content analysis to catego-
rize and analyse the barriers discussed by the participants.
Data saturation was conducted to check for repeated themes in the
data. As shown in Fig. 6, no new themes emerged after the 9th partici-
pant, leading to the conclusion that the data collected was sufficient for
analysis.

Content analysis
Barriers impeding RE integration
Data analysis was performed using quantitative content analysis by
frequency to evaluate the responses to the closed-ended questions. The
analysis involved counting the number of times participants agreed that
each barrier applies to the South African context. The barriers were
assessed based on Trudgill’s AKTESP theoretical framework, with the
addition of two other categories: Market, and Geographical and
Environmental.
Each barrier category was discussed in detail to understand how it
impedes the development of renewable energy projects in South Africa.
The frequency and percentage distribution of responses, indicating how
many participants agreed or confirmed the existence of each barrier, are
presented in this section.
Illustrated in Fig. 8 is an overview of the participants' responses,
highlighting their perceptions of the most significant barriers to
renewable energy adoption in South Africa. Most participants (67%)
identified the agreement category as the most significant barrier, fol-
lowed closely by social at 62% and technological challenges at 61%. In
contrast, only 36% of participants considered economic and knowledge
as significant obstacles. This visual summary underscores the relative
importance of these barriers and sheds light on the key challenges
impeding South Africa's renewable energy transition. Interestingly,
these findings differ from the studies conducted in Canada (Mercer et al.,
2017; Richards et al., 2012), where political, economic, and techno-
logical factors were identified as the most significant barriers. Similarly,
in Malaysia, economic, knowledge (awareness), and political factors
were ranked among the most critical challenges (Kardooni et al., 2015;
Sulaiman et al., 2014).

c. Technological Barriers
The Technology category assesses the availability of necessary tech-
nology to address renewable energy challenges. In South Africa, while
the country does not have domestic technology to deploy renewable
energy projects, it relies on importing technological solutions and
expertise from foreign countries. This reliance on external technology,
including adherence to international standards and codes, has not posed
a significant barrier to the development and construction of RE projects.
This contrasts with the findings of the study by (Kardooni et al., 2015),
where most respondents cited the lack of cutting-edge technology and
technical standards as the most significant technological challenges in
Malaysia.
According to the participants, South Africa faces a shortage of local
infrastructure, manufacturing capabilities, training programs, and
research and development facilities. However, while these limitations
exist, they do not significantly hinder RE progress, as the necessary
components and knowledge are sourced from abroad. A key concern
highlighted by most respondents is the intermittency of renewable en-
ergy sources, emphasizing the need for large-scale development of bat-
tery energy storage systems to address this issue. This aligns with
findings from studies by (Bahour et al., 2021; Mercer et al., 2017;
Richards et al., 2012), where the intermittency of RE sources was
similarly identified as a major drawback in the development of these
energy solutions.
The most significant barrier identified, as shown in Table 5, is the
lack of sufficient grid capacity to connect renewable energy projects. A
senior Manager in the greenfield development sector emphasized this
challenge, stating, “Eskom’s process to access the grid, particularly for
private projects, is one of the biggest barriers.” Similarly, a participant
from the IPP space noted, “The grid issue is massive in South Africa, and
it is forcing the developers to look for projects in other land.” This issue
is particularly acute in the Cape provinces, where wind and solar re-
sources are more abundant, but the infrastructure to accommodate such
projects is insufficient. Addressing grid capacity constraints is essential
for unlocking the full potential of renewable energy in these resource-
rich regions.
d. Economic Barriers
The Economic category evaluates the economic feasibility of inte-
grating renewable energy projects into South Africa’s energy system.
According to the participants, South Africa has sufficient funding
mechanisms in place to support the development of renewable energy
projects. However, the main challenge lies in the accessibility of these
funds. This concern was highlighted by participants in technical and
senior management roles, who noted, “The funding is adequate but not
easily accessible.”
Renewable energy project development requires significant capital
investment, as shown in Table 6, this is a barrier to developing these
projects in South Africa. Although there is a lack of specific financing
mechanisms, such as targeted tax incentives, grants, and subsidies, their
absence has not entirely hindered the progress of RE projects. One
participant with project management experience asserted, “RE projects
do not need subsidies because they are competitive.”
These findings contrast with previous research, which identified
economic challenges, particularly the lack of subsidies, financial
mechanisms, and cost-related barriers, as major impediments to inte-
grating RE into the energy system (Bahour et al., 2021; Kardooni et al.,
2015; Richards et al., 2012).
It is clear from the findings of this research study that improving
access to existing funding could further accelerate the development and
integration of renewable energy projects into South Africa's energy mix.

participants pointed out that public awareness of renewable energy

f. Political Category
The Political category evaluates whether adequate political and
policy processes exist to facilitate renewable energy development in
South Africa. The analysis highlighted that although the South African
government is committed to supporting renewable energy projects, lack
of policy continuity due to frequent changes in government leadership
and ministers poses significant challenges to their progress. As indicated
in Table 8, all participants (100%) emphasized the lack of policy con-
tinuity, particularly highlighting the delays experienced during the BW
4 procurement round of the REIPPPP.
A private-sector participant in project development described the
issue: “There was a political pushback from the Minister of Energy and
Eskom management in 2014-2019; no RE projects were built, and this
led to significant delays in these projects.” Another participant echoed
this concern, stating: “There was a very long haul of signing financial
close documentation because of a change in the Ministry, which delayed
the project. It takes longer for a new minister to get up to speed with
what was agreed upon and sign certain documents.”
These challenges underscore the importance of consistent political
support for the successful implementation and continuity of renewable
energy projects in South Africa. These findings align with the literature,
which also highlights that change in political leadership (Alyamani
et al., 2024; Ghimire & Kim, 2018) and lack of political support mech-
anisms (Akusta et al., 2025) are significant barriers to RE projects in
various regions.
Additionally, corruption and nepotism are often highlighted in the
literature (Pathak et al., 2022; Shah & Longsheng, 2022) as significant
barriers to renewable energy development. However, the findings of this
study show that these barriers are not substantial concerns within South
Africa's renewable energy industry, particularly in the REIPPPP. Ac-
cording to the participants, the program is recognized as a transparent
and well-managed process, with projects awarded based on merit.
While previous research has highlighted the lack of comprehensive
policy as a common barrier to RE development globally (Kardooni et al.,
2015; Mercer et al., 2017; Shah and Longsheng, 2022), the findings in
Table
8 reveal a different perspective.
All participants (100%)
acknowledged that South Africa has a robust policy framework sup-
porting renewable energy deployment. However, significant delays in
the implementation of these policies remain a critical challenge, hin-
dering the effective progress of renewable energy projects. An IPP
participant noted this issue, stating: “Delays are incurred mainly by poor
government execution of policy and slow implementation, as well as a
lack of adequate planning and resourcing for the industry for the future.”
The inefficient and bureaucratic permitting process emerged as a
significant barrier to renewable energy development in South Africa.
Project development is often delayed by lengthy processes involving
multiple requirements and licenses to ensure compliance with envi-
ronmental and legislative standards. A public sector participant high-
lighted this challenge, stating: “There are several channels to go
through, and the processes take long, which becomes a challenge.”
These findings contrast with the studies by Asante et al., (Asante et al.,
2020; Asante et al., 2022), where bureaucratic permit procedures were
identified as the least significant barrier to renewable energy adoption.
This discrepancy suggests that the impact of permitting inefficiencies
may vary across regions, with South Africa facing more substantial de-
lays due to complex regulatory processes.

logistics and construction. Furthermore, much of this land is publicly
owned, and acquiring usage rights is frequently challenging or impos-

participants highlighted these challenges, and one private-sector

renewable energy projects in South Africa. These findings contrast with
the literature, where geographic location (Asante et al., 2022) and land

ered among the least significant barriers to renewable energy develop-
ment. Furthermore, while studies have pointed the handling and cost of
waste disposal during the construction of renewable energy projects as
barriers (Mehmood et al., 2022; Pathak et al., 2022) these issues do not
seem to be significant concerns in South Africa.
Discussion
This section interprets the findings of this research through the lens
of the AKTESP framework, exploring the broader theoretical implica-
tions for renewable energy integration in South Africa. The study cat-
egorised and analysed renewable energy barriers faced by Independent
Power Producers in South Africa into six categories: Agreement,
Knowledge, Technological, Economic, Social, and Political. The findings
of the interview and triangulation with the literature reveal the com-
plex, multidisciplinary, and interrelated barriers that collectively hinder
the integration of renewable energy into the energy system (Mercer
et al., 2017).
As previously noted in Section 3, these categories of barriers are not
mutually exclusive and do not necessarily exist in a linear sequence.
Instead, they are often interlinked and overlapping, underpinning one
another in complex ways. For instance, the agreement barrier, which
emerged as a dominant challenge in this study, is rooted in both political
and knowledge-related barriers, and its impacts cascade into economic
and technological barriers. This was apparent when the lack of agree-
ment between stakeholders (Eskom and the government) resulted in the
delay of signing PPAs for the BW 4 projects (Kruger et al., 2021). These
disruptions, rooted in a lack of policy continuity and coordination be-
tween stakeholders, significantly eroded investor confidence, limiting
access to financing mechanisms and prompting several technology
providers to withdraw from the South African market.
The results also highlighted a range of technological barriers such as
lack of grid connection, lack of skilled labour, insufficient R&D capacity,
intermittent
energy
supply,
the
absence
of
local
servicing
and
manufacturing facilities, and shortage of training institutions, which
continue to impede large-scale integration of renewable energy in South
Africa. These insufficiencies not only hinder project implementation but
also increase reliance on foreign technologies and expertise, reducing
the long-term sustainability of renewable initiatives.
Social-related challenges emerged as the second most prominent
barrier category. Factors such as limited public awareness and lack of
public acceptance highlight the critical role of community engagement
in the development of renewable energy projects. Public interference
can significantly hinder the development of these projects even when
technological and economic conditions are viable.
Collectively, these findings reinforce the theoretical argument that a
narrow focus on technical or economic constraints is insufficient to
understand the dynamics of energy transitions. The AKTESP framework
proved effective in providing a holistic approach through which barriers
could be examined in an integrated manner. The summary of the find-
ings, theoretical and empirical implications, and novel contributions is
presented in Table 11.
In addition to validating the relevance of the AKTESP framework
within the South African context, this study also extends the theoretical
model by incorporating two additional barrier categories: market-
related and geographical/environmental factors. These included bar-
riers such as market uncertainties, an underdeveloped supply chain
largely due to a high dependency on imported components, as well as
challenges related to geographical distance, land requirements, and
transport problems. This extension highlights that the challenges facing
renewable energy adoption in South Africa cannot be explained solely
by agreement, knowledge, technical, economic, social, or political fac-
tors (Richards et al., 2012).
These findings emphasise the fact that South Africa’s renewable
energy barriers cannot be explained by one factor in isolation. Instead,
they form a reinforcing web where shortcomings in one category
intensify weaknesses in the others, collectively hindering the integration
of renewable energy into the grid.
Overcoming these barriers requires a coordinated and multi-
dimensional approach that tackles issues concurrently across the
different categories, addressing agreement, knowledge, technology,
economic, social, political (AKTESP), as well as market, environmental,
and geographical challenges. Therefore, unlocking South Africa’s
renewable potential requires collaboration across multiple institutions,
aligning energy, environmental, and social policies within a transparent,
long-term framework.

Despite its abundance of renewable energy sources, South Africa
remains heavily dependent on fossil fuels for economic growth and
electricity generation. Renewable energy development has the potential

to significantly transform the future of the country’s power generation

journey toward renewable energy integration into the national grid is
impeded by various barriers that delay procurement, construction, and
commercialization processes.
This study adopted Trudgill’s AKTESP theoretical framework to
categorize and analyse the barriers to integrating renewable energy into
South Africa’s energy mix. Interviews with industry experts provided
valuable insights into the country's specific challenges. The findings
highlighted agreement, social, and technological categories as the most
significant. Key obstacles include a lack of policy continuity, a lack of
public awareness and acceptance, and grid connection challenges. The
lack of coordination between key institutions responsible for renewable
energy initiatives also contributes significantly to project stagnation.
Conversely, economic and knowledge categories were identified as less
prominent in the South African context. The country benefits from
comprehensive policies, knowledgeable stakeholders, and an attractive
investment for renewable energy projects despite the long return-on-
investment period.
This study enhances the theoretical understanding of the barriers to
renewable energy adoption in South Africa. It offers a comprehensive
framework for identifying and categorising these barriers, which can
serve as a foundation for future research in the renewable energy tran-
sition. From a managerial perspective, the findings of this research
provide valuable insights for stakeholders, enabling them to develop
strategic plans and policies aimed at overcoming the identified barriers.
These contributions can guide decision-makers in navigating the chal-
lenges of renewable energy implementation, ensuring more effective
and sustainable energy transitions in South Africa. However, the study
has certain limitations that must be acknowledged. Renewable energy
barriers are complex and multi-dimensional, and their assessment and
prioritization require a holistic and integrated approach. While this
research provided a structured categorization of barriers using the
AKTESP framework, it did not fully explore the underlying relative
weight of each barrier. Therefore, future research should utilise multi-
criteria decision-making techniques to systematically prioritize and
address barriers based on their interdependencies. Secondly, the sig-
nificant influence of social barriers, particularly the lack of public
awareness and acceptance, underscores the need to extend existing
theoretical models to account for behavioural and cultural dimensions,
which are often underexplored in energy transition literature.
The study concludes by outlining the following policy implications,
which may also be applicable to other regions experiencing challenges
similar to those faced by South Africa:
• Addressing renewable energy challenges requires transparency,
consensus, and collaboration among stakeholders from both the
public and private sectors. This approach ensures that decisions are
inclusive, well-informed, and beneficial to all parties, ultimately
accelerating the integration of renewable energy.
• Promoting knowledge sharing through investments in education and
capacity building, fostering partnerships between academic in-
stitutions, industry experts, and government agencies, will ensure
that the renewable energy sector is well-equipped with the expertise
and skills necessary for successful project implementation.
• Investing in grid infrastructure, R&D facilities, technological ad-
vancements, and technical skills development will facilitate the
seamless integration of renewable energy projects into the energy
system.
• Establishing dedicated financial institutions that provide low-
interest financing, credit guarantees, and targeted subsidies will

• Social barriers can be effectively addressed by prioritizing early
public consultation during the project development process. This
approach will increase public awareness, foster positive attitudes,
reduce public resistance, and enhance community acceptance of
renewable energy projects.
• Addressing political barriers requires the establishment of robust and
long-term strategies to ensure consistent government support and
minimize policy discontinuity. Cross-party agreements should be
pursued to protect renewable energy projects from leadership

• Strengthening local manufacturing capacity for renewable energy
