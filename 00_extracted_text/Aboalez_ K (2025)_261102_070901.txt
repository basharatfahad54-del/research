1. Introduction 
The relationship between engineering and social justice is a 
scholarly area gaining much attention, especially among energy 
engineers and educators. Jen Schneider [1] mentions that 
historians Noble and Layton have “considered how the 
engineering profession has been constituted such that it exists 
largely in opposition to the values and practices of social 
justice” (p.1). Although engineers, in their design objectives, 
always want to achieve social benefits for the well-being of 
society, the end products do not always serve the social justice 
values in reliable and uncontested ways. Many engineering 
professionals adopt an ideology of depoliticisation of 
technology, which means that cultural and social values or 
inequality are considered “political” and thus irrelevant—
perhaps even risky—to technical engineering work [2]. Social 
justice in engineering education is divisive due to different 
institutional and political contexts. According to Lucena [3], the 
two typical perspectives of engineering educators and students 
on social justice are to consider it to fall outside the technical 
curriculum or to place it in a complementary social science 
course. Lucena [3] proposes a crucial question: “How might 
engineering educators, students and practitioners begin 
integrating these two fields of practice in the classroom, the lab, 
in the fieldwork, in conferences, and other spaces of scholarly 
and other pedagogical activity, in a way that results in more just 
technologies?” (p.3). There is an urgent need to address 
important 
questions 
about 
inequalities, 
discrimination, 
community engagement, and decolonisation, previously 
invisible in the engineering curriculum.
Researchers in 
engineering education for social justice investigate pedagogies 
that will assist engineering students in applying social justice 
values in their profession by understanding the conflicts and 
contestations between social justice and engineering to develop 
combined values. Traditional engineering education aims at 
graduating engineers who act unilaterally to achieve what they 
consider to be an ideal engineering design and implementation, 
irrespective of the socio-environmental impacts [4]. Nieusma &

1. Abstract
The energy sector worldwide is continuously growing to provide sustainable energy solutions employing renewable energy resources. This 
transition has benefits such as reduced emissions from traditional fossil fuel power plants and economic and technological opportunities. Low-
carbon energy projects' financial and environmental sustainability based on resources such as wind and solar systems are carefully assessed. 
However, these energy projects usually need more assessment of the social impacts that may lead to social energy injustice. Therefore, there is a 
need for a social life cycle assessment to determine the potential social effects on local communities where these projects are implemented. This 
assessment will rely on developing consciousness and concern for social justice issues related to energy engineering, starting from the engineering 
education phase, to help build a provision for a just, sustainable, and inclusive energy transition. For this aim, this paper will review the 
relationship between energy engineering and social justice and investigate a sustainable design approach based on critical post-humanism 
principles that consider the conflicts and contestations between social justice and energy transition.

2. Social justice in the engineering context 
This section discusses social justice in engineering, focusing 
on energy engineering. 
2.1. Social justice in engineering
Leydens, Lucena, & Schneider [7] define engineering as 
“educational and professional practices aimed at developing, 
implementing, and improving technologies for a specific 
purpose” (p.63). They define social justice as “the practices 
aimed at a more equitable distribution of societal advantages or 
disadvantages, or what has more traditionally been called 
rewards (or opportunities) and burdens (or harms)” (p.63). The 
authors assume that there are intersections between engineering 
and structural functionalism (SF), which is a theoretical
framework that observes society as a complex network whose 
elements are working in harmony to ensure the stability of this 
network. This resonates with critical posthumanism theory, 
which requires an analysis of relations and entanglements 
between human and non-human actors. This will develop a 
broader understanding of the relationships between technology 
and society. In a similar description, Leydens, Lucena, & 
Nieusma [8] describe social justice in engineering as: 
“engineering practices that strive towards an equitable 
distribution of opportunities and resources to enhance human 
capabilities while reducing imposed risks and harms among 
citizens of the society” (p.1). 
Despite these descriptions of social justice in engineering, 
engineering academic institutions are conservative regarding 
social justice issues [9]. In such an environment, Riley [9]
argues that “anyone who wasn’t a wealthy straight white, able-
bodied male was bound to encounter obstacles” (p.42).  In 
contracts to this humanistic view, Braidotti [10] argues that 
critical posthumanist ethics provide an alternative view of the 
world in contrast to that of humanism. Braidotti suggests that 
the humanist view promotes the idea of human superiority and 
dominion at the expense of any other. This humanist view of 
the engineering profession shows that “the profession of 
engineering has historically served the status quo, feeding an 
ever-expanding materialistic and militaristic culture” [11: iv]. 
This culture is seen as capitalist, where “capitalism, through its 
production process, produces an awesome schizophrenic 
accumulation of energy or charge, against which it brings all its 
vast powers of repression to bear, but which nonetheless, 
continues to act as capitalism's limit.” [12].
2.2. Energy engineering and social justice 
Looking at energy engineering projects, it is evident that 
they helped improve energy security for increased industrial 
production [13]. However, the growing implementation of 
these 
projects 
has 
caused 
both 
environmental 
and 
socioeconomic impacts.  Energy engineering education often 
emphasises teaching optimum design and planning of energy 
projects for power system reliability and security. Attention is 
paid to such constraining factors as the social acceptance of 
local communities [14]-[16]. Local communities often suffer 
violent disruptions in their social, economic, and cultural 
activities due to unequal power dynamics between them and 
energy companies [17]. Forlano [18] argues that the outcomes 
of engineering training are based on market approaches, which 
often disregard the needs of specific communities in favour of 
others. Thus, the existing social inequalities are exacerbated. 
Lucena [3] describes the risky interventions to integrate social 
justice into engineering education and practice. These 
interventions can be seen as “micropolitical instances of 
activism” [19: p.269] by educators/engineers in a profession 
focusing primarily on the design and implementation rather 
than the social and environmental impacts. Braidotti [19]
explained these interventions as a challenging mission because 
“This desire for change clashes constitutionally with the 
guardians of the status quo: the judges and managers of truths 
and the clarity fetishists” (p.268).

3.2. Using social justice as a critical lens for looking at 
engineering education and practice  
Winberg & Winberg [22] draw on Nancy Fraser’s justice 
theory to construct a decolonised socially just engineering 
curriculum by getting students to engage with social justice 
principles and critique the current curriculum. Fraser’s model 
of social justice assists students in understanding collective 
human activity through interactions between humans and their 
social, economic, and cultural contexts to develop innovative 
engineering solutions for local community needs. The notion 
of “interaction” assumes the prior existence of independent 
entities or “relata”, whereas posthumanism presents an 
insightful conceptual shift through the idea of “intra-action”
[23]. Barad [23] explains that “It is through specific agential 
intra-actions that the boundaries and properties of the 
components of phenomena become determinate and that 
particular concepts become meaningful.” (p.139). This intra-
action can be observed through an adapted phenomenographic 
framework explaining how students (from engineering and 
social science) in cross-disciplinary courses on engineering and 
social justice move toward a conception of social justice as a 
lens for deconstruction and critical analysis of the injustices 
brought by engineering practices [24]. Therefore, “the task of 
social justice must move beyond the human and humanist 
traditions” [25: p.145].  Ellis [25: p.140] further explains that:
A concern for social justice, therefore, commits us to attend 
to how the human/nonhuman binary not only functions to 
privilege Homo sapiens above other animal species but is 
simultaneously deployed to differentiate Homo sapiens into 
different categories of legal and social protection. 
One possible approach to consider social justice concerns in 
engineering education is through an emerging model for 
educating engineers on new literacies that target social justice 
aspects in the engineering curriculum [26]. The aim is to 
develop engineering students with high levels of technical 
competency and a critical awareness of social justice through 
reflective thinking. In this approach, the task of educators is to 
guide their students through engagement via a range of literacy 
activities. The approach of Cumming-Potvin& Currie is 
anthropocentric when it addresses the social injustices related 
to industrialisation and related engineering practices. This 
approach assumes that humans are the cause and the effect of 
social injustices, while other non-human factors (environment 
and technologies) don’t play any role. In contradiction, Forlano
[18] argues that human and non-human actors share equal 
agency in shaping world issues such as social injustices. 
Therefore, Forlano explains that climate change and 
environmental sustainability have become widely recognised
as significant, pressing concerns related to social justice in 
communities affected by industrialisation. 
4. Increasing 
social 
justice 
awareness 
for
energy 
engineering through community engagement
As an example of the work done concerning community 
engagement in energy engineering projects to increase the 
social awareness of engineers, Gross [27] studied a rural 
community where a wind farm was being built. Justice theory 
is used as a lens to assist students in comprehending how 
different groups of a community are affected by the project. 
The author develops the “community fairness framework” 
based on the analysis of different community member’s
fairness perceptions of the development. Approaching 
environmental justice issues by considering how they are also 
social justice issues allows engineering students to connect 
human actions to human impacts when discussing energy needs 
and environmental impacts. The ecological implications for 
social justice are unlimited, as examining nonhuman actors’ 
impacts on humans reveals social injustice and ways to remedy 
it [28]. For example, Coole & Frost [28] suggested that 
examining the root cause of high school drop-out rates and high 
crime rates in poor areas, including nonhuman agents such as 
air pollution, might lead to better results than increasing the 
police force or imposing harsher penalties.
5. Critical Posthumanism framework for social justice in
energy projects
We can conclude from the previously discussed issues that
the energy engineering lecturer, the energy project designer, 
and the project manager are the agents responsible for 
transformative actions toward a socially just energy transition 
that considers social and environmental justice principles when 
designing and implementing energy projects.
Social justice concepts based on different theories see the 
integration of social justice in engineering education and 
practice
as a reaction to inequalities and protests by 
disadvantaged groups and communities, where engineering 
education and practice are based on a neoliberal order [28]. It 
is unsustainable since it benefits a small minority at the cost of 
most others, both human and nonhuman. This is echoed by 
critical posthumanism as a paradigm shift towards a necessary
and deconstructive stance in how we view ourselves, 
enlightened by recognising
the past while planning a 
comprehensive and generative standpoint to create options for 
the present and the future [29]. This posthumanist view 
emphasises that learning is not only limited to the learner but 
also a relational process in human/more-than-human relations.

5.1. Key theoretical principles of critical posthumanism
Braidotti [10] Believes that critical posthumanism can assist 
in producing socially relevant knowledge “that is attuned to 
basic principles of social justice, the respect for human decency 
and diversity, the rejection of false universalisms; the 
affirmation of the positivity of difference; the principles of 
academic freedom, anti-racism, openness to others” (p.11). to 
explain science's role in crafting the posthuman notion, 
Braidotti affirms that scientific achievements on digital 'second 
life', genetically modified food, advanced prosthetics, robotics 
and reproductive technologies are familiar facets of our 
globally linked and technologically mediated societies. This 
has blurred the traditional distinction between humans and 
others, exposing the non-naturalistic structure of humans.
The fundamentals of critical posthumanism’s new ethics of 
engagement, as described by Radomska [30], are overcoming 
humanist anthropocentrism, considering nonhuman others, and 
affirming differences. Critical posthumanism tries to counter 
dualisms between humans and nonhumans by developing a 
new and optimistic perceptive of difference leading to justice
[31]. Ulmer [32] suggested that “justice involves more than 
what can be found solely within the realm of human relations. 
Rather, justice is also material, ecological, geographical, 
geological, geopolitical, and geo-philosophical.” (p.2). 
Consequently, “As humans become more entangled in intricate 
relationships with technology and science, with other animals 
and the environment, notions of the human, along with various 
humanisms and anthropocentric approaches, have become 
difficult to uphold” [33: p. 11]. Braidotti [10] explains that: 
Critical posthumanism theory can be utilised as both a 
genealogical and navigational tool or, more specifically, it can 
be used as a generative tool to help us re-think the basic unit of 
reference for the human in the bio-genetic age known as the 
‘Anthropocene’. (p.5). The fundamental theoretical principles 
of critical posthumanism are shown in Fig.1.

For engineering education and practice, adopting the new 
ethics of engagement would allow engineering students to 
prioritise issues linked to social justice, ethical accountability, 
sustainability, and intergenerational solidarity [10] and [34]. To 
graduate engineering students into socially responsible 
professionals, their education should provide them with 
knowledge and “empower them with multiple perspectives and 
questioning habits” [35: p.102]. This helps develop a suitable 
ethical framework, where engineering education positions 
students to critically view their ‘engineering’ actions' impact 
on the world.
5.2. Posthumanism social justice principles 
Concerning
social
transitions for
energy engineering 
projects, the two dimensions of social justice, as explained by
Gewirtz [36], are distributional justice, referring to the 
principle of fair distribution of different resources, and 
relational justice, which focuses on micro and macro power 
relations in a society. These two dimensions are related to two 
principles of justice: the first relates to social and economic 
inequalities, and the second addresses equal liberty, which 
explains that all people are entitled to the same level of freedom 
within a society irrespective of power relations [37]. These 
dimensions and principles of anthropocentric social justice 
seek to eliminate structural inequalities that lead to unjust 
power imbalances, usually among people—that is, human 
actors. 
The 
Posthumanist 
worldviews 
counter 
these 
anthropocentric theories based on human discourses and 
societal structures [38]. Instead, social justice, as seen through 
the lens of posthumanism, will consider human and nonhuman 
actors, where the human/nonhuman hierarchy flattens, and the 
agency of nonhuman actors and assemblages of actors can be 
explicitly recognised and accounted for [39]. 
In this sense, Bozalek & Carolissen [40] explain social
justice is “the ability to participate as equals and full partners 
in social interaction” (p.13). Besides, Posthumanism could be 
used as a lens to “understanding techno-scientific and other 
natural cultural practices” [23: p. 135]. Barad [23] adds that 
“posthumanism refuses the idea of a natural division between 
nature and culture, calling for an accounting of how this 
boundary is actively configured and reconfigured.” (p.136). 
hence, “posthumanism doesn't presume the separateness of 
anything, let alone the alleged spatial, ontological, and 
epistemological distinction that sets humans apart.” (p.136). 
Therefore, incorporating social justice in engineering education
and practice is a transformative and inclusive action that must 
be located against broader ecological ethics. This is needed 
because the same processes of abuse that harm humans and 
promote social injustices lead to environmental degradation 
and vice versa, especially when building large energy projects, 
whether based on fossil fuel or renewable energy resources.
A critical posthuman pedagogy could be helpful to change 
this view by understanding how we teach global energy issues, 
how we introduce the content on the design and 
implementation of energy projects and what particular topics
need to be discussed about the local communities and the 
environment, and “how students interpret and integrate the

6. Conclusions 
Focusing on engineering standards and neglecting the 
importance of social and cognitive development issues for 
engineers is typical in engineering education and practice. Life 
cycle engineering, which evaluates environmental and social 
impacts from resource extraction to waste management, offers 
a unique opportunity to address these issues in energy systems 
by embedding social justice values into the design and 
decision-making processes. Therefore, there is a need for a 
social life cycle assessment to determine the potential social 
effects on local communities where these projects are 
implemented. This assessment will rely on a posthumanist 
cartographic approach, which harnesses the power of 
posthumanism to be a navigational and analytical tool needed 
in the design, planning, and implementation stages of energy 
engineering projects. Developing and including this approach 
in the engineering curriculum is essential
to build 
consciousness and concerns about social justice issues in 
energy engineering for a just energy transition. Posthumanist 
cartography will be used to critique social justice's absence in 
how we present technical knowledge for energy engineering 
education. The critique will track the origins and development 
of the current prevailing worldview of the engineering 
profession. Posthumanist cartography will allow the mapping 
of power relations and other socio-material factors.
