# 9. Expected Contributions

## 9.1 What the study claims and what it does not

A PhD proposal is expected to state its contribution, and there is a temptation to overstate it. This section tries to be precise. The study claims four contributions, each of which is bounded, and each of which can be assessed against the evidence the study will produce. It does not claim to have discovered a new theory, to have solved the problem of energy poverty in South Africa, or to have produced a technology that will work everywhere.

The contributions are theoretical, methodological, practical and policy-related. They are set out in Table 7.1 with the evidence that will support each one.

## 9.2 Theoretical contribution

The theoretical contribution is the argument that acceptance is an emergent property of a socio-material configuration, and that this claim has consequences for how energy innovation should be designed and evaluated.

The claim is not new in its general form. Practice theory, socio-technical systems theory and actor-network theory have all argued that technology and social order are co-produced. What is new is the specific combination: a posthumanist ontology applied to the design of a community-scale energy system in a South African informal settlement, with the justice literature supplying the normative criteria. The combination produces a set of propositions that can be examined and, importantly, can fail.

The test of the contribution is whether the framework explains something that a TAM study would have missed. The study expects to find at least three such things. First, cases in which a household reports a positive attitude and stops using the system, because a material condition changed: the water supply failed, a part could not be sourced, a landlord raised the rent. Second, cases in which a household reports a negative attitude and continues to use the system, because there is no alternative. Third, cases in which the same technology produces different outcomes in adjacent households with similar incomes, because the configuration differs in a way that an attitude survey cannot detect.

If none of these is found, the contribution is weakened. The study will report that.

## 9.3 Methodological contribution

The methodological contribution is the combination of four analytical practices and the documentation of what each contributes.

Reflexive thematic analysis is well established, but it is usually used alone. Situational mapping is used in grounded theory but rarely in energy research. Causal loop diagramming is used in systems research but rarely with qualitative community data. Diffractive reading is used in posthumanist research but rarely in engineering. The study will report how the four were combined, what each produced, and where they produced incompatible accounts.

The combination is not offered as a template to be copied. It is offered as a documented experiment in method, with an honest account of its costs. Diffractive reading, in particular, is slow and difficult to explain to a reader who is not familiar with the vocabulary. The study will show what it added and what it cost.

The second methodological contribution is the operationalisation of justice. The energy justice literature is rich in principles and thin in indicators. The twelve indicators in Table 4.4 are an attempt to close that gap for a project-scale intervention. They are not the only possible set, and the study will report which ones proved usable and which did not.

## 9.4 Practical contribution

For practitioners, the study offers a design protocol and a body of evidence.

The protocol treats the configuration as the unit of intervention. In practice this means that a project team working on a biogas system would specify the feedstock supply, the water budget, the siting, the maintenance arrangement, the spare-parts route and the governance rules at the same time as the digester volume and the gas yield. The study will produce a template for that specification, derived from the case and tested against it.

The evidence concerns the conditions under which a community-scale biogas system is sustained. The literature contains many accounts of why systems fail and few longitudinal accounts of how a system was maintained, by whom, at what cost and with what problems. The study will produce such an account for one case, with the maintenance record and the cost data.

These outputs are modest, but they are the kind that a project manager can use. A framework that is elegant but unusable is of limited value in engineering management.

## 9.5 Policy contribution

At the policy level, the study speaks to two questions that municipalities currently answer by default.

The first is how to evaluate an energy programme against justice criteria. Municipal Integrated Development Plans routinely state commitments to redress and inclusion, and they rarely specify how those commitments will be assessed. The indicator set in Table 4.4 offers a way of doing so at project scale, with data that a municipality could collect.

The second is how to organise participation so that it is more than consultation. The study will document the arrangements that made participation possible for tenants, women and youth, and the arrangements that did not. That evidence is directly relevant to the design of municipal engagement processes, and to the conditions attached to Just Energy Transition Partnership funding.

The study does not claim that its findings will change national policy. It claims that they provide evidence that is currently missing from a policy conversation that is conducted mostly at national scale.

## 9.6 Contribution to the supervisor's research programme

The study is located within a research programme on gendered energy innovation in poor urban environments, led by the supervisor and colleagues. That programme has produced a gender analysis framework (Anditi et al., 2022), a systems model of energy service security (Batinge et al., 2022), a co-design toolkit (Petrulaityte et al., 2022) and a process for developing energy Living Labs (Mukama et al., 2022). It has also produced a posthumanist argument for a social approach to energy transition in engineering education (Aboalez, 2025).

This study extends that programme in three ways. It moves the posthumanist argument from engineering education to settlement-level design. It adds a justice evaluation that the earlier work identified as necessary but did not operationalise. And it provides a detailed case that can be used to test the programme's frameworks in a new site.

The relationship is a strength and a risk. It is a strength because the study can build on established concepts and access. It is a risk because a study that is too closely aligned with its supervisor's framework may not test it hard enough. The design addresses this through negative case analysis, through the inclusion of a critical friend on the advisory group, and through the explicit statement of what would count as disconfirming evidence.

## 9.7 Contribution to the researcher's development

A doctoral study is also a process of formation, and it is reasonable to state what the researcher expects to learn. The proposal is written by a candidate who is developing competence in three areas: qualitative research in a context of poverty and inequality; the application of posthumanist theory to engineering problems; and the management of a multi-stakeholder design process.

The study is designed to build all three. The fieldwork requires the researcher to work in a language and a setting that are not their own, with the humility that entails. The theoretical work requires a facility with concepts that are not native to engineering. The design work requires the management skills that the study is, in part, about.

None of this belongs in a claim about contribution to knowledge. It belongs in the statement of what the study is for, and it is stated here because it shapes the choices in Section 10.

## 10.2 Phase detail

### 10.2.1 Phase 1: Foundation and ethics (months 1–6)

The first phase establishes the conditions for the study. It includes a literature update to catch work published since the proposal, the development of the interview and workshop instruments, the ethics application and the site scoping.

The ethics application will be submitted in month 3, and the study assumes a three-month review period. If clearance takes longer, the scoping work can continue because it involves no data collection, but the fieldwork start will slip.

The site selection memorandum will be completed by month 6. It will assess at least three candidate settlements against the criteria in Table 5.1 and will state the reasons for the choice.

The deliverables are an approved proposal, an ethics certificate, a set of instruments and a site selection memorandum.

### 10.2.2 Phase 2: Descriptive study I (months 7–14)

The second phase produces the diagnostic account. It begins with key-informant interviews, which establish the institutional context and identify the households and groups to approach. Focus groups follow, then transect walks, then diaries, then photovoice. The sequence is not rigid, but it is deliberate: the earlier methods inform the later ones.

Twelve to fifteen households will keep diaries for four weeks. Ten to twelve residents will take part in photovoice. Twenty to twenty-five household interviews and eight to ten interviews with non-users will be conducted. The exact numbers will be adjusted as saturation is assessed.

Transcription begins in month 9 and continues through the phase. Coding begins in month 10.

The deliverables are a site report, a diagnostic account organised around the ten components, and a validated coding frame.

### 10.2.3 Phase 3: Analysis and modelling (months 15–21)

The third phase consolidates the analysis. It produces the situational maps, the causal loop diagrams and the first revision of the framework. It also produces a draft of the findings chapter, which is submitted to the supervisor for review.

This phase includes a member-checking session with participants, in which the emerging findings are presented and discussed. The session is not a validation exercise in the positivist sense; it is an opportunity for participants to disagree, and the disagreements are recorded.

The deliverables are a findings chapter draft, a set of maps and diagrams, and a revised framework.

### 10.2.4 Phase 4: Co-design and prototyping (months 22–27)

The fourth phase is the design intervention. Four workshops produce the service model, the governance agreement, the technical specification and the evaluation plan. Construction begins after the specification is agreed, and is undertaken by a local contractor with the technology team, using local labour where possible.

Commissioning includes operator training and a maintenance schedule. The phase ends with a working prototype, a signed governance agreement and a trained operator group.

This phase carries the highest risk in the plan. Construction can be delayed by weather, by supply, by permission and by disagreement. A two-month contingency is built into the schedule.

The deliverables are an agreed specification, a working prototype, a governance agreement and a trained operator group.

### 10.2.5 Phase 5: Evaluation and drafting (months 28–33)

The fifth phase assesses the intervention and writes the thesis. Monitoring data are collected over six months: gas output, water use, feedstock input, maintenance events, cost and use by household. Follow-up interviews are conducted with the original participants and with a sample of households that did not take part.

The justice profile is constructed and discussed with participants before it is finalised. The thesis is drafted, and a paper is prepared for a conference or journal.

The deliverables are a justice profile, a full thesis draft and a conference paper.

### 10.2.6 Phase 6: Finalisation (months 34–36)

The final phase completes the thesis, prepares the examination copy and submits. It also includes the exit handover: a community feedback session, the transfer of records and contacts, and the establishment of the maintenance arrangement agreed in Phase 4.

The deliverables are a submitted thesis and a completed handover.

## 10.3 Milestones and decision points

Five decision points are built into the plan. Each is a point at which the study could be adjusted.

*Month 6: site confirmation.* If no site meets the criteria, the study will either relax a criterion with documented reasons or delay the fieldwork by three months while a new site is scoped.

*Month 14: framework revision.* If the diagnostic evidence does not support the framework, the framework is revised before the co-design phase. This is the point at which a major revision is cheapest.

*Month 22: go/no-go on construction.* Construction proceeds only if a governance agreement and a maintenance arrangement are in place. If they are not, the study will run a design-only intervention and report the reasons, which is itself a finding about the conditions for community-scale technology.

*Month 27: prototype acceptance.* If the system does not perform to specification, the study will document the shortfall and analyse it as evidence about the configuration.

*Month 33: submission readiness.* If the draft is incomplete, a three-month extension will be requested.

## 11.2 Notes on the chapters

Three chapters carry the weight of the argument and deserve comment.

Chapter 6 is the diagnostic chapter, and it does the work that the literature review cannot: it establishes what the configuration actually is in this place. It will be organised around the ten components, but it will not follow them mechanically. It will begin with a day in the settlement, then move to the systems that produce the services, then to the points of failure, then to the relations of power that shape who bears the cost.

Chapter 8 is the evaluation chapter, and it is the one most likely to produce uncomfortable findings. It will report the justice profile in full, including the dimensions on which the intervention performed poorly. It will also report the lab's own performance against the same criteria.

Chapter 9 is the discussion, and it is where the theoretical contribution is made. It will return to the six propositions and state which were supported, which were qualified and which were not supported. It will also discuss what the case shows about the limits of the framework.

## 11.3 Publication plan

Three outputs are planned. A conceptual paper on the systemic Living Lab and justice will be prepared for a journal in engineering management or energy research during the second year. A methods paper on combining situational mapping and diffractive reading may follow. The main empirical paper will be prepared after the evaluation.

Authorship will follow the university's policy, with the candidate as first author on papers derived from the thesis and the supervisor as co-author where the contribution warrants it.

## Appendix B: Draft interview guide

The guide is indicative. It will be piloted and revised before use.

**Opening**

- Thank you for making time. This conversation is about how energy is used in your household and what makes it difficult or easy. There are no right answers, and you can stop at any point or decline a question.

**Section 1: Current practice**

- Walk me through yesterday. What did you cook, and what did you use?
- Which fuels do you use in a normal week? Why those?
- Where does the fuel come from, and who fetches or buys it?
- What happens when the electricity goes off?

**Section 2: Cost and labour**

- Roughly how much does energy cost your household in a month? How does that compare with other expenses?
- Who pays, and how is the decision made?
- Who does the cooking, the collecting and the cleaning? Has that changed?

**Section 3: Failure and coping**

- Tell me about a time when the system did not work. What happened?
- What did you do instead? What did it cost?
- Is there anything you have stopped using? Why?

**Section 4: Decisions and voice**

- If a new energy system were installed here, who would decide how it is run?
- Have you been asked about a project before? What happened?
- Who speaks for the community in meetings? Do you feel represented?

**Section 5: Fairness**

- If a new system were installed, who do you think would benefit most? Who might be left out?
- What would make you say the project was fair?
- Is there anything a project like this should not do?

**Closing**

- Is there anything I should have asked and did not?
- Would you be willing to speak again?

## Appendix E: Workshop protocol

Each workshop runs for three hours and follows the same structure.

1. *Welcome and purpose* (15 minutes). Restate what the workshop can and cannot decide.
2. *Ground rules* (10 minutes). Agreed by the group, including how disagreement will be handled.
3. *Evidence presentation* (30 minutes). Findings from the diagnostic phase, presented visually, with time for challenge.
4. *Small-group task* (60 minutes). Groups of five to seven, mixed by gender and tenure where possible, each with a facilitator and a visual template.
5. *Plenary* (45 minutes). Each group presents; disagreements are recorded on a flip chart rather than resolved.
6. *Decision record* (20 minutes). What was decided, what was deferred, who will do what.

A decision log is completed after each workshop. It records the decision, the alternatives considered, who supported and who opposed, and the reasons given. The log is a primary data source for C9 and for the procedural justice indicators.
