ABSTRACT

South Africa's just energy transition is framed nationally by renewable procurement
and grid decarbonisation, yet its outcomes are decided locally, in configurations
where households, fuels, appliances, dwellings, tenure, infrastructure and
institutions interact. In informal settlements these configurations often reproduce
energy poverty, insecure supply and an unequal distribution of costs and benefits.
Community-scale biogas is promoted as a decentralised alternative, but uptake is
uneven, and existing acceptance models treat the technology as given and the user
as an individual chooser, leaving the socio-material conditions of acceptance and
justice unexamined.

This study develops SETILL, a Systemic Living Lab for Just Energy Transition, as a
conceptual and methodological framework for examining how a community-scale biogas
innovation can be designed, accepted and sustained justly in an informal settlement
in Tzaneen, South Africa. The objectives are to frame the settlement as a
socio-technical system, identify the factors that shape end-user participation,
examine the cultural and technological conditions of acceptance, specify the
mechanisms through which a Living Lab can support a just transition, and establish
how such a Living Lab should be evaluated.

The study adopts a critical posthumanist paradigm, supported by systems thinking,
energy justice, socio-technical transitions, Living Lab theory and complexity
theory. It follows a qualitative, participatory case study design using a Living
Lab approach, with co-design workshops, semi-structured interviews, observation and
documentary analysis, analysed through reflexive thematic and framework analysis.

The expected contributions are a situated account of acceptance and justice in an
informal settlement, a transferable Living Lab framework for community-scale energy
innovation, practice guidance for participatory design, and methodological insight
into reflexive, posthumanist energy research in engineering management.
