Chapter 2 
Research Methodology and Process 
2.1 Introduction 
This chapter provides information on the research methodology, methodological procedures 
and strategies used in conducting this research study. Additionally, there is a consideration of 
the procedures of validity threat management and ethics of the study. The Living Lab concept 
happens to be a new field in the energy sector and would need suitable methods. 
Imperatively, the choice of the methods is not dependable on the research questions only, but 
on the state of the specific research and the conditions to obtain the data needed (Maxwell, 
1996). This implies that making the choices for the methodology is often not based on the best 
form of practices used in the case but depends basically on the familiarities and habits in the 
field. Therefore, the decision of the methodologies does not rule out the fact that they can be 
mixed to answer the research questions. This can also be referred to as the multiple research 
approach (Miles and Huberman, 1994; Yin, 2009). Therefore, the methodology can go from 
the empirical to a non-empirical and desktop studies approach which will include model-
building, and with an applied qualitative grounded theory approach (creation of a theory that 
is grounded in the field of study), quantitative approach, Ethnographic approach (which 
explains the social and cultural connection between a group of individuals) (Nicholls, D., 2009) 
and the (DRM) - Design Research Methodology framework (which is a design support 
framework used in this research study for the development of the energy technology design) 
(Blessing & Chakrabarti, 2009).   
The techniques for data gathering involved primary and secondary data collection which will 
include the perusal (library search) – data mining, observations (field research) - case study 
(using the Living Lab network to the poor informal settlements as case studies), questioning 
(field research) – interviews, surveys and questionnaires, the literature review, focus groups, 
document reviews, and workshop. The data analysis involved qualitative analysis, comparative 
analysis, stakeholder mapping, system dynamics, content analysis, narrative analysis, thematic 
analysis, coding, and memos. Some of the core methods to be used will be explained in this 
chapter relating to the research questions. 
Furthermore, the research will follow a maximum variation sampling approach which can 
allow for the comparison and contrasting of different cases for theory-building (Eisenhardt, 
1989; Kathleen and Melissa, 2007). The sampling technique will be a non-random probability

2.2.2 
The research study paradigms 
The phrase paradigm was used by Kuhn (1962) in his study to interpret the development of 
scientific discoveries in observations, instead of how they are ultimately metamorphosed in 
scientific journals and academic books. Maxwell (2005) while referring to the historian 
Thomas Kuhn indicated that the use of a research paradigm is paramount and hypercritical. 
Therefore, the chosen research techniques, research samples and methodologies can be 
determined by deciding the types of paradigms used, which permits the anthologies to see the 
study the way the academicians view it (Lincoln & Denzin, 2000). 
Furthermore, researchers out of various paradigms, tend to analyse and select a precise 
paradigm depending on the research study being carried out (Ponterotto, 2005). However, as 
indicated by Lincoln and Guba (1985), there are four typical paradigms which are categorised 
as being effective, and these paradigms are ‘positivism’, ‘post-positivism’, ‘constructivism-
interpretivism’ and ‘critical ideological theory’. These four paradigms will be briefly explained 
below. 
It is suggested that methods are connected to the paradigms; thus, this study might adopt the 
paradigm crossing to attain the method and answer the research questions, because an improved 
stance may arise by the use of the paradigm crossing (Yearworth and White, 2013). This 
permits the researcher to investigate the links between the subjective approach and hard 
approach (quantitative) regarding the constructivism-interpretivism frame (White, 2006). What

Page | 147 
 
this means is that the research may involve a mixed methodology that might require a paradigm 
crossing. The four paradigms are discussed briefly below: 
i. Positivism: The aim of this paradigm is essentially to predict and manage the classified 
phenomena being studied (Ponterotto, 2005). This paradigm is determined by the ‘hypothetico-
deductive’ method which is related to the quantitative research method in which the hypothesis 
is analysed (Guba and Lincoln, 1994; McGrath & Johnson, 2003). Furthermore, Easterby-
Smith et al. (2012:57) indicated that “the main concept of positivism shows the existence of 
the social world and indicates the measurement of its properties through objective methods, 
instead of being theorised subjectively through reflection or institution and sensation.” 
Therefore, this paradigm can be adopted for this research study based on its components and 
functionality, and if there is need for paradigm crossing. 
ii. Post-positivism: This paradigm is moderately adaptable to the grounded theory 
methodology (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 1999). The post-positivism paradigm indicates that 
truths are certainly not fully acknowledged, however, it can be accessed through a research 
study (Lincoln & Guba, 1985), or that the objective phenomenon is certainly not fully able to 
capture the entire reality (Lincoln & Guba, 2000). Additionally, the post-positivism paradigm 
is focused on the validation and exploration of theories. The positivism and post-positivism 
paradigms are interested in the explanation of a phenomenon which are often attributed to the 
quantitative research methodology (Lincoln and Guba., 1985). 
iv. Critical-ideological theory: This research paradigm refers to a social science system for 
the investigation of the indicated theories to address the current problems and research gaps 
(Alderson, 2016). The critical ideology paradigm influences the understanding of the world to 
allow for the development of forces to innovate and emancipate control (Ponterotto, 2005) in 
social settings. This has various significance in the management and engineering research 
study. Easterby-Smith et al (2012:74), explained that “critical theory creates a questioning eye 
on the reasons and effect of powerful individuals and groups which in an emancipatory process 
gives a concern for the notice of the least powerful individuals.” This paradigm was not 
considered for this research study. 
iii. Constructivism-interpretivism: This research paradigm accepts various and identical 
rational realities (Schwandt, 1994) which gives an insight into the theory of acceptance of the 
world by the experiences of human factors which is published through the use of languages. 
This research paradigm allows for the understanding of the phenomena by the researchers

through connections with the researchers and the phenomenon being studied or examined 
(Ponterotto, 2005). This paradigm is often used in qualitative research methods, which is 
positioned to the ‘subjectivist’ of this research study. This paradigm is satisfactorily chosen for 
this research study because it is workable and acceptable for the generation of theories. The 
rationale for choosing the constructivism-interpretivism paradigm for this study will be 
explained in section 4.2.3 below. 
2.2.3 
Relevance of the constructionist research design for this study 
The primary objective of this research study is, ‘to develop a Systems Living Lab that focuses 
on gender designs for the development of energy technology innovation up to a pilot scale 
through the involvement of end-users and other stakeholders in real-life scenarios to achieve 
the security of energy services’. This indicates that this research study is not focused on 
bringing liberation and power to the individuals in the communities, which can be ascribed to 
the ‘critical theory’ paradigm, but focuses on the people’s interpretations and procedures to 
answer the research questions, which can be ascribed to the ‘constructivism-interpretivism’ 
paradigm. Therefore, in this research study, ‘constructionism’ is associated with the processes 
of building the theoretical model, while ‘interpretivism’ is associated with the evaluation of the 
processes of building the theoretical model. 
Furthermore, the early stages of the ‘constructionist research design’, show theories with the 
absence of clear truth; therefore, it is the responsibility of the researchers to verify how various 
claims for truth and reality are constantly designed into existence (Easterby-Smith et al. 2012). 
This is why there are different types of methodologies which can be used in conjunction with 
the constructionist paradigm than the other epistemologies (Easterby-Smith, et al. 2012). 
Therefore, this research study has considered the grounded theory, which is frequently 
associated with the constructionist designs as the method to be used to direct this research 
study. The constructionist design can also be seen as a design where the researchers recognize

2.3 The research methodology stance 
The nature of this research problem is systemic; thus, this study requires a ‘systemic 
methodology’, to advance the problem-solving circumstances and assist with progressing the 
proficiency of the research participants. Several researchers when it comes to research designs 
have conducted research with the use of a logical combination of elements of various 
methodology research paradigms to address research problems or questions (Sharp, 1998; 
Mingers, 2000; Ackroyd, 2004). 
When referring to design methodologies, there is an adopted design research methodology used 
to collaborate with the methodologies and techniques used to conduct this research study. This 
design research methodology (DRM) (Blessing and Chakrabarti, 2009), was selected for this 
research study because the focus of this study was also on designing to a pilot scale an energy 
technology innovation with gender designs to advance energy security. The ‘relativist’ and 
‘subjective’ stance examined in this study gave the stance for adopting the DRM for this study. 
The DRM was selected for this research study because it gives the systematic investigation 
framework to tackle the research problems which has a link to the constructivist-interpretivist 
paradigm. The DRM has been adopted for several studies as a framework for the development 
of design tools in engineering education and for the development of a co-design toolkit for 
participant collaborations (Calderon, 2010; Petrulaityte et al., 2022). 
Therefore, according to Calderon (2010:1), “a design research methodology is an approach and 
a set of supporting methods and guidelines to be used as a framework for doing design

research”. Thus, this study will be designing and testing an energy technology with gender 
designs, in collaboration with several stakeholders and has selected the DRM as the framework

processes of energy technology innovation and this research studies more efficient if used 
according to the principles (Calderon, 2010).

Descriptive study 1 (DS-1) 
This stage can also be referred to as the ‘empirical data analysis’. The semi-structured

interviews and semi-structured interviews (Gill, Stewart, Treasure and Chadwick, 2008). 
Therefore, the semi-structured process is an interview process with various substantial 
questions that characterise the sections to be considered while allowing the researchers the 
possibility to provide effective insights as feedback (Gill et al. 2008). The semi-structured 
interview process is a link between the unstructured interview process and the structured 
interview process; thus, this interview process is suitable and chosen for this research study.

approach was conducted by the researchers and co-researchers in the communities used as case

This stage can also be referred to as the ‘assumption experience synthesis’. This stage also

This stage and the basic means were influenced by the grounded theory methodology (GT), 
which can be defined as a research approach for the construction of theoretical interpretation

Lingard, 2006). Furthermore, there is no hypothesis in grounded theory but there are open-

referred to as an emergent grounded theory (Tavakol et al. 2006). The major constraint of the 
grounded theory is that the results may be exposed to ‘research bias because of the processes 
of the approaches involved (Charmaz, 2014), however, this constraint was controlled by efforts 
used to analyse research bias in the validity section below.

questions and potential answers regarding their causal relationships are: 1) Open coding - open 
coding is often described as the initial conceptualization of abstract concepts or entities. The 
process of open coding commences with a constant comparison approach, wherein the 
researcher identifies categories based on the phenomena observed within the derived data. 2) 
Axial coding - This is the stage where results and interactions are thoroughly examined. During 
axial coding, the process of sample reduction takes place, involving the consolidation of 
categories by merging them into broader ones. 3) Categorization and Proposition - At this stage, 
the synthesis of relevance and impacts culminates in the formulation of a proposition regarding 
the phenomenon under study. This is achieved by taking into account the observed results and 
the ongoing developments. Hence, a category is established based on the proposition, and it 
can be employed to advance the research process. 4) Memo - These represent the insights 
gained during the categorization processes. They also empower the researcher to create a 
checklist for the categories, aiding in the ongoing comprehension of the data collection process 

########## PDF PAGE 164 ##########
Page | 158 
 
(Charmaz, 2008). 5) Selective Coding – This serves as a method for promptly narrowing down 
categories, thereby pinpointing the primary core variables. It is also the juncture at which the 
research trajectory shifts, with the categories guiding the research process. As Douglas (2003) 
articulates, "The selective coding process aids in reducing the categories distinguished by the 
researcher to a smaller sample size, within which propositions are assigned codes for the 
categories". 6) Theoretical sampling – This is a method wherein the refined categories are 
redefined, and additional data is gathered to align with these categories. It is a process through 
which data collection is systematically conducted to formulate a theory (theory description). 
Furthermore, other coding methods can be adopted for this stage, and this is the following: 1) 
Mini literature search - This is the phase where the research process involves the creation of 
memos and mini literature reviews following the collection and analysis of data in each stage. 
The researcher maintains this ongoing process as a collection of notes throughout the research 
journey, enabling them to stay informed and generate insights by reflecting on the collected 
data and making informal notes. This pivotal aspect of the research process serves as an 
intermediary step for drafting papers and gathering additional data (Charmaz, 2006). 2) 
Documentary research - As indicated by Bailey (1994), documentary research encompasses 
both primary and secondary documents. Primary documents entail firsthand accounts and 
"eyewitness" experiences relevant to the research study, while secondary documents provide 
insights from individuals who were not present but have knowledge of the event or research 
subject. The documentary research process involves the analysis of documents containing 
information about the studied phenomenon, as highlighted by Ahmed (2010). To ensure the 
authenticity of the documentary research, it is imperative to conceptualise, evaluate, analyse, 
and implement quality control measures on the documents. 
Therefore, the summary of this research process which is the dependability was utilised with 
the grounded theory, as it has the potential to yield both empirical findings (core variables) and 
a theoretical model (causal loop diagram). This process led to the development of concepts and 
the creation of a causal loop diagram which was used to further develop the SETILL model, 
demonstrating the reliability and credibility of the research study. The appendix --- provides a 
summary of the steps undertaken and illustrates the thorough application of the grounded

Establishment of the categories and analytic codes from data: In this procedure, the 
researcher constructs categories through ongoing comparison and analysis of the collected data.

What sets this process apart is the presence of a preconceived or predetermined theory that 
emerges during the research journey. 
Acknowledgement of the underlying collective process within the data: Following the 
creation of categories and analytical codes, the essential collective process is subsequently 
formulated around the core categories. 
Inductive creation of conceptual categories: Inductive reasoning is a fundamental 
characteristic of grounded theory, as outlined by Leboea (2017). It represents the qualitative 
approach employed by researchers to uncover a phenomenon by setting aside any preconceived 
notions and constructing a theory based on the phenomenon itself. In grounded theory, the 
theory is developed and often not subjected to testing. In This study, the theory will be tested. 
Enhancing categories through the process of theoretical sampling: In grounded theory, the 
primary objective for the researcher is to construct categories and subsequently transform these 
categories into a conceptual theory, representing the core purpose of grounded theory 
methodology. The method of theoretical sampling involves selecting data that align with 
theoretical considerations. This concept is reinforced by Breckenridge and Jones (2009), who 
emphasise that theoretical sampling is a fundamental hallmark of grounded theory and a critical 
component in the development of any theory grounded in data. 
Theory saturation: Saturation occurs when the researcher consistently encounters identical 
findings. It typically happens when, after multiple rounds of qualitative data collection and 
analysis, no fresh insights, inputs, directions, or data contribute to the established categories. 
Challenges in Grounded Theory: One significant concern regarding the challenges in 
grounded theory is that it often concludes with the theory itself, rather than delving into the 
elucidation of themes. Numerous studies offer comprehensive thematic illustrations without a 
substantive underlying theory (Kennedy and Lingard, 2006). This absence of theoretical 
abstractions makes it difficult to discern meaningful relationships and interconnections among 
the themes. Thus, it becomes imperative to develop and validate the theory before proceeding 
with the second phase of the research study. Another risk highlighted in this context is the 
misconception that researchers employing the grounded theory approach can initiate their study 
without any research questions or prior exploration of existing literature, potentially resulting 
in disorganised research manuscripts that impede the research process (Suddaby, 2006). In this 
particular study, this risk was mitigated by adopting a more controlled approach—beginning

2.4.1 
Application of system dynamics 
System dynamics, which is a part of the systems thinking approach, was used in this study. 
Systems approaches are primarily characterised as a comprehensible cause-and-effect 
framework involving interconnected variables within a system. It necessitates the independent 
assessment and refinement of mental models aligned with a particular mode of thinking 
(Cabrera, Colosi and Lobdell, 2008). Furthermore, this approach can be viewed as a crucial 
means of scrutinising intricate interrelationships, which inherently entail complexity. 
Consequently, it has been recognized as a central approach in this research study and was 
discussed in detail in Chapter 2 of this study. Moreover, System dynamics is acknowledged as 
a suitable methodology for examining complex social and economic systems within the context 
of systems Living Lag, energy studies and gender inequality in informal settlements against 
energy poverty. 
This systems approach, in particular, proves valuable in simulating intricate problems and 
policy frameworks through experimental approaches (Forrester, 1994). It centres on control 
theory (Kirkwood, 1998) and modern nonlinear dynamics theory (Amigun, Musango and 
Stafford, 2011). Often, the interrelationship diagram takes the form of stock and flow diagrams. 
Notably, systems approach equips policymakers with ready-made tools to address significant 
complex challenges (Sterman, 1991) such as energy poverty and gender inequality. 
Significantly, this approach finds application in modelling the connections and 
interdependencies among variables that influence the contribution of women as end-users in 
the informal settlements in energy-challenged areas and contribute to energy poverty reduction 
efforts in the Western Cape, South Africa, and beyond. The approach is also characterised by 

########## PDF PAGE 169 ##########
Page | 163 
 
its utilisation of causal loop diagrams to depict causal relationships among seemingly unrelated 
elements within a system (Forrester, 1973; Forrester, 1987, 1991). Likewise, causal loop 
diagrams have proven effective in illustrating intricate relationships and feedback mechanisms 
across various sectors, including agriculture (Vennix, 1999) and the energy sector (Amigun, 
Musango and Stafford, 2011; Okoye, 2020). 
Therefore, to answer research questions 3 and 4 respectively: the “system dynamics 
(community-based system dynamics)” modelling which is a part of the “systems thinking 
approach” was used to improve understanding of the issues and behaviours of the stakeholders. 
The case study, comparative analysis and stakeholder mapping were also used as the researcher 
mapped the stakeholders in Chapter 3 to understand the level of influence in the decision-
making process and compare the case study with the Living Lab concept. Therefore, the system 
thinking gives an insight into who links to whom during the stakeholder mapping and allows 
for mapping the decision-making which can be the high-level points for gender innovation 
decision-making. It also helped to understand or determine the extent of women's decision-
making in that part.  
Community-based system dynamics modelling 
Community-based system dynamics which is a part of the system dynamics originated from 
the study of Hovmand (2014). According to Hovmand (2014), community-based system 
dynamics is the engagement of communities to participate in the creation of models that 
produce recommendations and insights to the system, empowerment of women, and encourage 
the community members to implement changes relating to the insights. According to Suzanne 
Smit (2019), “Community-based system dynamics is an adaptation of mainstream system 
dynamics and group model building, which emerged because of the need to engage with less 
sophisticated audiences to improve learning and understanding of the systems that they are part 
of”. 
The community-based system dynamics were relevant to understanding behaviours expected 
of stakeholders influencing the energy technology innovation developed through the systems 
Living Lab. It has also been used for several issues like the study of the abuse of alcohol in 
college students (Apostolopoulos et al., 2018), the issues of “mental health in Afghanistan” 
(Trani et al., 2016) and many more in the natural management like “water and forestry 
planning” (Rosenthal et al., 2017). 

########## PDF PAGE 170 ##########
Page | 164 
 
Therefore, this systems thinking method was adapted to build a model that helped map the 
interrelationships to show the stakeholder contributions, the level of gender innovations 
decision-making, and the extent of women's decision-making in the systems Living Lab 
(research questions 2 and 3). 
Causal loop diagram 
As a result of this application, an interrelationship diagram is generated, consisting of 
interconnected loops with visual representations and dynamics employed to elucidate the 
theory underpinning the system. The connections to various variables serve as the foundation 
for constructing the causal loop diagram, with feedback loops providing insights into the 
behaviour of the phenomenon. This research has led to the modelling of the SETILL with 
gender design as a systemic behaviour.

Resource Limitations: Carrying out research in different case studies is time-consuming and

testing phase of the SETILL theoretical model on one research case.  
Time Constraints: This research study had set timelines or time barriers from the institute of

study (Gerring, 2007; Creswell, 2014; Yin, 2014). 
Feasibility: The case was more difficult to conduct research for this study than the other 
because of some logistical, political or environmental circumstance in Umuahia, Nigeria. It 
was more practical to direct the attention at validating the theoretical model by advancing the 
biogas technology in one, yet more reachable center (Tzaneen, South Africa) (Stake, 1995; 
George, A. L., & Bennett, 2005; Creswell, 2014; Yin, 2014).

was focused on the most promising or relevant case study (Tzaneen, South Africa) (K. M. 
Eisenhardt, 1989). 
Pilot Study: Also, we intended to use one case study that will serve as a pilot or preliminary 
investigation to refine research methods and gather initial data, which informed a more 
comprehensive study in the selected case (Creswell, 2014; Denscombe, 2014; Yin, 2014). 
Comparative Analysis: Also, we intended to compare and contrast the results between the

Eisenhardt, 1989; Gerring, 2007; Baxter, P., & Jack, 2008; Creswell, 2014; Yin, 2014; Patton,

2.6 The trustworthiness of this research study 
This section of the study serves a critical purpose in addressing several key questions: How 
might the results and conclusions be susceptible to inaccuracies? How can the data potentially 
acquired enhance our understanding within the context of the conceptual framework? 
Furthermore, why should the readers or stakeholders involved in the research study have 
confidence in the results? To ensure productivity and mitigate the risk of ineffectiveness, it is 
essential to remain vigilant about potential threats, understand them, and proactively seek 
strategies to address them within the research study. As emphasised by (Maxwell, 1996), the 
significance of this section lies in the researchers' ongoing efforts to minimise validity threats 
through the collection of supporting evidence throughout the research process. 
Trustworthiness holds particular relevance in this research study, as it serves as a means to 
establish credibility in the study. It emphasises the reliance on evidence rather than methods, 
ensuring that the research continually verifies the methodological processes employed." 
2.6.1 
Trustworthiness considerations in the context of a constructionist design 
While discussions surrounding the assurance and demonstration of the quality of 
constructionist design are prevalent, it is noteworthy that the term 'validity' is seldom used by 
authors (Easterby-Smith, Thorpe, & Jackson, 2012). Golden-Biddle and Locke, (1993) have 
identified three essential components for establishing trustworthiness within a constructionist 
framework, which are elaborated below: 
i. Authenticity: This pertains to convincing individuals that the researcher possesses a 
profound understanding of the insights derived from the institution. 
ii. Plausibility: This involves considering the presence of reviews or interest among fellow 
researchers. 
iii. Criticality: This encompasses persuading readers to question unexamined assumptions, 
thereby contributing genuinely innovative insights. 

########## PDF PAGE 184 ##########
Page | 178 
 
Easterby-Smith et al. (2012:96) noted that David Silverman (2000) advocates for a more 
objectivist stance due to concerns that researchers may selectively choose evidence to support 
their preconceived biases. Silverman (2000) also offers guidance to researchers on methods 
that can safeguard against anecdotal charges, as detailed below: 
i. Constant comparison: This employs grounded theory concepts to identify new contexts that 
extend the existing theory. 
ii. Comprehensive data treatment: This involves analysing all primary data before 
concluding. 
iii. Refutability: This entails actively seeking instances that could potentially challenge or 
disprove current beliefs. 
iv. Tabulations: This emphasises thorough organisation of data, which can prove useful in 
elucidating the occurrence of phenomena. 
Easterby-Smith et al. (2012:97) emphasised that constructionist research outcomes should be 
credible and reached through transparent methods. Therefore, the researcher must elucidate 
how they gained access to the organisation, describe the informant selection process, document 
data creation and recording, delineate processes for data summarization and synthesis, expound 
on how data evolved into tentative ideas and explanations, and convey their personal feelings 
about the research. Many of these aspects were duly considered and implemented in this 
research and chapter. Below, we will delve into elements of trustworthiness before addressing 
concerns related to external and internal validity, validity procedures, and validity threats." 
2.6.2 
Components of trustworthiness 
In this research study, a comprehensive evaluation of all aspects of the research study is 
challenging, and a researcher cannot provide a universal measure of the study's trustworthiness. 
Instead, the responsibility lies with the researchers to demonstrate the evidence of 
trustworthiness in the research (Maxwell, 1992). Similarly, just as quantitative research 
requires reliability for validity, qualitative research requires dependability for transferability. 
This means that judgments or inferences cannot be made directly from one perspective to 
another. 
In establishing the worth of research, the concepts of validity and trustworthiness are 
paramount (Lincoln & Guba 1985). A crucial question in assessing trustworthiness is, 'How 

########## PDF PAGE 185 ##########
Page | 179 
 
can a researcher convince the audience, or even oneself, that the research findings merit 
attention?' This was achieved, assessed, or addressed through four key elements, briefly 
explained below, to aid in evaluating the trustworthiness of this study: 
i. Credibility: This pertains to the extent to which the researchers believe in the truthfulness 
of the research results, prompting the question of whether they appear genuine. 
ii. Confirmability: This measures the level of objectivity, determined by whether the research 
findings are shaped by the respondents rather than influenced by researcher bias, ensuring that 
the findings genuinely reflect the research focus. 
iii. Dependability: This signifies that the research results can be replicated or repeated and 
maintain consistency within the same or similar context. 
iv. Transferability: This denotes that the research findings can be applied to some extent in 
another context." 
2.6.3 
Alternatives to internal and external validity 
In the context of enhancing the credibility of research findings and addressing potential threats 
to validity, nine procedures can contribute to trustworthiness. These procedures include 
triangulation, the incorporation of disconfirming evidence, fostering reflexivity in the research 
process, employing member checking, engaging in prolonged fieldwork, fostering 
collaboration, maintaining an audit trail, providing rich and detailed descriptions, and seeking 
peer feedback and debriefing (Creswell & Miller, 2000:126). 
In this study, the term 'credibility' may be used interchangeably with 'internal validity.' This 
substitution aligns with established validity procedures, where 'internal validity' can be 
replaced with 'credibility' (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). However, when it comes to 'external 
validity,' which is beyond the researcher's control, it may be replaced with 'transferability.' As 
Shaw (2013:58) notes, this redefinition signifies a notable shift in the concept's meaning, as the 
responsibility for demonstrating transferability lies with those seeking to apply the findings in 
different contexts when the findings are context-specific.

2.6.4 
Categories of validity (trustworthiness) and their associated challenges 
The categories of validity encompass descriptive, interpretative, theoretical, and 
generalizability validity. 
Descriptive validity: In the process of depicting the researcher's observations and auditory 
experiences, the primary threat to description validity pertains to potential gaps or inaccuracies 
in the data. To mitigate this concern, the researchers employed audio or video recordings during 
observations and interviews, along with subsequent verbatim transcriptions of these recordings, 
effectively averting a significant validity risk within the study. This precaution is essential 
because any inferences or conclusions drawn by the researcher based on the descriptive data 
can be called into question if the initial description of the observed events or conducted 
interviews lacks validity. Consequently, interviews are routinely recorded and transcribed, 
unless compelling reasons exist to deviate from this practice (Maxwell, 1996). 
Interpretive validity: As Maxwell (1996) suggests, a key challenge to achieving valid 
interpretation is the temptation to superimpose one's framework or meaning onto the 
perspectives of the individuals under study, rather than authentically representing their 
viewpoints and the meanings they ascribe to their words and actions. This potential pitfall can 
manifest in several ways: 
1. When the researchers fail to attentively discern the intended meanings of the 
participants. 

########## PDF PAGE 187 ##########
Page | 181 
 
2. When the researchers are unaware of the frameworks and assumptions of the 
participants. 
3. When the researchers do not allow the participants to give their views in full and often 
when the researchers use leading and brief questions. 
In order to counteract this threat to validity, it was not a random way of re-categorizing 
participant expressions based on certain prerequisites (Maxwell, 1996), but incorporated a 
systematic method of tracing how the participants interpreted the research situation, and a 
member-checking technique to conquer this threat of invalidity. 
Theory and generalisation validity: Strauss and Corbin (1990) identified another cardinal 
issue: lack of concern for other, perhaps competing or even conflicting interpretations of the 
phenomena in study or other ways of making sense of collected data. To avoid this threat the 
researchers adopted the practice of reviewing reasearch theories dealing with concepts not 
captured in this study. 
Maxwell (1996) posited that there are two forms of generalisation: internal and external 
generalizability. Internal generalizability pertains to the extent to which conclusions can be 
applied within the specific settings or groups studied, whereas external generalizability 
concerns the broader applicability of conclusions beyond the studied settings or groups. In the 
realm of this research study, internal generalizability assumes particular significance. 
2.6.5 
Threats to validity 
In this research study, two primary categories of validity threats exist, although Maxwell (1996) 
noted that it is not feasible to enumerate all potential validity threats to the conclusions drawn 
in this research. These categories encompass researcher bias and reactivity (about the 
researcher's influence on the settings or individuals being studied). 
Researcher Bias: These instances refer to situations where a researcher's theories, values, or 
preconceptions inadvertently shape the collection and analysis of data. In this research, the 
primary concern is to understand how an individual researcher's values and perspectives might 
influence the study's approach and findings, rather than erasing the diversity in researchers' 
values and viewpoints they bring to the study (Hammersley & Atkinson, 1995). 
The potential biases in this research might include researcher bias and participant bias. The 
researchers addressed these concerns by meticulously recording responses during interviews 
to mitigate researcher bias. Additionally, personal, face-to-face interviews were conducted to 

########## PDF PAGE 188 ##########
Page | 182 
 
promote transparency, and participant identities were kept confidential to minimise participant 
bias. 
Reactivity: Reactivity refers to the impact of the researchers on the settings or individuals 
under study. In this research, the objective is not to eliminate the researcher's influence but 
rather to comprehend and harness it effectively, as entirely eradicating the researcher's impact 
is not feasible (Hammersley & Atkinson, 1995). 
Maxwell (1996) elucidated that in participant observation studies, reactivity is not considered 
a significant validity threat, contrary to common belief. However, in the case of interviews, 
reactivity poses a substantial validity threat that cannot be completely avoided. For instance, 
the information provided by participants is influenced by both the interviewer and the 
interview's context. 
It is argued that incorporating credibility and originality can enhance the resonance and utility 
of this research (Charmaz, 2006). This notion has prompted several questions, as outlined 
below: 
i. Credibility: Can readers independently evaluate the researcher's claims? Does the argument 
draw strong connections to the collected data? Are there sufficient empirical observations 
presented for each category? Has enough data been provided to support the claims? 
ii. Originality: Can novel ideas be derived from the established categories? What is the 
significance of the research study? Can the methodologies contribute to the modification of 
existing ideas and practices? 
iii. Resonance: Do participants recognize the essence of the methodologies? Does their lived 
experience align with the identified categories? Can participants see that the analysis provides 
insights into their communities and lives? 
iv. Usefulness: Can the researchers propose additional areas for research? Does the research 
contribute to expanding knowledge? Can individuals or communities apply the results in their 
daily lives and environments?

5.  Ethical considerations 
Ethics is based on justifiable standards of right and wrong that specifies what people should do 
mostly in terms of rights, responsibilities, and benefits to society, fairness, or virtues, which 
could be right to life, freedom, injury, and privacy. Such standards are suitable standards of 
ethics because they support dependable and justifiable grounds. Ethics in this research would 

########## PDF PAGE 196 ##########
Page | 190 
 
be a study and formulation of one’s ethical standards. It is essential to always check one’s 
standards to ensure that they are reasonable and justifiable. 
In this research, no harm is predicted or expected; therefore, the research will be done ethically, 
and there will be no discrimination against any group or persons. During the data collection 
process, there will be no risk of ethical violations as only available public sources will be used. 
The researcher also will participate in the fieldwork and workshops relating to Living Labs and 
energy, will have meetings with some of the members of the urban poor informal settlement 
and stakeholders in the energy sector (especially women and girls) with granted permission. 
The participants will also be informed before the interview or group discussions, and consent 
forms will be signed by the participants who would allow for the use of audio recording, group 
discussions, and they will be informed that they could stop participating anytime if they are 
uncomfortable. Also, the participants will have the right to choose a comfortable place for the 
interview. 
In summary, this research study will be conducted ethically, and it will be done with the 
University of Pretoria’s ethics committee approval.

7. 
Conclusion 
The Living Lab concept happens to be a new field in the energy or power sector and would 
need a suitable method. The qualitative then quantitative approach that could be suitable for 
this research is proposed for this study. The research work is proposed to be done in a poor 
urban informal settlement in South Africa. The proposed kind of technology is the household 
energy technologies, relating to electricity and cooking technologies. 
The people to be involved will comprise the stakeholders and the end-users (men and women) 
in and around the case study. The development of Living Lab relating to the urban informal 
settlement will continue to be one of the most critical methods of reducing gender inequality 
in the energy sector in South Africa. The SETI Living Lab will support and help to broaden 
the understanding of the gendered energy technology innovations and their interactions with 
the urban informal settlement in Africa. 
In this study, the theoretical models will be developed to answer the research questions and

Page | 193 
 
the theoretical model will be sufficiently abstracted that it could be applied to different 
communities, informal settlements, energy industries and energy poverty prone areas around 
South Africa and African environs, and any other situation that requires the development of 
gendered energy technology innovations. The theoretical model will also provide an 
understanding that in focusing on security of energy services, ‘gendered energy technology 
innovations will attempt to reduce energy poverty by focusing on using the model’s or the 
mechanism(s) that make up the model for insights that will contribute to understanding gender 
and energy security. 
The theoretical models to be developed will go through checklists, especially against data to 
indicate that the model explains the real-world outcome and that the model applies to the case 
study. To instill the confidence that the research result would be worth paying attention to or 
worth considering, the data confirmability, dependability, and credibility procedures will be 
addressed, as introduced by Guba et al. (1982), to replace the terms commonly used for 
quantitative research. 
Finally, management, engineering, gender, and energy researchers will have the chance to 
contribute to empirical relevance and provide the energy sector and stakeholders, institutions, 
and policymakers with the right decisions to make.
