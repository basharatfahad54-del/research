2.1 Introduction 
The literature review is used to conceptualise the Living Lab, gendered energy security, energy 
poverty, biogas technology, the informal settlement and give a critical analysis of the terms 
defined and how the proposed study may conceptualise it for the context-specific application 
in the case studies. 
This literature review seeks to discuss the role of gender in energy technology innovations in 
the context of Living Labs. Living Labs are pre-competitive, open innovation environments 
that engage real-life communities in the development and testing process (Clermont & Fionda, 
2016). By structuring Living Labs based on gender, new energy solutions can be created that 
are equally useful for all community members with particular focus on women. To address this 
challenge, it is therefore very important to apply new strategies which should take into account 
the Social, Economic and Cultural aspects of these communities. In addition, this review 
underlines the need to embrace gender empowerment as captured in the SDG 5, which seeks 
to address inequalities for gender at all levels. Energy poverty and the gendered nature of it 
further is a compelling social justice issue, but also a determinant in the viability of 
effectiveness and practices of energy solutions at a worldwide scale (Callaghan and Herselman, 
2015). Thus, raising awareness of gender aspects in energy technology innovations as well as 
including girls and women into the enumerated list of beneficiaries will help to contribute 
toward sustainable and fair development on the basis of improved energy access for South 
African communities. 
The literature approach utilised in this study is what the researcher calls a stand-alone 
qualitative systematic literature review (realist review) (Finfgeld-Connett and Johnson, 2013), 
conducted using a rigorous quality (Okoli and Schabram, 2010). This type of literature review 
focuses on limiting random and scientific misconceptions and requires rigorous use of 
scientific plans (Paré, 2017), with close adherence to clear scientific concepts (Liberati et al., 
2009). The realist review as a new method of a systematic review “does not provide simple 
answers to complex questions, might not tell policy-makers or managers whether something 
works or not, but will provide the policy and practice to the community with the kind of rich, 
detailed and highly practical understanding of complex social interventions which is likely to 
be of much more use to them when planning and implementing programs at a national, regional 
or local level” (Pawson et al. 2005:21). This type of review method is theory-driven which can

be used to improve the systematic review by applying qualitative strategies of complex 
interventions that could be used to inform policy decision-making (Paré, 2017).

2.2.2 Historical context and current status of gender inequality in energy access 
Prominent gender inequality in energy access has historical political and social causes that can 
be traced to South Africa and many other African countries. The culture of male dominance 
that has been evident in many African societies tends to influence gender equality or the lack 
of it in many sectors like energy (Johnson, Gerber and Muhoza, 2019). Traditionally, women 
in African societies have been confined to the home, socially and economically powerless, and 
have had little scope or opportunity for education, business, and other ventures. This division 
of labour based on gender has greatly influenced energy access since women are responsible 
for supplies of energy for home use, which include cooking, water heating, and space heating, 
services that mainly depend on traditional sources of energy such as firewood and charcoal 
(Jagoe et al., 2020). 
In South Africa, the apartheid policy system aggravates gender and racial disparities and 
provides a background to the country’s current socio-economic system that gives differentiated 

########## PDF PAGE 34 ##########
Page | 28 
 
access to the mineral-energy complex. There have been challenges in the South African energy 
landscape since the definite post-apartheid progressive upturns; today, millions of people in 
the country, including those who are rurally and informally housed, remain inadequately 
electrified (Mario, Brincat and Moe, 2024). Women suffer grave consequences in these 
communities due to their social and economic status and roles based on gender (Heise et al., 
2019). 
The currently available data shows that there has not been much progress in addressing the 
gender gap in energy access. Estimates by the International Energy Agency (IEA) show that in 
sub-Saharan Africa alone, more than 600 million people have no access to electricity, and 
women endure the worst impacts of energy poverty (Ferdous, 2021). Although there are 
improvements in the electrification rates in South Africa, this does not necessarily mean that 
everyone can access modern energy services or have reliable energy for several activities, 
especially for rural women. 
Patriarchal norms shape access, use, and control of energy resources in African societies to a 
large extent. This research study also examines how the energy sector has remained a male-
dominated industry in the past, where very few women were employed, let alone given 
decision-making authority. It is also examining how human factors affect energy security. It is 
also established that men dominate the formal employment sector, whereby they are the major 
participants in generating, distributing, and vending energy (Oyiana, 2021). At the same time, 
women are either ceremonially involved in the same processes or are part-time employees 
involved in the informal vending of energy in their homes. The next section explains gendered 
energy technology innovation leading to energy access. 
2.2.3 Energy technology innovation for achieving gender equality in energy services 
As explained above, ensuring reliable energy services continues to pose a significant challenge 
in impoverished rural areas across Africa. A considerable number of households face a deficit 
in appropriate energy technologies to meet their modern energy demands. Women bear the 
brunt of this issue due to entrenched gender roles within households. Despite the evident gender 
disparities in energy services, the incorporation of gender perspectives in the innovation of 
energy products remains largely unaddressed. This research study delved into the intersection 
of gendered innovation and energy innovation, investigating how this combination can 
alleviate issues surrounding the security and accessibility of energy services in households

within impoverished rural settings and went ahead to develop a biogas technology to a pilot

Innovation has long been a cornerstone of human progress, driving pivotal shifts in agriculture, 
science, and industry throughout history. Today, in our modern, technology-driven societies 
powered by digital advancements, energy innovation plays a pivotal role in facilitating access 
to a wide array of services (batinge et al., 2022). Energy services encompass a spectrum of 
benefits facilitated by energy carriers, fuel sources, and technologies, (Sovacool, 2011). These 
benefits extend to various outcomes of energy utilisation (Modi et al., 2005), ranging from 
preserved food and cooked meals to illuminated spaces, chilled beverages, rapid internet 
connectivity, and cosy sleeping arrangements (Sovacool, 2011).  
The conventional centralised approach to innovation is swiftly losing relevance 
(MacCormack et al., 2007). Top-down innovation typically follows a sequence of idea 
generation, screening, market research, product development, commercialization, and 
implementation (Gaynor, 2013). However, the limitations of these traditional top-down 
models have been evident in academic discourse for approximately four decades, these 
limitations encompass neglect of alternative actors (Sabatier, 1986), limited user ownership, 
prolonged innovation readiness periods, and potential market evolution by the time of 
product availability. Furthermore, even in cases where the market remains static, there's a 
considerable risk that the innovation may not align with market needs due to flaws in market 
research or misperceptions regarding innovation diffusion (Batinge 2022). The process by 
which innovations are adopted within a society (innovation diffusion) is a dynamic and 
evolving phenomenon (Bale et al., 2013). The concept of how innovations spread is well-
established in the study of household energy usage (Wilson & Dowlatabadi, 2007). Studies 
across various disciplines have examined how technologies and ideas spread through 
networks (Valente, 2005). An infection paradigm may also be used here to indicate the 
process of network diffusion (Akbarpour & Jackson, 2018; Pastor-Satorras et al., 2015; Raj et 
al., 2012), as well as an epidemic with the very beginning and the potential development of 
the spread of a disease and its further consequences. This analysis would cannot be complete 
without the mention of the Bass model which was proposed in 1969 and is a clear example of 
a diffusion model. 
The Bass model developed by Frank Bass defines the process of new products adoption via the 
communication between current and potential users. Although several models of diffusion 

########## PDF PAGE 36 ##########
Page | 30 
 
focus on social aspects, which in the model of Bass are in the form of word-of mouth influence 
(Bale et al., 2013), the decision making process of the adoption of a new product depends on 
this. Such elements can include the likelihood of its purchase, compatibility of the new product 
with other products, appropriateness of the technology in view of the individual characteristics, 
and the social-cultural factors. Those decisions are usually taken based on such relative factors 
as ‘intrinsic advantages for the intended adoption’ and the technology ‘compatibility with the 
existing technology environment’, as well as ‘simplicity in the usage of a given technology’, 
the possibility of ‘trial run’, and the ‘clearliness of results’ (Vagnani & Volpe, 2017). Perceived 
benefits refer to the extent to which an innovation is believed to be more beneficial than the 
concept which it replaces. Adoption by others is the degree to which an innovation matches up 
to current practices, past experiences and needs of other users. IPO means how difficult or easy 
it is considered to understand and implement an innovation. Trial option can be defined as 
potential in which an innovation can be first tried on a limited level. Observability of its impact 
is the level of measurability in terms of how others are aware of the presence of an innovation. 
On this note, it is in most models explaining the diffusion of innovations that an innovation's 
ability to fulfill users' needs is critical towards the adoption decision. For these reasons, this 
study suggested that the innovation process takes into consideration the context in which the 
user will eventually apply the innovation while developing the innovation based on the specific 
attributes identified in Vagnani and Volpe, (2017) for the innovation to serve exactly according 
to the needs of end consumers. In low-income households, where females are the majority, 
their energy use is significantly higher—about 80% more overall, with a notable 54% increase 
in electricity consumption compared to males (Elnakat et al., 2016). These communities, as 
highlighted by Elnakat et al. (2016), have a higher energy consumption per unit of living area, 
attributed to the prevalence of female-led households and the inefficiency of their homes. The 
role of women is pivotal in achieving satisfaction with household energy services in 
economically disadvantaged areas, given their dominance in such households (Kovacic et al., 
2019) and their higher usage of energy services. 
Concerning energy access, there was a reveal that gender disparities were apparent in many

2.2.5 Energy (in)security challenges and its gender-specific impacts 
Energy security concept evolved during the production of oil to the military in the 
unfortunate 20th century (Yergin et al., 2006). Energy security was also considered in the 
2000s during producing oil circulations from Asia to Europe (Yergin et al., 2006; Hughes, 
2016). Energy security in the earlyyears and recent years are not the same because of climate 
change mitigation and inability to access supply of energy services. As a result, continued 
effort is crucial in thoroughly re-considering the concept of energy security (Cherp & Jewell, 
2014). 
Writing in 2011 explaining the current state of the art, Sovacool has found about 45 definitions 
of energy security in the literature. Nonetheless, Parag (2014, p.99) operationalized energy 
security as “the adequate, reliable and competitive supply of energy”, including adequacy 
which is the “availability of sufficient energy to support economic and social activity”; 
reliability which is “the availability of energy with little interruption in supply”; and 
competitiveness which is “the availability of energy at a reasonable price that does not harm 
the competitiveness of the economy while supporting investment in energy sector. Some 
definitions also consist in the procedures defining the parameters and areas of the 
“environmental” or “equity” (International Energy Agency, 2011) and “public acceptability” 
(Jansen, 2009; B. Sovacool, 2011). According to Parag (2014) p.99, “Public acceptability is 
linked to social, psychological and cultural factors which make the supply of generation 
technology unpopular”. In the contrast, none of the definitions have included the notion of 
gender and the Living Lab that would encompass the issues of the energy security challenges. 
Similarly, at the level of the household where women are present, there is still conceptualisation 

########## PDF PAGE 43 ##########
Page | 37 
 
and investigation of what energy security definition(s) should be and what energy security 
means (Musango et al., 2020). 
Barrett et al., (2010, p.4) argued for the necessity to broaden the extent of the analysis of energy 
security: “The level of security is not determined by supplies alone, but by the immediate 
balance between supply and demand and the longer-term trade-off between more energy 
security and environmental considerations (e.g., more wind farms vs. open spaces or more 
nuclear power vs. global security and nuclear proliferation)”. They indicated that there should 
be socio-technical and interdisciplinary methods to the thorough understanding of energy 
security, and these methods should consider the relationships between those engineering for 
the demand of energy and various energy security components (Parag, 2014). Therefore, this 
research study suggests that a thorough understanding of energy security in the poor urban 
informal settlement should require the situation of a socio-technical and user-oriented (women 
and girls) method that focuses on energy services, the security of energy services, and gender. 
This is because Pasqualetti and Sovacool (2012) establish that energy security and energy 
policy decisions’ importance must be viewed and advocated from the micro level to the 
household level. Energy security in the household means a steady and cheap supply of largely 
ubiquitous ‘clean energy fuel’ needed in performing activities in the households. However, up 
to date, there are few number of studies for defining the energy (in) security particularly at the 
level of the household (Musango et al., 2020). Hence, Hernández (2016, p.2) defined energy 
insecurity as the inefficiency which is experienced in the provision of adequate household 
energy necessities. Unauthorized sources also define energy insecurity of the household as “the 
status quo that resulted from the interplay of inadequate and insufficient energy consumption 
which does not meet the basic energy needs of a household”. They also defined energy security 
in the household as the ‘degree of energy required to support the fundamentals of daily life of 
the individual and the household in areas such as cooking, lighting, cleaning/washing and 
warming and cooling the house. These definitions focus mainly on the basic demand of society, 
that’s energy services consumption. 
Therefore, Shrestha et al. (2020) generated a framework for the research of the differences in 
gender for decision-making in the household. The framework underlined categories which are 
“subjective variables, objective variables, differences in reasons, differences in decision-
making, use, purchase, and action needed” (Musango et al., 2020, p.9). Therefore, this research 
study indicates this framework by Shrestha et al. (2020), and the variables could be used to

develop a theory for gendered energy technology innovation in poor informa; settlements. 
Also, for the data collection purposes, Smit et al. (2019) developed a tool called the 
MuSIASEM tool that is used in the research study for generating data relating to gender 
equality indicators towards achieving security of energy services in the poor informal 
settlement. 
Furthermore, access to energy in informal settlements is a concern across Africa and this is due 
to high energy tropical, low accessibility of energy infrastructure and instability in supply 
(Yvonne Chivanga, 2023). All these constraints not only stifle growth and development of the 
economy but also have a more negative impact on members of the society’s vulnerable group, 
including women who are always charged with the responsibility of managing the compound’s 
energy (Pereira et al., 2020). Problems of energy security differ in informal urban settlements 
as they occupy peripheral status and lack proper physical infrastructure. Internal electricity 
constraint, as well as inadequate access to modern energy services puts the residents to rely on 
traditional and often unsafe technologies like kerosene lamps, and biomass fuels (Anditi et al., 
2022). These energy sources not only cause impersonal domestic air pollution but also are 
dangerous to health, particularly for women and children who most of the time are confined to 
the house (Yvonne Chivanga, 2023). 
With energy costs as an added expense, the challenges faced by the households in informal 
settlement are further compounded by the fact they have to pay for the energy they consume. 
Expensive energy services compared to household income limits the quantity of energy which 
the residents are able to afford leading to a large chunk of their income going into meeting their 
energy needs bare minimum (Choi and Kwon, 2021). This economic burden prevents families 
from obtaining resources and improving their educational systems, healthcare and other crucial 
services, thus causing more suffering by continuing poverty and impartment cycles. However, 
instead of availability, if the energy supply is unreliable then it creates another challenge to 
energy security in the informal settlements. Recurrent power blackouts and interruptions 
interfere with productivity and also pose a risk to safety; these interruptions affect users reliant 
on electricity for domestic chores like cooking, heating, and lighting (Følstad, 2008). Such 
disruption affects the daily tasks which are most often carried out by women and can include 
childcare and house chores hence, it has negative consequences on their wellbeing. 
Managing these energy security challenges therefore calls for a comprehensive approach, 
where the issues of gender are duly considered, and policies put in place to support this cause.

Musango et al., (2020) further emphasise the need for integration of gender issues for energy

and access challenges of the female gender. This includes offering the people the best 
renewable energy options that are cheap, available, and efficient, so that they are not 
constrained to using conventional fuels but have a sustainable way of generating power. 
Despite the lack of tailor-made methodologies for researching and implementing innovations, 
Living Labs holds a potential to provide suitable ground for testing and development of the 
energy solutions that address challenges faced in the informal settlements. These collaborative 
platforms support community, research and design thinking gathering, co-design processes and 
the engagement of community, researchers and policymakers (Grové, 2022). Women 
themselves can get empowered to engage in the innovation processes where Living Labs sit 
aptly with an ability to contextualise the attention and care required by organisations to enhance 
the creation of solutions and services that respond to these needs. South Africa summed up the 
possibilities of Living Labs for engaging in the issue of energy security complemented with 
participative processes. Herselman et al. (2015) reflect on and bring insights regarding Living 
Labs as Futures Centre in South Africa and reflect on the role of communities and localization 
of futures. Programs like the GENS Living Lab demonstrate the involvement of stakeholders 
in the advancement of empowering and gender-sensitive energy solutions (Sphokazi Phelokazi, 
2022). 
Besides the physical and economic barriers, social and cultural factors tend to worsen the 
energy security challenges experienced by women in the informal settlements. With this, 
customary patriarchal values and unfair gender standards deny women equal participation in 
the decision-making process or avenues of economic enfranchisement (Musango et al., 2020). 
This exclusion not only narrowly constrains their capabilities as standard bearers for the 
development of energy policies but also bears direct responsibility for perpetuating poverty 
and reliance on unsound energy systems in these nations. Remedial steps alongside these 
challenges can be correlated with steps that might facilitate Women Education, Women 
Empowerment and Women in Leadership within universally driven energy diplomacy and 
sustainable development projects (McDonald, 2015). Empowering women and achieving the 
related gender equality presents thousands of communities with a new chance on fostering 
further inventive efforts towards innovation in sustainable energy for worldwide society.

Furthermore, relating to the gender-specific impacts, several issues about energy security in 
the informal settlements are awaiting the women depending on their gender issues. Out of these 
challenges, the utilisation of traditional biomass for cooking and heating is one of the most 
significant sources of risks and likely to cause serious illness to women such as indoor air 
pollution Anditi et al., (2022). This is due to poor methods in cooking and lack of proper room 
airing whenever the population relies on biomass fuels which has contributed to respiratory 
illnesses and other ailments such as a result of inadequate ventilation especially affecting 
women who spend much of their time inside the house (Musango et al., 2020). Likewise, the 
female gender is more exercise-enhanced and vulnerable to health complications during 
biomass collection and preparation processes (von Geibler et al., 2014). 
Aside from the health concern, the utilisation of energy by women in informal settlements is 
hindered by time and mobility constraints. Electricity and other modern energy services are not 
available to many women; they spend much of their time in search of fuelwood or else waiting 
in a queue to purchase cooking fuel, which from a time perspective reduces the amount of time 
women can devote to income-generating activities or to acquire education (Hossain et al., 
2019). Also, insufficient lighting to common areas and other paths leads to numerous risks 
concerning personal security at night as well as restricting women’s access and exposes them 
to the increased likelihood of domestic abuse (Sphokazi Phelokazi, 2022). These mobility 
restrictions not only limit measures that can be taken to advance women’s economic status but 
also break the cycle that many women find themselves in where poverty and marginalisation 
become the norm. 
Mitigating these gender-specific impacts requires the promotion of women-centred approaches 
in energy innovation and policy-making processes. Campos and Marín-González (2023) posit 
that it is crucial to foster women’s engagement and inclusion in policies and planning initiatives 
that affect informal settlements, with consideration given to the gendered nature of encounters 
and resource necessities. This encompasses reaching out to women as partners to take charge 
in decision-making within the energy sector and devise solutions on how the challenges faced 
by women and their preferred solutions can be addressed (Herselman et al., 2015). 
Furthermore, the interventions must include focusing the market-based interventions on access 
to clean and affordable energy services; decreasing the reliance on traditional biomass, 
especially among women; and reducing the perceived health risks related to its use (Callaghan 
& Herselman, 2015).

Therefore, there are different energy security scales related to energy security issues, and these

should be on the security of energy services from energy security (Parag, 2014), which means

described by (Jansen, 2009) as “the extent to which the population in a defined area (country

energy services of adequate quality”. 
Moreover, this research study is suggesting that strategies for addressing energy security in the

the top-down approaches (Parag, 2014). In this regard, the Living Lab technology would be 
employed to convene the stakeholders, the institutions as well as the end-users comprising of

2.2.6 Gender-focused case studies of energy technology innovation 
This section describes the energy technologies relating to gender that could be investigated 
during this research study. Renewable energy technologies are one of the main examples of 
energy product distributions and for the provision of distinctive chances for the supply of 
indispensable energy services (Musango et al., 2020). However, to achieve the necessity of 
energy services at the level of the household, it is vital to focus on more than one energy 
technology. 
Therefore, to address energy insecurity, research studies have had the propensity to focus on 
one energy technology. These research studies include Pascale et al. (2016) for the 
investigation of ‘solar photovoltaic technology’ which was led by women in Myanmar rural 
areas to achieve electricity, (Ockwell et al., 2018b) for the focus on ‘solar photovoltaic 
technology’ to achieve energy services in evolving places, and Islam et al. (2005) for the 
development of a diminutive regionalized ‘renewable energy technology’ in evolving places. 
Other energy technologies are empirical research on the investigation of ‘cooking technologies’ 
(Goswami, Bandyopadhyay and Kumar, 2017), underpinning the supply and acceptance of 
optional ‘clean cooking technologies’ (Manjula and Gopi, 2017; Karanja and Gasparatos, 
2019), and (Castán Broto, Arthur and Guibrunet, 2020) for attention on traditional biomass 
(charcoal). Nevertheless, charcoal is well known and seen as the major fuel utilised in the urban 
informal settlements in Africa (Musango et al., 2020). These technologies in the literature 
above can impact gendered energy innovations in the urban informal settlements, and they are 
given attention to politics, economy, social-cultural applications, and technical features. 
Furthermore, the energy sector of Africa has witnessed rather profound changes in recent 
decades – the continent has turned to using renewable energy sources massively. This 
transformation is informed by the fact that Africa holds, for instance, solar, wind, and 
hydroelectric power and biomass very well; in addition to this, though, there is a concern about 
energy insecurity in the continent (Adulugba, 2021). Solar energy is, therefore, one of the 
brightest stars of sustainable power generation, and Africa, in particular, receives some of the 
highest irradiation levels in the world (Abdelrazik et al., 2022). This makes it suitable for solar 
electricity production, and many solar investment areas, including large-scale utility solar and

distributed solar for off-grid villages, are observed in the country and many other African 
nations, including South Africa, Kenya, Morocco, etc (Agoundedemba, Kim and Kim, 2023). 
Wind energy is another strategic renewable energy source that holds the prospect for 
development in the African continent, especially in the areas bordering the coastal regions' 
highland climate areas. Hydropower has traditionally been used in Africa to provide renewable 
electricity with large, relatively tapped resources (Ugwu, Ozor and Mbohwa, 2022). The hydro 
potential of the African continent is considerable. So many big and small hydropower 
companies can invest in them, especially in the Congo, Nile, and Zambezi rivers. Other 
laudable projects, such as the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD) and Inga Dam in the 
DRC, seek to increase power generation, making energy a strong pillar that underpins 
integration (Tesfa, 2013). 
In developing strategies to enhance gender equality in energy distribution, one needs to focus 
on methods that will capture the energy patterns and disparities faced by women across the 
globe. Some of the gender-focused energy technologies and programs that have been put in 
place to support and empower women in the energy sector include. The invention of the solar 
home system and the solar lantern, which eliminates the use of the kerosene lamp in the 
provision of artificial light and charging of mobile phones and other small electrical appliances 
(Aghaei et al., 2020). These systems greatly help women who live in rural settings where they 
often use kerosene lamps and candles for illumination. These are the positive impacts of Solar

and, thus, make them more productive (Beyene et al., 2024). These strategies include solar 
programs, which involve training women to act as solar sales ladies, which enhances both the 
provision of solar-aided technologies and empowers women economically. 
Clean cookstoves are another smart improvement aimed at enhancing the effectiveness of 
cooking processes and lessening the emission of dangerous substances. Based on statistics, 
barbecuing or boiling foods on open fires or using simple stoves hurts the air quality within 
homes; these mostly affect women and children (Rybak and Pieśniewska, 2024). Clean 
cookstoves require less fuel, release fewer pollutants, and take less time to cook compared to 
the traditional three-stone stove; therefore, the survey showed improved women's health was 
achieved through clean stoves since they had more time on their hands (Phillip et al., 2023). 
Biogas availability has an added advantage to females since it conserves the time used in

scale energy systems provide a steady power supply for lighting, communication, and 
productive functions. Thus, mini-grids facilitate electricity access that helps women to go to

constant and viable energy supply (Kipsielei, 2020).

2.2.7 Indicators of energy poverty 
Various definitions of energy poverty have been investigated by different researchers 
throughout the world (Team and Baffert, 2015; Day, Walker and Simcock, 2016). However, 
universally, there is no accepted definition of energy poverty recently (Sadath and Acharya, 
2017). According to Kohler, Rhodes, and Vermaak (2014), energy poverty was defined as the 
absence of access to any kind of present-day energy services which could include cooking 
fuel/heating and electricity. Therefore, this research study would adopt the definition of energy 
poverty by the International Energy Association (IEA) which described energy poverty as the 
absence of present-day, clean and economical energy services that could mainly involve clean 
provisions in households and electricity (United Nations, 2014b). 
The phrase “fuel poverty” is referred to as an absence of heating fuels and the phrase “energy 
poverty” is referred to as the absence of electricity in developing countries, however, both fuel 
poverty and energy poverty tend to describe energy impoverishment in the household and 
industries. The energy poverty in research and policies champions several results and vital 
consequences. These include health problems arising from the use of biomass, air pollution in 
an enclosed place, time wastage, average incomes and productivity, lack of education for the 
women and girls because they are always available fetching firewood and above, and gender 
inequality (Day, Walker and Simcock, 2016). There is energy poverty in rural informal 
settlements and globally. This is evident due to the level of energy in the rural areas, where

2.2.8 Supporting energy access sustainability 
Theoretically, the energy steps illustrate the interrelationship between the kind of energy 
consumptions, energy income, and the transitional switch (Rahut, Behera and Ali, 2017). It is 
hypothesised that the increase in the household income leads to an increase in the household 
energy consumption shifts from the less traditional energy shape to additional present-day 
energy security services like electricity and liquid fuels. This does not stop the access to the 
traditional biomass and cleaner alternative forms of energy by the poor informal settlement 
even with the increase in income. 
In consultation with the energy steps concept, availability, energy standard and cost come from 
the consumption of traditional solid fuels, down to cleaner liquid fuels (gas and kerosene), then 
lastly to electricity consumption. However, the electricity consumption is common in 
advantageous households, while the poor households are common with the fuelwood and dung 
consumption rate. According to Day, Walker and Simcock (2016), energy services access is 
defined in a descriptive step in figure 2.3, which shows the ‘energy-arising ladder from scarcity 
to the development levels and this was developed by the IEA. As the wealth of the household 
levels increases, the rate of clean energy increases. Therefore, this illustrates the idea of the 
‘energy ladder’ showing that the present-day access to energy services provides a better level 
of energy services with reduced negative effects.

Although Gross Domestic Product (GDP) has been used as a standard to quantify energy access 
globally, it has disregarded the efficacy of the capital assets’ devaluation, satisfaction, and

however, there are still visible gender inequalities in the poor urban and rural areas, including 
among the rich and poor because of the huge impact of the laws of apartheid. It is required to 
involve both the quantitative and qualitative approaches to the energy access metrics. However, 
with constant examinations, and connection with the Gross Domestic Product (DGP), energy 
access breaches a lot of quantitative illustrations and concepts related to energy poverty 
(Khennas, 2012). 
It is of no doubt that the significance of having access to sustainable and present-day energy 
services is vital for bringing solutions to the challenges of energy poverty, climate change, and 
gender inequalities in the poor urban informal settlements. Most oil-producing countries in 
Africa (e.g., Nigeria), have energy prospects but lack full access to present-day energy forms 
like Liquid gas, natural gas, and most importantly, electricity (Khennas, 2012). This shows that 
energy prospects are not mostly the main factor for energy access, even though it is vital in 
increasing energy access, just like gender equality is also vital in increasing energy access. 
Energy prospects, just as the development of infrastructure are vital for better governance and 
sustainable development. Therefore, there should be energy access for all which is connected 
to gender equality leading to sustainable development in poor informal settlements. Therefore,

2.2.9 Women empowerment: participation of women in energy technology innovation 
There is a saying by Melinda Gates (2019, p.28) that “if you search for poverty, you will find 
women who don’t have power, and if you explore prosperity, you will find women who have 
power and use it”. Some energy research studies classify gender equality and gender challenges 
with women (Fathallah and Pyakurel, 2020), and SDG 5 also directs the issue of gender to 
women and girls. However, Musango et al. (2020, p.8) indicated that “gender does not refer to 
women or differences between men and women”, but that gender is an avenue for the 
clarification and conceptualisation of the supposed biological gendered dissimilarities in a 
certain circumstance (Nightingale, 2006). 
Therefore, empowering women in the energy industry and household is inevitable. This is as 
explained earlier that the impartial statistics on women in the positions of leadership, energy 
business, finance, politics, and the gender pay gap indicates that there is a long way to go on 
the concept of gender equality, and it is more difficult when it involves women of colour 
(Gillard and Okonjo-Iweala, 2020). Socialisation and stereotyping are some of the factors that 
form women and men including their participation in co-designing of energy technologies. 
However, this research study aims to do more than checking on the stereotyping of women and 
men and focus more on the technological, political, and structural barriers that limit women

Page | 54 
 
from being part of the decision-making during the design and testing phases of the energy 
technology innovations. These barriers which are going to be investigated in this research study 
are seen in this study as a challenge, as it prevents women from progressing in the energy 
sectors and households for the achievement of energy security. 
When women are actively involved in energy innovation processes, they improve the chances 
of creating positive change that is effective and diversified to suit the interests of most people. 
Studies have shown that incorporating female voices means occupying a seat that comes with 
fresh ideas, and uncovered knowledge and vantage points that improve the energy sector’s 
behind-the-scenes work (Habiyaremye, 2019). Thus, adopting gender mainstreaming could 
ensure more extensive engagement of women in Living Labs projects, apply their perspectives 
and expertise for co-creating energy innovations not only for the benefit of the participants but 
also for the improvement of the energy framework (Fortiş & Petcu, 2022). 
In most uses of energy, the role of women is either hidden, neglected or not given its deserved 
attention. The literature has established that women have a distinct understanding and 
information concerning energy consumption, control, and requirement as pertains to their 
homes and societies (Anditi et al., 2022). Through engaging in the energy’s innovation 
processes, such actors can assist in the discovery of other solutions that are more likely to be 
effective, affordable or suitable in the light of the target customers, especially those in the 
developing rural settlements whose access to energy is limited. 
The implementation of gender-mainstreaming in energy innovation requests conscious 
attempts to remove system barriers to women’s engagement and their contributions recognized 
and valued. According to Callaghan and Herselman (2015), it is also critical for Women’s 
Voice to ensure that the female gender feels empowered to express herself in Living Labs. It 
may consist of empowering women to take up innovation by offering training and capacity 
enhancement projects meant for women, and challenging gendered norms that decrease 
women’s engagement in innovation and a call to female engagement in leadership standings of 
advancement innovations.
