# Research Gaps

**Purpose:** to state, defensibly, what is not yet known and how this study addresses it. Gaps are derived by comparing four literatures against the empirical context, using a gap-spotting strategy (Sandberg & Alvesson 2011) — i.e. gaps are constructed by interrogating assumptions, not merely by asserting that a topic is under-researched.

> **Rule applied:** no claim of the form "no study has ever…" is made unless it is narrow enough to be defended by a documented search. Where the evidence is insufficient, the gap is stated as *"the reviewed literature does not…"* rather than *"no one has…"*.

---

## 1. Gap-spotting strategy used

Three complementary moves:

1. **Confusion spotting** — identifying where the literature disagrees or uses one term for several things (e.g. acceptance vs adoption vs uptake; energy poverty vs energy insecurity; Living Lab as method vs as organisation vs as space).
2. **Neglect spotting** — identifying what a literature overlooks (e.g. energy justice scholarship rarely engages design practice; Living Lab scholarship rarely engages African informal settlements; posthumanism rarely engages energy engineering management).
3. **Application spotting** — identifying where a theory developed elsewhere has not been extended to a new domain (posthumanist ontology → energy technology design in informal settlements).

## 2. Theoretical gaps

### G1. No posthumanist account of energy technology innovation in informal settlements

Posthumanism is well developed in education (Bozalek, Murris, Postma, Motala in South Africa), geography, feminist technoscience, design (Forlano 2017) and organisation studies. It is essentially absent from energy engineering management, and the supervisor's own paper (Aboalez 2025) is one of very few attempts to connect it to energy transition — and it does so in *engineering education*, not in settlement-level innovation.

**Gap:** there is no developed account of how distributed agency (human and non-human) shapes the design, acceptance and use of energy technologies in informal settlements.

**Addressed by:** Chapter 3 §§3.3–3.7; RQ1, RQ2, RQ3.

### G2. Energy justice theory is normatively rich but practically thin

Energy justice scholarship (distributive, procedural, recognition, restorative; cosmopolitan and comparative principles) is conceptually mature. But it is predominantly applied to *policy*, *siting conflicts* and *large-scale infrastructure*, and it rarely specifies the *design mechanisms* through which justice could be achieved at household or community scale. Sovacool and Dworkin's principles do not tell an engineer what to change in a prototype.

**Gap:** the absence of a translation from energy justice principles into design and experimentation practice.

**Addressed by:** Chapter 3 §3.6; RQ4; conceptual framework component 10.

### G3. "Acceptance" is under-theorised and over-used

The draft uses acceptance, adoption, uptake, take-up and acceptability interchangeably, and the wider literature is split between attitude-based models (TAM, UTAUT), diffusion models (Rogers; Bass), social-acceptance models (Wüstenhagen et al. 2007) and practice-based accounts. These rest on incompatible ontologies: TAM assumes an individual choosing among finished artefacts; practice theory and posthumanism assume a socio-material configuration that makes some practices possible and others not.

**Gap:** no account of acceptance that is consistent with a relational/posthumanist ontology and usable as a design criterion.

**Addressed by:** Chapter 3 §§3.4, 3.7, 3.9; RQ3, RQ4.

### G4. Systems thinking and Living Lab theory are combined rhetorically but not theoretically

"Systems Living Lab" is used in the draft and in some literature, but the *theoretical* relationship between systems thinking and Living Lab methodology is asserted rather than derived. If a Living Lab is "systemic", what follows for its boundaries, its stakeholder set, its feedback architecture and its evaluation?

**Gap:** no explicit theoretical justification for a *systemic* Living Lab, as distinct from a Living Lab that happens to use systems tools.

**Addressed by:** Chapter 3 §3.8; RQ1, RQ5.

## 3. Empirical and contextual gaps

### G5. African energy Living Labs are thinly evidenced

The Living Lab evidence base is dominated by European cases (ENoLL network, Nordic and Dutch urban living labs) and by ICT/health applications. Energy-focused Living Labs in Africa are recent and few; South African examples tend to be documented as innovation hubs rather than evaluated as energy transition instruments. The draft itself concedes the literature is "very lean" in this area (citing Mukama et al. 2022).

**Gap:** little empirical evidence on whether, how and under what conditions an energy Living Lab produces just-transition outcomes in a South African informal settlement.

**Addressed by:** RQ5; Chapters 5–6.

### G6. Informal settlements are under-represented relative to rural energy poverty

Much energy-poverty research in Africa focuses on rural areas. Informal settlements are a distinct socio-material configuration: dense, grid-adjacent but often unmetered or prepaid, insecure tenure, mixed fuel use, landlord–tenant relations, and municipal ambiguity. The draft acknowledges this (citing Azimoh et al. 2015; Eder et al. 2015; Zavratnik et al. 2019) but then repeatedly drifts into rural framing ("rural informal settlements"), which is a category error.

**Gap:** analysis of energy innovation acceptance that takes the *urban informal settlement* seriously as a socio-material context.

**Addressed by:** Chapters 2 and 5; RQ2, RQ3.

### G7. End-user knowledge is invoked but not characterised

The draft asserts repeatedly that end-users hold knowledge that designers lack, but never specifies *what kind* of knowledge (tacit, situated, practice-based, cultural, embodied), how it is elicited, or how it is translated into design decisions.

**Gap:** no characterisation of end-user knowledge in energy innovation, and therefore no basis for evaluating whether participation actually changed the artefact.

**Addressed by:** Chapter 3 §3.7; RQ2.

## 4. Methodological gaps

### G8. Participation is assumed to produce acceptance and justice

The draft's implicit causal chain — participation → acceptance → energy security → justice — is treated as self-evident. It is not. Participation can be tokenistic, can reproduce local power asymmetries, can raise expectations that are not met, and can legitimise decisions already taken. Posthumanism is useful here precisely because it directs attention to the *material* and *institutional* arrangements that constrain what participation can achieve.

**Gap:** no critical treatment of the conditions under which participation fails, or of the barriers (institutional, financial, cultural, technological) that mediate the participation–acceptance relationship.

**Addressed by:** Chapter 3 §3.7; Chapter 4 (conceptual framework, which must include barrier pathways, not only virtuous loops); RQ4.

### G9. Evaluation of Living Labs lacks justice criteria

Living Lab evaluation tends to measure innovation outputs (prototypes, patents, spin-offs, user satisfaction). There is no established evaluation framework that asks whether a Living Lab produced *just* outcomes — who was included, who bore costs, whose knowledge counted, what was distributed.

**Gap:** no justice-oriented evaluation criteria for energy Living Labs.

**Addressed by:** Chapter 3 §§3.6, 3.8; RQ5.

### G10. The claimed "systematic review" is not systematic

The draft claims a "stand-alone qualitative systematic literature review (realist review)" but reports no protocol, databases, search strings, date limits, inclusion/exclusion criteria, screening counts or synthesis method. As it stands the claim cannot be defended.

**Gap (internal to the thesis, not the literature):** the review method must either be made genuinely systematic (and reported) or re-described honestly as a critical/narrative review with a documented search strategy.

**Addressed by:** Chapter 2 revision (Phase 5).

## 6. Gaps the study should *not* claim

To protect the thesis at examination, the following claims in the current draft should be withdrawn or heavily qualified:

| Current claim | Problem | Recommended reformulation |
|---|---|---|
| "No study ... has considered reducing energy poverty through the Living Lab concepts while using the SE or SsT approaches concurrently" (p. 5) | Universal negative; no documented search | "The reviewed literature contains few accounts that combine Living Lab methodology with explicit systems thinking in energy-poverty contexts, and none identified in this review addresses South African informal settlements." |
| "None of them used the Living Lab and the systems approach to address energy poverty in South Africa's informal settlements" (p. 6) | As above, and vulnerable to a single counter-example | "The studies reviewed do not combine these three elements; the present study is positioned at that intersection." |
| "Most typologies of Living Labs ... do not have the gender or are gender blind" (p. 20) | Not evidenced by a typology-by-typology analysis | Either evidence it in a table or delete |
| Implicit: participation produces acceptance | Contradicted by literature on tokenism and non-use | Treat as a hypothesis to be tested, not a premise |

## 7. Consequence for Chapter 3

The gap analysis sets the Chapter 3 agenda precisely:

- §3.3–3.4 must supply the ontology and epistemology that the energy literature lacks (G1, G3).
- §3.6 must translate justice into design criteria (G2, G9).
- §3.7 must theorise participation *and its failure conditions* (G7, G8).
- §3.8 must derive the "systemic" in Systemic Living Lab (G4).
- §3.9 must adjudicate TAM and socio-technical theory rather than list them (G3).
- §3.11 must justify posthumanism against alternatives in exactly these terms.

A Chapter 3 that merely describes posthumanism would leave every one of these gaps unfilled.
