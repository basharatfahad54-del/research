**Primary theory:** Posthumanism (supervisor-mandated; not substitutable).
**Derived from:** D-05 §"Please note" (four mandatory elements), D-06 item 1, D-01 (Aboalez 2025), and the 13-section structure specified in the client brief.

## 1. The governing argument (one paragraph, to be tested section by section)

> Energy technologies in informal settlements do not fail or succeed because individual users decide to accept or reject them. They fail or succeed because of how they are configured within an assemblage of people, materials, infrastructures, institutions, fuels, dwellings, meters, tariffs and practices. A humanist, user-centred framework can describe what users say they want, but it cannot explain why the same technology is accepted in one street and abandoned in the next, nor why well-designed technologies reproduce the inequalities they were meant to redress. Posthumanism supplies the ontology needed to treat that assemblage as the unit of analysis, and therefore supplies the theoretical basis for a Living Lab that experiments on configurations rather than on products. Justice, on this account, is not a distribution of benefits added on at the end; it is a property of how the assemblage is composed — who and what is included in its intra-actions, whose knowledge registers as knowledge, and which agencies are enabled or suppressed. The Systems Living Lab is the institutional form through which such composition can be deliberated and tested.

Every section of Chapter 3 should advance, qualify or defend some part of this argument. If a section does not, it should be cut.

## 2. Section-by-section plan

### 3.1 Introduction (c. 1,200 words)

**Purpose.** Establish what a theoretical framework *is and does* in doctoral research, before any mention of posthumanism.

**Must contain:**
- Definition and function of a theoretical framework: a logically connected set of assumptions and concepts that determines what is observed, how it is interpreted, and what counts as an explanation.
- Why frameworks matter: they prevent description from masquerading as analysis; they make assumptions contestable; they connect the study to a tradition of scholarship; they discipline the interpretation of findings.
- Relationship to research questions, methodology, and findings — a two-way relationship, not a one-way application.
- **Theoretical vs conceptual framework** (the brief insists on this distinction): the theoretical framework is the general body of theory adopted and justified; the conceptual framework is the researcher's own model of the specific problem, derived from theory *and* literature *and* context. Chapter 3 = theory; Chapter 4 = model.
- Consequences of absent or weak frameworks (common in applied engineering management research): ad hoc variables, unfalsifiable claims, inability to generalise.

**Sources to anchor (foundational, retain regardless of age):** Grant & Osanloo (2014) on framework construction; Adom, Hussein & Agyem (2018) on the role of theory; Maxwell (2013) on conceptual frameworks; Ravitch & Riggan (2017) on reason and rigor; Creswell & Creswell on the place of theory; Sandberg & Alvesson (2011) on theorising and gap-spotting. For engineering management specifically, the supervisor's own group uses this convention in D-02 (Molepo et al. 2025, "Connection to the theoretical framework"), which is useful precedent.

**Avoid:** generic encyclopaedic paragraphs on "what is research". Every sentence must be doing work that the rest of the chapter needs.

### 3.2 Understanding theoretical frameworks in Engineering Management research (c. 1,500 words)

**Purpose.** Justify why *this* discipline needs *this kind* of theory.

**Must contain:**
- Engineering Management as a field at the intersection of technical systems, organisational behaviour, innovation management, human–technology interaction, socio-technical systems, sustainability and institutional arrangements.
- The specific difficulty: engineering management problems are simultaneously technical, social, economic and political; single-level theories (purely technical, purely behavioural) systematically mis-specify them.
- Why energy transitions are a paradigm case: they involve artefacts, infrastructures, markets, regulators, users, norms, and ecologies at once.
- The consequence for theory choice: the framework must be **multi-level and relational**, able to hold human and non-human elements in one analytical frame.
- Brief critical note: the field's dominant frameworks (TAM, Lean, complexity theory, RBV) were developed for firm-level or individual-level problems and travel poorly to infrastructural justice problems. This sets up §3.11.

**Sources:** the supervisor's framing in D-05 and D-01; socio-technical systems literature (Trist & Bamforth 1951 as origin; Geels 2002, 2011 for transitions); engineering management theory discussions; complexity (Cilliers 1998) if used.

### 3.3 Philosophical foundations of Posthumanism (c. 3,000 words)

**Purpose.** Supply the ontology, epistemology and ethics. This is the section most likely to be judged "thin" if written as a history of ideas.

**Sub-structure:**

**3.3.1 Humanism and its limits.** The humanist figure of "Man" as the measure of all things; its exclusions (who counts as fully human); its separation of culture from nature, mind from body, subject from object. Use Braidotti's critique of humanist universalism and Haraway's critique of the view from nowhere.

**3.3.2 From posthumanism to critical posthumanism.** Three distinct formations that must not be conflated:
- *Posthumanism* as a broad condition (the decentring of the human).
- *Critical posthumanism* (Braidotti) — a normative-philosophical project grounded in anti-humanism, feminism and postcolonial thought, affirming difference and rejecting false universalisms.
- *Transhumanism* — the enhancement project, which remains humanist in its values (perfectibility, control, transcendence). **Must be explicitly distinguished and rejected** as the opposite of what this study means.
- *Technological determinism* — also rejected: technology does not drive history; configurations do.

**3.3.3 Relational ontology.** Entities do not precede relations. Barad's claim that relata do not pre-exist relata-within-phenomena; Haraway's relationality; Braidotti's monistic ontology. Contrast with atomistic/individualist ontology that underlies TAM and much economics.

**3.3.4 Distributed agency.** Agency is not a property of humans but an enactment distributed across human and non-human actants. Barad's agential cut and intra-action; Latour's actants (use carefully — ANT is adjacent, not identical); Bennett's thing-power (optional). Consequences: the artefact, the meter, the fuel, the dwelling, the tariff structure all *do* something.

**3.3.5 Materiality and technological agency.** Matter is not passive substrate; it is generative. Implications for energy: a prepaid meter is not neutral infrastructure — it enacts a particular relation between household, utility and money. A biogas digester enacts relations between waste, labour, space, smell, gender and cooking practice.

**3.3.6 Ecological interdependence.** Nature–culture continuum; the Anthropocene as the material condition that makes posthumanism necessary; environmental justice as inseparable from social justice.

**3.3.7 Power, ethics and justice.** Posthumanist ethics as affirmative and relational (Braidotti's ethics of becoming; Haraway's response-ability; Barad's ethico-onto-epistemology). Power as *potestas* and *potentia* (Braidotti 2006, as used in D-01).

**3.3.8 What posthumanism is not.** Summary differentiation table: critical posthumanism vs transhumanism vs ANT vs social constructionism vs technological determinism.

**Sources:** Braidotti (2013) *The Posthuman*; Braidotti (2019) *Posthuman Knowledge*; Braidotti (2011) *Nomadic Theory*; Braidotti (2006) *Transpositions*; Haraway (1991) "A Cyborg Manifesto"; Haraway (1988) "Situated Knowledges"; Haraway (2016) *Staying with the Trouble*; Barad (2003) "Posthumanist Performativity"; Barad (2007) *Meeting the Universe Halfway*; Hayles (1999) *How We Became Posthuman*; Wolfe (2010) *What Is Posthumanism?*; Coole & Frost (2010) *New Materialisms*; Forlano (2017) "Posthumanism and Design"; Ulmer (2017) on posthumanism as research methodology. Southern African scholarship to situate the work locally: Postma (2016), Motala (2017), Bozalek and colleagues, Murris (where relevant to relational pedagogy). **All to be DOI/edition-verified before citation.**

### 3.4 Major posthumanist thinkers and their contributions (c. 3,500 words)

**Purpose.** Critical comparison, not four mini-biographies. The examiner must see that the four thinkers disagree.

| Thinker | Core concepts to develop | What it supplies this study | Where it is limited for this study |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rosi Braidotti** | Nomadic subject; posthuman knowledge; affirmative ethics; *potestas/potentia*; zoe/bios; the posthuman as a navigational tool | A normative vocabulary for justice and difference; a rationale for situated, socially relevant knowledge; the supervisor's own frame | Abstract and European in reference; weak on empirical design; says little about infrastructure or households |
| **Donna Haraway** | Cyborg; situated knowledges; partial perspective; response-ability; sympoiesis; making kin; the Chthulucene | An epistemology for situated, partial, community-generated knowledge — directly relevant to end-user knowledge; a strong basis for rejecting the "view from nowhere" of technocratic design | Deliberately refuses to prescribe method; cyborg imagery may need careful translation to energy contexts |
| **Karen Barad** | Agential realism; intra-action; apparatus; phenomenon; diffraction; ethico-onto-epistemology | The most rigorous ontology for the human–technology relation; supplies the analytical unit (phenomenon, not user); a methodology (diffraction) applicable to Living Lab analysis | Demanding physics-derived vocabulary; risk of obscurity if not carefully explained; can flatten human suffering if applied mechanically |
| **N. Katherine Hayles** | The posthuman as informational-material; embodiment; the cognitive nonconscious; technogenesis | Corrects the tendency to treat the posthuman as disembodied; insists on materiality and embodiment — crucial for households, bodies, fuel, heat, smoke | Less developed as a political/justice theory; more useful for the technology–embodiment relation than for participation |

**Critical moves to include:**
- Where they converge: rejection of the autonomous humanist subject; relationality; critique of nature/culture dualism.
- Where they diverge: Barad's realist ontology vs Braidotti's vitalist monism vs Haraway's situated pragmatism vs Hayles's media-theoretical embodiment. Do not present them as a single "posthumanist theory".
- Which concepts this study adopts and which it sets aside, with reasons. Recommend: adopt Barad's intra-action as the analytical unit, Haraway's situated knowledge as the epistemological stance, Braidotti's affirmative ethics as the normative stance, Hayles's embodiment as the corrective to abstraction.
- The supervisor's paper (Aboalez 2025) relies primarily on Braidotti and Barad; this chapter should show awareness of that lineage and extend it rather than replacing it.

### 3.5 Posthumanism and socio-technical energy systems (c. 2,000 words)

**Purpose.** Move from philosophy to energy.

**Must contain:**
- Energy systems as assemblages: generation, transmission, distribution, metering, tariffs, appliances, fuels, dwellings, practices, institutions.
- Energy access and energy vulnerability as *relational* achievements, not individual attributes.
- The material turn in energy social science: infrastructures and artefacts as participants, not backdrops.
- Environmental sustainability as internal to the assemblage, not an externality.
- Socio-technical transformation: how configurations change, and why substitution of a technology without reconfiguration fails.
- **Worked illustration** (essential): take one technology from the study — the biogas digester, or a prepaid meter — and analyse it posthumanistically. Show what the framework reveals that a TAM analysis would miss. This is the section where the theory proves its worth or does not.

**Sources:** Geels (2002, 2011); Hughes (1983); Sovacool (2011, 2014); Labanca; Wilhite on energy cultures/lifestyles; Stephenson et al. (2010) Energy Cultures; Shove on social practice; Barad/Forlano for the posthumanist layer; South African energy transition work (Molepo et al. 2025 for barriers; Musango and colleagues for energy security).

### 3.6 Posthumanism, social justice and just energy transition (c. 2,500 words)

**Purpose.** Establish the normative core, and be honest about the limits of posthumanism here.

**Must contain:**
- Just transition: origins in labour/environmental movements; expansion to distributive, procedural and recognition dimensions; the South African debate (coal phase-down, IPP transition, municipal capacity).
- Energy justice: distributive, procedural, recognition, restorative/cosmopolitan principles (Sovacool & Dworkin; Jenkins et al.; McCauley et al.; Walker & Day; Heffron).
- Energy poverty and energy security as justice issues, not merely technical deficits.
- What posthumanism **adds**: (i) extends the moral community beyond humans (materials, ecologies, future generations); (ii) reframes justice as composition of the assemblage rather than allocation of goods; (iii) makes visible forms of exclusion that are not captured by human-centric recognition (e.g. dwellings that cannot accommodate a technology; infrastructures that exclude by design); (iv) supplies a critique of the technocratic "view from nowhere".
- What posthumanism **cannot do alone**: it does not specify distributive criteria; it does not generate policy instruments; it can obscure human suffering if applied without a human-centred justice vocabulary; it has no theory of institutions. Therefore energy justice theory is retained as a **normative-operational complement**, not as a rival.
- **Explicit statement (required by the brief):** posthumanism does not automatically address every dimension of social justice.

**Sources:** Sovacool & Dworkin (2015); Jenkins et al. (2016); McCauley et al. (2013); Walker & Day (2012); Heffron & McCauley (2017); Newell & Mulvaney (2013); Swilling & Annecke (2012); Winkler (2020); South African just-transition policy literature.

### 3.7 Posthumanism and end-user participation in energy innovation (c. 2,500 words)

**Purpose.** The pivotal section for RQ2, RQ3 and RQ4 — and the place where the "end-user" construct is theorised (see `research_topic_analysis.md` §6).

**Must contain:**
- The humanist construction of the "user" in design and innovation (user-centred design, participatory design, co-design, co-creation, co-research, prototyping, testing) and its limits.
- Posthumanist reconstruction: the end-user as a participant in an assemblage; agency as distributed; the artefact as co-author of use.
- End-user knowledge: tacit, situated, embodied, practice-based, cultural. How it differs from expert knowledge, and why it is systematically excluded.
- Cultural practices and norms as constitutive of acceptance, not as "barriers" to be overcome.
- Participation and its **failure conditions**: tokenism, power asymmetry, expectation inflation, consultation fatigue, capture by local elites, participation that legitimises predetermined decisions. This is essential; the draft romanticises participation.
- Acceptance re-theorised: from intention (TAM) to emergent property of a configuration; from attitude to practice.
- Practical implication: what a posthumanist design process would do differently (e.g. prototype the *configuration*, not the product; include the meter, the tariff, the dwelling, the maintenance arrangement).

**Sources:** von Hippel (2005) democratising innovation; Sanders & Stappers (2008) co-creation; Arnstein (1969) ladder of participation (foundational, and essential for the critique); Forlano (2017); Barad (2007); Haraway (1988); Wüstenhagen et al. (2007) social acceptance; Davis (1989) and Venkatesh et al. (2003) as the position being critiqued; Shove (2003) and practice theory; South African participatory energy work.

### 3.8 Posthumanism and Systems Living Labs (c. 2,500 words)

**Purpose.** Derive the "systemic" in Systemic Living Lab, and give a philosophical account of what happens inside a Living Lab.

**Must contain:**
- Living Lab: definitions, origins (MIT PlaceLab; ENoLL), typology (provider-, utilizer-, enabler-, user-driven), core elements (real-world setting, multi-stakeholder, co-creation, iteration, experimentation).
- Systems thinking: holism, interrelationship, feedback, emergence, boundary critique (Checkland; Meadows; Midgley; Senge). The distinction between *systematic* and *systemic*.
- Why "systems thinking + Living Lab" is more than a label: systems thinking supplies boundary critique and feedback architecture; Living Lab supplies the empirical setting and the participatory mechanism.
- Posthumanist reading of the Living Lab: a controlled reconfiguration of an assemblage; an apparatus in Barad's sense (which produces the phenomena it observes — a crucial and sophisticated point); a site where agential cuts are made and can be contested.
- Implications: what counts as an "intervention", what counts as an "outcome", how to avoid the Living Lab becoming a laboratory that extracts data from a community.
- Real-world experimentation, multi-stakeholder collaboration, feedback, adaptation, scaling.

**Sources:** Leminen, Westerlund & Nyström (2012); Schuurman et al. (2015); Ståhlbröst & Holst (2012); Dell'Era & Landoni (2014); Hossain, Leminen & Westerlund (2019); Callaghan & Herselman (2015); Mukama et al. (2022); Petrulaityte et al. (2022); von Wirth et al. (2019); Checkland (1981); Meadows (2008); Midgley (2000); Barad (2007) on apparatus.

### 3.9 Supporting theoretical perspectives (c. 2,500 words)

**Purpose.** Adjudicate, do not accumulate. For each candidate: conceptual compatibility, analytical contribution, limitation, verdict.

| Perspective | Contribution | Compatibility with posthumanism | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Systems thinking** | Structure, feedback, boundaries, emergence, leverage points | High — both relational and anti-reductionist; systems thinking is however often functionalist and human-centred in its goals | **Retain as methodological-strategic complement** |
| **Socio-technical systems theory / MLP** | Levels (niche, regime, landscape), transition dynamics, alignment of actor groups | High on relationality; moderate on agency (MLP can be structuralist) | **Retain as middle-range bridge** |
| **Energy justice theory** | Normative criteria (distribution, procedure, recognition, restoration) | Partial — anthropocentric in its criteria, but necessary for operationalising justice | **Retain as normative-operational complement** |
| **Living Lab theory** | Method, participation architecture, experimentation | High — Living Labs are already relational and material | **Retain as methodological frame** |
| **Technology Acceptance Model (TAM/UTAUT)** | Individual-level predictors of intention to use | **Low** — atomistic, intention-based, artefact-taken-as-given; incompatible with distributed agency | **Retain only as a contrast/foil; do not use as explanatory framework.** Justify exclusion explicitly. |

**Required argument (per the brief):** explain why TAM is *not* adopted as a core theory, despite being the default in engineering management. The reason is not fashion: TAM's ontology (individual chooser, finished artefact, measurable intention) contradicts the study's ontology (distributed agency, configuration, practice). Using both without reconciliation would produce incoherent findings.

### 3.11 Justification for selecting Posthumanism (c. 2,000 words)

**Purpose.** Answer the examiner's question: *why this theory and not another?*

**Must contain:**
- Criteria for theory selection: explanatory power for the phenomenon; ontological fit with the research questions; capacity to handle human and non-human elements; normative adequacy for a justice study; empirical tractability; supervisory and institutional fit (state this honestly — the theory was introduced by the supervisor and is retained because it is defensible, not merely because it was assigned).
- Comparison with alternatives: TAM/UTAUT; diffusion of innovations; social practice theory; actor-network theory; capability approach; political ecology; institutional theory; systems thinking alone.
- What posthumanism explains better: why identical technologies produce different outcomes; why participation can fail; why material and infrastructural conditions matter; why justice is not an add-on.
- What it explains worse: individual motivation; measurable acceptance; policy design; institutional strategy. State these limitations plainly.
- **Verdict:** posthumanism is retained as the primary foundation because it is the only candidate that supplies an ontology adequate to the human–technology–infrastructure relation at the heart of the study; the supporting perspectives supply what it lacks.

### 3.12 Theoretical framework synthesis (c. 1,500 words)

**Purpose.** Convert the theory into propositions that the empirical work can examine.

**Proposed theoretical propositions (to be refined, not asserted):**

- **P1.** Energy technology acceptance in informal settlements is an emergent property of a socio-material configuration, not an individual decision; therefore interventions that change only the artefact will produce unstable acceptance.
- **P2.** End-user agency is distributed across people, artefacts and institutions; participation mechanisms that address only human voice will fail to shift configurations.
- **P3.** Justice outcomes depend on which actors and materials are admitted to the intra-action (who is in the room, what counts as evidence, which infrastructure is assumed fixed).
- **P4.** A Systems Living Lab functions as an apparatus that produces the very phenomena it studies; reflexivity about its cuts is therefore a condition of validity, not an optional extra.
- **P5.** Acceptance and justice are related but not identical: high acceptance can coexist with injustice (e.g. acceptance of a technology whose costs fall on those least able to bear them).

These propositions are the bridge to Chapter 4 (conceptual framework) and to the empirical chapters.

|---|---|
| **Theory tourism** — posthumanism described but never used | Every section must end by stating what the concept *does* for this study; §3.5 must contain a worked analysis |

| **Obscurity** — Baradian vocabulary used without explanation | Define every technical term at first use, in plain English, with an energy example |
| **Overclaiming** — posthumanism solves justice | §3.6 must state its limits explicitly (the brief requires this) |
| **Incoherence with methodology** | §3.3 must be written with Chapter 5 in view; the paradigm decision must be made before writing |
| **Duplication with Chapter 4** | Chapter 3 = theory; Chapter 4 = model. The model is derived, not restated |
| **Plagiarism risk** | No copied paragraphs from D-01 or any source; all synthesis original; quotations short, marked, page-referenced |
| **Fabricated citation risk** | No citation enters the draft until verified in Phase 6; placeholders marked `[VERIFY]` |
