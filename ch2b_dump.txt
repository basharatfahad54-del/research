2.3.1 The 
Living 
Lab 
definitions 
The Living Lab started to come to the limelight in early 2000 by (Markopoulos, 2000), whose 
purpose then was to try new technologies in the household erected environments. The concept 
or ideas of Living Labs has since advanced, and currently, Living Lab is found in a real-world 
context (Bergvall-Kåreborn, Holst and Ståhlbröst, 2009; Mukama et al., 2022). There is no 
standardised definition of a Living Lab concept. Thus, many definitions of the Living Lab exist, 
but for this research study, some of the identified selected definitions provided an initial 
understanding of the concept, thus, throughout the study, many definitions of the Living Lab 
will be identified. Relating to this study, the Living Lab has been defined in chapter 3 of this 
study. 
Bergvall-Kåreborn et al. (2009:1) defined the Living Lab as “an environment in which people 
and technology are gathered and in which the everyday context and user need stimulate and 
challenge both research and developments since authorities and citizens take active part in the 
innovation process.” Bergvall-Kåreborn et al. (2009) definition seems to highlight aspects 
similar to the focus of this proposed research, although the definition was not specific to the 
end-users. For this research study, the end-users will include women. 
Furthermore, one of the purposes of the Living Lab is to generate a shared sector in which 
energy designs, innovations, services, and new processes are developed and tested with the 
involvement of the end-users (women) and researchers. Therefore, the Living Lab, as defined 
by Santoro and Conte (2016:39),  is “open innovation environments in real-life settings, in 
which user-driven innovation is fully integrated within the co-creation process of new services, 
products, and societal infrastructures.” Similarly, Santoro and Conte (2016) definition have 
elements similar to the research focus. 
Herselman et al. (2009:6) gave an extensive definition of the Living Lab, which they consider 
the terms are fundamental as the “Living Labs are environments, an approach, user-driven, 
open innovation, collaborative and engagement of various stakeholders.” The part of ‘user-
driven’ and ‘open innovation’ is an essential aspect of this proposed research focus. However, 
the part of being an ‘approach’ is out of the context of this research study which was why 
further studies were done and realised that CoreLabs (2007) defined the Living Lab as a system, 

########## PDF PAGE 66 ##########
Page | 60 
 
Eriksson (2006) defined the Living Lab as a methodology or approach, while Ballon and 
Pierson, (2005); Schaffers et al. (2007) defined the living Lab as an environment. The 
researcher does not see these three definitions as oppositions but regards them to be compatible 
with the researcher's viewpoints. This is because the viewpoints of the definitions depend 
totally on the focus and context of the study. Therefore, the Living Lab as a system brings about 
the concept of the Living Lab used in this research study. This concept is the use of systems

Bergvall-Kåreborn et al. (2009) explained that the ‘environment viewpoint’ is made up of items

hand, the ‘system viewpoint’ is made up of the connection or interaction between the Living 
Lab and specific different parts. In this research study, the focus is on the systems viewpoint. 
However, the environmental viewpoint and the methodology viewpoint are also very essential 
in this research study as the phenomenon being studied also relates to these viewpoints. 
2.3.2 The 
Living 
Lab 
concepts 
Living Lab is an active and effective concept of research and development where the actual 
user of the product is involved in the process of development (Choi and Kwon, 2021). Being 
open innovation platforms focused on the users, Living Labs offers a systematic approach of 
co-creation that involves the combination of research and innovation activities in the real 
contexts of utilisation (Fortiş and Petcu, 2022). This methodology ensures that not only are the 
solutions that are being developed workable because they serve a developmental function, but 
they are also realistic and feasible, given their organisational applicability. 

########## PDF PAGE 67 ##########
Page | 61 
 
 
Figure 2.4: The Living Lab process. Source: Vicini et al. (2012:203) 
Figure 2.4 depicts the process of Living lab according to Vicini et al. (2012) outlining the 
necessary stages and interactions for creating the innovative technology in the Living Lab 
context: which further explains that the core principle underlying a Living Lab is to obtain 
direct and unmediated insights from users ideas, knowledge and experiences which is an 
approach that focuses on their everyday needs and aspirations for support through various 
products, services, or applications. In the frame of reference of Living Labs provided by Huang 
and Thomas (2021), it is worth mentioning that Living Labs emphasise the importance of 
participation and are inclusive of various forms of actors like the users, researchers, developers, 
policy makers, and business actors. This is critical due to the fact that creation, prototyping, 
validation, and iteration of innovative solutions for a wide range of challenges are best achieved 
through cooperation. Living Labs brings things a step further and directly engages end-users 
in the innovation process to ensure that the products that are being developed meet the users’ 
requirements (Herselman et al., 2015). 
Living Labs are designed on the understanding that the environment of actual life is the most 
suitable for evaluating new technologies and particular solutions (Hossain, Leminen and 
Westerlund, 2019). This approach is hence different from traditional research and development 

########## PDF PAGE 68 ##########
Page | 62 
 
strategies conducted in conventional research laboratories. For example, in a specified area of 
a Living Lab, the community becomes the place where the new solutions will be tested, 
providing a better understanding of the use and functioning of the innovation. It is necessary 
for this task to be conducted in real-life scenarios for purposes of realising available problem 
areas and fine-tuning of the solutions with respect to improved efficacy and uptake among its 
users. Living Labs initiated from the idea that innovation is a social process which involves all 
stakeholders. According to Ahmed, McGough and Mateo-Garcia (2017), Living Labs establish 
a place where stakeholders can collaborate: using their knowledge and experiences. 
User co-creation is also an important aspect when it comes to defining and implementing a 
Living Lab. This includes seeking the involvement of end-users right from the identification 
of the technology idea up to the development of the final technology product. In this way, 
Living Labs can improve upon the existing solutions, by designing and adapting solutions 
according to the user’s experiences and concerns shared by the target group. Precisely this 
focus within users not only makes the innovations more relevant and easier to use but also 
increases the probability of the adoption of these innovations. 
Also, as a result of their philosophy, Living Labs encourages an iterative development approach 
(Huang and Thomas, 2021). Solutions are gradually iterated when users give feedback or when 
it is determined that solutions do not perform as expected in real life (Callaghan and Herselman, 
2015). This cyclical process makes it possible to develop solutions at a very fast rate while they 
are tested and improved as the process continues to the end and the final solutions have to stand 
as the best. It is most effective in situations where there are many subtle factors involved such 
as a complex problem that needs many contemporary and varied solutions to resolve it. Living 
Labs also play the role of a connector between various sectors and disciplines and therefore 
enable interdisciplinary cooperation (Leal Filho et al., 2022). Since Living Labs involve people 
from different disciplines like technology, social sciences and policy, problem solving can be 
viewed and tackled from various perspectives at the same time as opposed to traditional 
perspectives where a problem is solved strictly from a single angle (Tercanli and Jongbloed, 
2022). This is a relevant approach since issues like energy poverty are social problems, which 
also have a technical and economic component. 
Referring to the areas of energy technology innovation, Living Labs creates the possibility to 
build and install solutions directly into contexts (Mbatha and Musango, 2022). Living Labs can 
increase accountability by involving community stakeholders, especially women, during the 

########## PDF PAGE 69 ##########
Page | 63 
 
innovative process to guarantee energy solutions that look into the needs and limitations of the 
informal settlements (Sphokazi Phelokazi, 2022). This approach ensures that the solutions that 
are provided are easy to use and will help the said communities achieve proper energy access 
and security (Fortiş and Petcu, 2022). Furthermore, gender equality may be a key priority for 
Living Labs when it comes to energy innovation (Botreau & Cohen, 2020). When women are 
included in the innovation process that Living Labs brings, as users and actors, access to them 
in the innovation process can guarantee that women’s voices and needs are being heard. This 
is in conformity with the principles of the reviewed United Nations’ Sustainable Development 
Goal 5 (SDG5), which envisions the promotion of gender equality and women’s fair treatment 
(Giannetti et al., 2020).

Basic Living Lab concept in education 
The education Living Lab concept (Callaghan and Herselman, 2015), describes the initiation 
procedure of a Living Lab in education. The explanation of this Living Lab in education was 
done by a case study, context process, and the data collected was done through a qualitative 
nature. These will become the basis for testing the energy Living Lab relating to this research 
study. 
Context of the education Living Labs 
To address the challenges facing the education system in Southern Africa, there was a need for 
a research unit or a hub that could be used to improve participatory, innovative sustainable 
research in the education sector. This hub was established as the Living Lab concepts with the 
support of the Living Lab Institute of South Africa (LLiSA) for an interdisciplinary research 
space where participants, students, and teachers can carry out research and bring about 
solutions to the challenges facing the educational environment (Callaghan and Herselman, 
2015). 
Establishment process of the education Living Lab 
The establishment of the education Living Lab was done by the process of the appreciative 
inquiry process (Callaghan and Herselman, 2015). According to Bushe (2013:1), “Appreciative 
Inquiry is a method for studying and changing social systems (groups, organisations, 
communities) that advocates collective inquiry into the best of what is to imagine what could 

########## PDF PAGE 71 ##########
Page | 65 
 
be followed by the collective design of a desired future state that is compelling and thus, does 
not require the use of incentives, coercion or persuasion for planned change to occur.” 
The appreciative inquiry also promotes the Living Lab by relating to the central concept of 
‘open innovation,’ ‘user-driven,’ ‘co-design’ and multidisciplinary (Callaghan and Herselman, 
2015). Therefore, the process of establishing the Living Lab in education is in different phases, 
and these are briefly described below with examples (Callaghan & Herselman, 2015:6-7). 
These phases will be suitable for this research study and during the initial stages of the energy 
Living Lab to be developed. 
Phase 1 – Dream 
This is the point where the purpose and structure of the Living Labs are established and 
involves the identification of stakeholders, discussions/presentations, and workshops. In this 
phase, the participants dream about the chances of collaborative research with the users or other 
participants relating to education challenges. The focus in this phase is on innovative 
contributions, which will involve an extensive selection of stakeholders and the end-users. 
Phase 2 – Design 
In this phase of the education Living Lab, there was a finalised documentation to conclude on 
the design of the emerging Living Lab. In this phase, the purpose, mission, vision, and structure 
will be refined, and the approval of the recognised bodies or institutions (case studies) will 
commence. 
Phase 3 – Develop 
In this phase, final documentation and presentation of the proposal were done, and approvals 
were derived, which assisted in the initialisation of collaborative networking with the 
stakeholders and users. The stakeholders and users made their contributions towards the 
development of the Living Lab, and the process of establishing the Living Lab as a hub or unit 
continued all through the design and development phase. 
Phase 4 – Apply 
In this phase, the Living Lab paradigm continued as an “experimental application, and the 
Living Lab structure was developed” (Callaghan & Herselman, 2015:7). 
Phase 5 – Implement

In this phase, the implementation of the Living Lab of Education started, and the completion 
was recognised, which led to the launch of the Living Lab. Then the stakeholders and end-
users collaborated to expand and generate more ideas to improve the system. 
Phase 6 – Review 
This phase is a continuous one promoted by the reflection of an idea generated from the 
participants, which led to the evaluation of the programs and adaptations of the activities or

Utilizer-driven Living Labs 
This type of Living Lab is usually short-lived or in the form of a project-based because they 
usually contend for speedy results and supports research context and co-design with end-users 
(Schuurman et al., 2015). The focus here is to produce and test new products and services 
because they are launched to develop businesses. However, the utilizers make use of the Living 
Lab as a planned tool to investigate their product and services on the users to be in a central 
position before the products are developed (Schuurman et al., 2015). 
Therefore, this type of Living Lab seems to resemble the focus of co-designing, which requires 
end-users to take part in the decision-making activities before the development of the products, 
and this is in line with the research study. 
User-driven Living Labs 
Unlike the utilizer-driven Living Lab, the “user-driven Living Labs” are long-lived since they 
are developed in or around the end-users environments (Schuurman et al., 2015), and this kind 
of Living Labs are quite uncommon because it might be a result of the enabler-driven Living 
Lab (which is discussed below), where the end-users are required and propelled to participate 
in the innovation activities, although it could be challenging to start this type of Living Lab 
from scratch by the community end-users themselves  (Leminen et al., 2012). 
This type of Living Lab benefits the users more and benefits the stakeholders indirectly because 
the focus is co-created for the end-users mainly and established by end-users with a focus on 
solving everyday environmental life problems (Schuurman et al., 2015). This type of Living 
Lab shows promises of how the SETI Living Lab would look like. 
Enabler-driven Living Labs 
The enabler-driven Living Lab is also a long-lived one that involves the public sector actors, 
non-governmental sectors, towns, and municipalities whose aim is to carry out projects to 
produce social innovations or environmental developments (Schuurman et al., 2015). From 
table 2.4 above, the observation is that the “enabler-driven Living Labs” are similar to the 
knowledge sharing and collaboration activities which indicates that the central part here is the 
creation and dissemination of information and knowledge through the stakeholders and end-
users involved in the system (Schuurman et al., 2015). 

########## PDF PAGE 76 ##########
Page | 70 
 
Therefore, the SETI Living Lab also intends to create and disseminate knowledge gained from 
the participation of the end-users and those involved in the energy technology innovation 
processes and not mainly from the stakeholders. 
Provider-driven Living Labs 
The “provider-driven Living Labs” aims at improving the lives of the users by allowing all 
participants in the system to gain from the outcome of the innovation, which most of the 
innovation looks at producing candid knowledge and information for all involved in the system 
(Schuurman et al., 2015). These providers can be those in public and private organisations who 
participate in the “Living Lab” to co-design new products, services, and bring solutions to the 
needs of the industry. 
Some “provider-driven Living Labs” are established as long-lived platforms for innovation and 
aiming to advance research development of theory, expanding the creation of knowledge and 
bringing about specific solutions to problems (Schuurman et al., 2015). Observing table 2.4 
above, the provider-driven Living Labs falls into the Original American type of Living Lab, 
which uses a lab infrastructure for the study of end-users in an environment, like the urban poor 
environment. The testbed Living Lab can also fall into the Provider-driven Living Labs, where 
the “technical testbed infrastructure” is used to develop and test “innovative ICT” services 
(Schuurman et al., 2015). 
However, Schuurman et al. (2015, p.5-6) state that Leminen et al. (2012) argue that “Living 
Labs are usually either utilizer-driven or provider-driven because both emphasise efficiency 
with regards to the firm's investments, our observation complements this statement, as Living 
Labs can be a provider- and utilizer-driven, depending on the focus of the analysis (case-based 
or infrastructure-based).” This type of Living Lab is inversely related to the proposed SETI 
Living Lab. 
Therefore, the typologies of the Living Labs have been identified, however, most of them lack 
the aspect of gender. The argument remains that the existing Living Lab typologies are gender-
neutral, which means they focus on users without incorporating the gender aspect. They also 
do not incorporate the systems approach into the development processes. Therefore, this 
research study will contribute by developing the SETI Living Lab typology, which focuses on 
users incorporating the gender aspect with the use of systems approach.

2.3.5 Living

in 
the

Living Labs have become an indispensable tool for advancing innovations in the energy field, 
as they can serve as an experimental model for developing and implementing energy solutions 
within real-life contexts (Mbatha and Musango, 2022). This approach ensures that the 
developed technologies are not only technologically feasible, but it is also realistically possible 
and would focus on the end-users. Thus, when addressing energy needs throughout different 
contexts including rural informal settlements, energy Living Labs result in responsible 
innovation that has been grounded on the principles of inclusivity, reflexivity and 
sustainability. 
In the energy sector, Living Labs can be defined as a facility, venues where stakeholders such 
as end-users, researchers, from the industry, policymakers and community organisations work 
in cooperation to create and with the help of experiments, test energy solutions (Tercanli and 
Jongbloed, 2022). This participatory approach is essential because the innovations would in 
real sense come with realities for the targeted users, especially individuals in the underserved 
markets. Living Labs include people directly in the innovation process, capturing vital ideas 
and input that traditional research settings may miss (Campos & Marín-González, 2023). 
The energy efficiency projects implemented in Living Labs can have many benefits, one of 
them is pre-implementation of activities in real-life conditions (von Geibler et al., 2014). This 
implies that energy technologies can be subjected to usage conditions before requiring 
complete use with the real world, making the tests more realistic, practical and revealing 
dependability, practicality and benefits. For example, new technologies in renewable energy 
like solar energy or wind energy devices can be installed in societies in order to assess the 
adaptability of the technology in offering permanent and sustainable energy. In the following 
deployments of these technologies, information collected from the real world can then be used 
to enhance them and adapt them to suit the needs of the users (Fortiş & Petcu, 2022). 
In the context of energy Living Labs, Campos and Marín-González (2023) emphasise the 
necessity of being responsible for the innovation processes that are implemented. It implies a 
situation whereby all parties can contribute their quota towards the generation, development, 
and marketing of the innovation, preferably, women. This is of particular concern whenever 

########## PDF PAGE 86 ##########
Page | 80 
 
energy poverty also comes into the picture, such as when women are mainly responsible for 
managing energy, fuel, and lighting at home but are rarely involved in decision-making about 
energy sources (Botha et al., 2012). In this Case, Living Labs could involve more Women in 
co-creation as well as testing of energy technologies to ensure products with satisfactory 
solutions to their problems are designed. In the Energy Living Labs area, reflexivity is 
understood as the dynamic self-reflectiveness of the innovation processes (von Wirth et al., 
2018). This means that as in any innovation process the ongoing evaluation of the energy 
solutions and of the process of innovation has to be done, in order to fine tune the energy 
solutions and the process to fit the user needs. This cyclical action of thinking and modifying 
guarantees that the changes remain appropriate and efficient throughout the period (Herselman 
et al., 2010). 
Another principle is sustainability where energy Living Labs aim to develop solutions that can 
be managed in such a way as to protect the environment and preserve resources for future 
generations (Callaghan and Herselman, 2015). This relates not only to the creation but also to 
the long-term effectiveness of innovation solutions based on environmentally friendly energy 
technologies. Sustainable innovation in energy Living Labs aims at maintaining economic, 
social, and environmental aspects while seeking to meet overarching sustainability objectives 
of energy production and use like reduction of emission levels and more equality in energy 
access (Ersoy & Van Bueren, 2020). The figure 2.7 below extracted from Veeckman et al

2.3.6   Conceptualising systems Living Lab: Living Lab, energy Living and systems approach 
The systems energy technology innovation Living Lab (SETILL) will be developed in chapter 
three of this study, which will lead to the final conceptualization of the systems Living Lab. 
The SETILL is the link between the Living Lab, energy Living Lab and systems approach (SD 
and SE). Further details of the systems approach used in the SETILL will be presented in the 
Level 3 section of this literature review chapter. 
Systems Living Labs can be characterised as a new paradigm of innovation and problem 
solving, especially useful for use in modern society, where problems are often closely 
connected. Systems Living Labs differ from the previous ones as the latter are applied to single 
technologies or solutions for complex issues within complex and constantly changing 
environments (Hossain, Leminen, & Westerlund, 2019). Thus, the systems Living Lab will be 
developed in this study as a Living Lab used to solve energy challenges with the use of a 
systems approach. This approach accepts that problems that exist in real life occur within the 
context of connected webs of multiple associates that are interdependent, such that actions 
taken in one part of the web influence and affect the rest of the web (von Wirth et al., 2018). 

########## PDF PAGE 90 ##########
Page | 84 
 
Therefore, in the SETILL, a systems thinking researcher, expert or academic must be involved 
as one of the stakeholders who would understand the systemic variables or models involved 
for the development of energy innovations. 
 
Figure 2.8: The Living Lab process and systems approach: Source: Yasuoka et al. (2018:135) 
Figure 2.8 displayed from Yasuoka 2018 and his perspective on the Living Lab linking with 
the systems approach highlights some of the specifications of Living Labs as previously 
discussed, to prioritise problem identification through stakeholder dialogue and the learning 
process. This approach shares several similarities with Soft Systems Approaches (SSA). 
However, unlike SSA, which explores a problem space for solutions, Living Labs focuses on 
the concrete interactions of services and products (Yasuoka et al., 2018). Therefore, to logically 
implement the Living Labs approach, it is necessary to use models that not only capture 
stakeholders' subjectivity but also describe the concrete features and systems of the service 
(Yasuoka et al., 2018).  
Furthermore, the structural model for Living Labs closely resembles those used in Hard 
Systems Approaches (HSA) (Yasuoka et al., 2018). Consequently, it is crucial for Living Labs, 
as a method for addressing social challenges, to incorporate both SSA and HSA models and 
methodologies, as they complement each other (see Figure 2.8). 
The emphasis in Systems Living Labs is therefore to foster cooperation from different 
stakeholders, including academia, systems thinking professionals, industry, government, and 
civil society. Interdisciplinary teamwork is highlighted by Callaghan and Herselman (2015), 
indicating that this multiplicity of specialties brings a diversified perception and approach to 
probing and analysing complex innovative challenges. For positioning multiple stakeholders, 

########## PDF PAGE 91 ##########
Page | 85 
 
systems Living Labs enhances the provision of contextually consistent and corroborative 
interventions that respond to the needs and barriers within the system (Leal Filho et al., 2022). 
In addition, Systems Living Labs are based in contexts where they offer possibilities to 
implement and adjust solutions in as close to real-life situations as possible. This approach is 
in contrast to the conventional elements used in laboratories, in distinct ways, and interventions 
from the realities and associated risks of daily life (Motloung, 2022). The Living Lab directly 
interacts with the problem environment, which leads to the creation of the empirical and 
practical data that are critical for making a proper policy decision and designing relevant 
interventions (Musango et al., 2020). Therefore, Systems Living Labs for social innovation 
play a role in transforming the system by providing fields for experimentation and learning. 
According to Van Rensburg et al. (2014), this Lab helps to support repetitive attempts at 
experimentation with new ideas that positively help cultivate on-going learning or refinement 
processes. Thus, in Living Labs, knowledge is created through experimentation, where the 
efficiency of interventions is proven and the presence of unanticipated outcomes and new 
features inherent in the system is revealed (Choi & Kwon, 2021). 
Integrative approach and applications to energy solutions 
Systems approaches with Living Labs are a full-spectrum approach to energy issues affecting 
informal settlements. Systems thinking offers a framework for approaching energy systems 
more comprehensively because it looks at the larger picture of how all the parts in the system

of procedures for developing and implementing systems more efficiently. These two systems 
approaches were applied to develop the SETILL model for this research study. 
As expressed by Mulugetta, Ben Hagan and Kammen (2019), integration is helpful as it 
encompasses several advantages, which include the following : 
1. To address the problem of a lack of integrated approach by ensuring that energy 
solutions cannot be developed without considering socio-economic and environmental 
implications. This makes it easier in the long run to develop more sustainable and 
stronger systems of energy that would be more suited to suit the needs of society. 
2. To lead to innovation by incorporating the end-users in the co-design activities. It 
makes these solutions more relevant and acceptable in the scope by involving the key 
stakeholders.

3. The aspects of Living Labs which include real-life testing where technologies can be 
tested and modified to fit the end users. 
To foster this integrated approach, growing the recognition and understanding of the multiple 
intersecting roles and responsibilities of all organisations and agencies involved, as well as 
the processes and the objectives for co-design and co-production and the mechanisms for 
assessing and evaluating the strategies and approaches, must be established. Some crucial 
processes in this approach include the identification of the stakeholders, the active 
involvement of the users who identified as the stakeholders, the design of the prototypes, the 
implementation of the prototypes in real-world settings, and the final refinement of the 
solutions (Coulentianos et al., 2022). It focuses all the possibilities of solutions, makes them 
technologically practical and sociopolitically acceptable which engenders more relevance and 
sustainability.

settlements. Systems Living Labs could also construct summed up strategies for innovation 
founded on systems thinking as the means to produce an area in which stakeholders 
collectively create, collectively experience, and collectively transform innovative answers for 
energy demand. The same approach also locates relevancy and effectiveness of innovations 
as well as community enablement (Musango et al., 2020). Stakeholders within the vicinity of 
the Systems Living Labs include inhabitants, traders, civil society organisations, as well as 
relevant government authorities, who participate in the progressive creation of localised 
energy solutions (Hossain et al., 2019). Being engaging in developing such solutions assist in 
ensuring that the solutions this technology needs are accurate and plausible depending on the 
application of the technology and the ability of the people in the community to accept the 
change or willingness to embrace the change. Nonetheless, the cyclically systematic nature of 
System Living Labs means that some energy solutions can be tweaked over time depending 
on the outcome from actual real-life practice cases (Habiyaremye, 2019). This means that 
implementation risks that may exist within the local context are properly controlled and at the 
same time, the scalability and replicability of innovation are achieved (Choi and Kwon 2021). 
It also affords the demarcation of realistic concerns that may obtain with some innovations 
from the incubation phase thus ensuring nearly total optimality once the proposed innovations 
are put into practice (Herselman et al., 2015).

In addition, Systems Living Labs involve and welcomes all the stakeholders and concentrate

Systems Living Labs contain a focus on testing energy innovations in real-life situations where 
the concepts are supposed to operate (Rezaee, 2022). Therefore, the SETILL will be tested in 
the case study identified in this study. This approach is essential for making sure that adopted 
or to-be-adopted technologies are feasible, sustainable, and adequate for addressing the needs

of final consumers (Rezaee, 2022). Combining concepts of real-world testing and an agile 
development methodology, will allow feedback to inform ongoing improvement and

The assessment of proofs in Systems Living Labs contexts consists of experimenting with 
typical energy-focused innovations in real-life contexts adequately (Huang and Thomas, 2021). 
However, the importance of image processing in applications implies their differences from 
typical lab experiments, which commonly do not consider actual conditions of operation. As

of issues that have not been foreseen during the testing and the data that is gathered about users’ 
interactions with the technology. It enables the fine-tuning of the solutions in order to best fit 
the tastes of the end-users, since all organisations’ applications are designed to improve the 
end-users’ quality of life. 
The iterative development process is an important part of Systems Living Lab methodology, 
in which a few iterations of development that are based on the same logical framework are 
made (Leal Filho et al., 2022). This process encompasses a cycle of prototyping the new 
communication technology, testing it with users, gathering feedback, and improving on it. It 
fits within the plan, do, check, and act (PDCA) cycle, where each step focuses on modifying 
the design and operation of the energy technology that was reviewed in the previous step. 
Campos & Marín-González (2023) show that this approach encapsulates a culture of learning 
and innovation assurance to guarantee that the final product is well adopted by the local context 
and users. The identification of stakeholders and end-users as participants in the development 
process is indispensable to the success of energy innovations. In the case of Botha et al. (2012), 
the authors underscore that adopting the co-design and co-testing of the solutions makes it 
possible not only to arrive at technically feasible solutions but also to evaluate the social 
acceptability of the solutions. 
Benefits and challenges of real-world testing and interactive development 
Field testing in the actual informal settlements used as case study assures that the energy 
innovations are functional and applicable to the contexts that the various informal settlements 
present. As Anditi et al. (2022) pointed out, this approach is beneficial as it originates from the 
contextualization of the problems and comes up with solutions for them considering the 
hardships of the community; for instance, they have to work with limited resources and

restrictions on accessible infrastructure. Proliferation methods in the development of such 
technologies involve the incorporation of continual feedback from the targeted users to 
improve the chances of acceptance and usage of the technology (Mbatha and Musango, 2022). 
From the literature, Choi and Kwon (2021) explained that when users are presented with the 
fact that their feedback is being implemented in the design, they are likely to accept the 
technology. Generally, solutions that are cultured and designed through experiences and 
subsequent feedback are much more likely to be transferable across different contexts and, 
hence, easily scalable. Ersoy and Van Bueren (2020) highlight that systems Living Lab can 
also enable the creation of a variable and more resilient infrastructure that may be expanded or

As highly flexible learning environments, however, systems Living Lab real-world testing and

development involves ongoing commitment and investment and can be a challenge even in 
organisations that have relatively easy access to resources when operating in contexts in which 
resources are limited (Motloung, 2022). However, such challenges are most surmountable by 
first having proper strategies for outlining relationships and expectations, as well as the overall 
flow of operations, and second, designing a proper organisational structure for handling data. 
Grové (2022) also notes that the implementation of the iteration approach needs to be 
orchestrated based on a clear methodology and accompanied by support frameworks.

2.4.1 The systems approaches for this research study - SsT (SD) and SE 
Researchers like Kalnins and Jarohnovich (2015); Behl and Ferreira (2014) have attempted 
definitions of systems thinking, but the term ‘systems thinking’ was initially invented by Barry 

########## PDF PAGE 101 ##########
Page | 95 
 
Richmond (1994) who refers to the demand for interdependence of systems thinking. Systems 
thinking could involve a trans-disciplinary approach from natural sciences, social sciences, and 
philosophy. According to Giddings, Hopwood, and O’Brien (2002), the three spheres of 
sustainable development which are “economic, environmental and social” are seen as “nested” 
within each other and not seen as separate views. Therefore, the systems approach that 
acknowledges the developing properties, complexity, and relationships should be undertaken 
to combine these three spheres of sustainability (Hardi and Zdan , In Giddings et al. 2002). 
This is because the systems approach is mostly preferred to the analytical approach due to its 
relationship to connections, context, and relationships (Gallopín, 2003). 
Systems thinking could bring about a solution to the problem of this research as it approaches 
problems and practicability as concurrently dynamic and multidimensional (Blewitt, 2012). 
Therefore, systems theory or systems thinking creates an integrated explanatory framework 
that considers different interactions as systems in the world and not as a single agent (Clayton 
& Radcliffe, 1996). Furthermore, to technically build innovation in the social and economic 
discipline, it would be necessary to apply the systems thinking approach to the process of the 
energy system. This is because the energy system has different features and functions as a 
synergy with diverse systems but intending to ensure services of energy delivery to the -users 
(women in this context). Therefore, the rapid growth of technology innovation emerges as 
constant complex systems that are dependent on the existing systems. This is related to global 
growth which increases the complex world’s social network through economic feedback loops 
(Arnold and Wade, 2015). Therefore, the systems thinking approach would allow for the 
understanding of the primary causes leading to the complex behaviour and dynamism, which 
allows for the forecasting of the causes and the adaptation of the effects. 
Arnold and Wade (2015) conceptualise SsT as a collection of synergistic analytical skills 
employed to enhance the ability to identify and comprehend systems, predict their behaviours, 
and devise modifications to achieve desired outcomes.

As illustrated in Figure 2.10, the primary processes of a Systems Thinking (SsT) approach start 
with the identification of elements, recognition of purpose, and comprehension of 
interconnections within a group of synergistic components known as a system. Gaining insights 
into the dynamics and behaviour patterns involves distinguishing stocks, variables, and flows, 
as well as identifying non-linear relationships within the system. Following this, deciphering 
complexity through conceptual modelling enhances the understanding of reinforcing 
behaviours and feedback mechanisms at various levels. This process, in turn, deepens 
comprehension of the system and solidifies concepts and patterns. The system's objective is 
achieved by unravelling complexity and understanding, predicting, and adjusting feedback 
mechanisms to yield effective outcomes. Within these key processes, new processes can be 
added or removed to improve the understanding of system behaviour. These processes create 
a behaviour pattern within the system that can be either 'reinforcing' or 'dynamic' in nature. 
Furthermore, the systems thinking approach that would be used for the study to answer the 
research question is the system dynamics modelling. System dynamics modelling, a particular 
approach used for system thinking, would be used in this study to achieve the desired purpose 
of the study. “The systems approach represents a broad view, taking all aspects into account 
and concentrating on interactions between different parts of the problem” (Checkland 1981, 
p.5). A system in this study is not a physical, objective entity, such as a nuclear power station

would be considered a system, the understanding of a system is as a conceptual idea for viewing

which may not or may plot onto reality; thus, it is a notation which is a choice of the viewer 
and a contribution to epistemology before the contribution of the ontology.” 
Within the range of methodological approaches for systems inquiry, system dynamics 
modelling is a technique that provides insight into the behaviour of complex systems and as 
noted by Wolstenholme (1985) encompasses the feedback or adaptive effects of such systems. 
System dynamics, according to Brent et al. (2018), is seen as a process that aims for the 
consideration of most of the possible impactful elements in a system which initiates their 
connections mainly through modelling. The theory of gender as an approach or procedure

Another systems approach used in the SETILL is systems engineering (SE). The

activities, including the system's duration, influenced by its nature, complexity, and function.

and maintenance until the system's purpose is fulfilled, and finally, it is retired and disposed 
of at the end of its life. This means that at each level of operation, the system design permits 
change towards a better result in totality of the system results (Esa 2006). This was 
particularly relevant in this research study as changes occurred at every stage of the SETILL 
development towards the biogas technology.

2.4.2 Biogas 
technology 
Biogas technology is a relatively new way of harnessing waste to produce renewable energy 
that is referred to as biogas, which is made of methane and carbon dioxide in large proportions 
(Luostarinen & Normak, 2011). This conversion is referred to as anaerobic digestion, and it 
entails the decomposition of organic inputs like agricultural waste, manure, municipal waste, 
plant biomass, sewage, and food waste using microorganisms that do not require oxygen 
(Luostarinen & Normak, 2011). The outcome is biogas, which can be used as fuel for cooking, 
heating, and the generation of electricity, thus acting as a sustainable and renewable source of 
energy. In the study area, Uganda, biogas is an ideal technology since it solves waste disposal 
problems in most areas with poor waste management systems in the informal settlements as 
well as serves the purpose of an energy source (Sphokazi Phelokazi, 2022). Due to its 
versatility, this technology can be applied more frequently to independents and communities, 
which are often prevalent in squatter camps. Since biogas technology manages to convert waste 
into energy, we can minimise the amount of waste and the risks associated with pollution. 
Mukumba, Makaka, and Mamphweli (2016) argue that biogas technology is another promising 
technology able to help South Africa overcome the shortage of energy sources and record the 
significance of waste management. However, they stress the need to establish adequate policies 
and a conducive environment that will encourage the rapid deployment of this technology. 
Secondly, they encouraged that communities should embrace the projects and be informed, as 
this will increase the chances of the successful advancement of biogas projects. 
Biogas digesters operate based on providing a specific environment where anaerobic 
microorganisms will break down organic matter (Achinas and Euverink, 2020). The process 
involves several stages: First, the process involves hydrolysis; second, it involves acid 
oogenesis; third, it involves acetogenesis, followed by methanogenesis. Various complex 
organic compounds get resolved or split into soluble molecules in hydrolysis. The process, 
often called acidogenesis, transforms these molecules into volatile fatty acids (Ramos-Suarez 
et al., 2021). In acetogenesis, these acids are further degraded to acetic acid, hydrogen, and 
carbon dioxide. Lastly, the methanogenesis of the products creates methane and carbon-dioxide 
to form biogas. The direct utilisation of biogas is applicable as fuel for cooking and heating or 
can be upgraded to electricity. This is in line with this research study. 
These biogas plants or systems vary depending on the kind of system and circumstances 
involved. For small-scale producers, family-sized digesters include fixed-dome or floating-

########## PDF PAGE 111 ##########
Page | 105 
 
drum systems installed in rural houses on average (Rasimphi et al., 2022). These systems are 
cheap & appropriate for handling organic waste from homes and animal manure. Small-scale 
digesters are suitable for single farm holdings or even small communities, while larger-scale 
digesters are suitable for larger volumes of organic waste (Huber, 2019). Industrial-scale 
digesters are used in industries, municipalities, and large farming operations. Large-scale 
digesters are planned for extensive production, waste management, and farming communities 
(Budiman, 2021). These systems are usually more comprehensive and may incorporate other 
sub-systems for purification and storage of the biogas, thus making them ideal for large-scale 
energy generation.

Energy needs and challenges in the informal settlements: current energy access and usage 
patterns 
Energy access and use or emerge as a factor deeply unequal in diagrams and largely 
dependent on traditional energy resources in the investigated IHs (Oswald et al., 2020). Most 
of the inhabitants living in these settlements rely on biomass energy which include firewood 
and charcoal and kerosene among others. These sources are commonly used for cooking, 
heating and lighting since these are cheaper and more easily accessible to people compared to 
today’s energy forms such as electricity and liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) (Puzzolo et al., 

########## PDF PAGE 119 ##########
Page | 113 
 
2019). Biomass fuels, however, come with a number of difficulties; they are not efficient and 
pose health issues from indoor pollution. 
Apparently, the energy utilization profiles in the IECs tend to be associated with the 
demographics and income standards of the households and existing energy supplies. Usually, 
the energy use is low given that only a small proportion of the population has access to 
modern energy services and owing to the high energy–income ratio (Jeuland, Biggs, & 
White, 2021). Majority of households only use energy for cooking and limited lighting hence 
leaving little power for other uses which are refrigeration, communication and entertainment 
(Aliero et al., 2021). This limited energy consumption limits economic growth and 
development and) nullifies quality of lives, keeping the community stuck in poverty. 
Some disparities in energy access become very noticeable in the informal settlements 
especially because development infrastructure in such areas is usually limited compared to the 
developed urban and rural areas. The absence of legal electricity relationships and secure 
energy contributes to making many families poor (Adom et al., 2021). On this regard, this gap 
is worsened by the fact most of these settlements are informal and rarely included in the 
national energy planning and policies (Kovacic et al., 2020). Prospects remain bleak for 
residents to attain overall energy security and end-users are forced to use costly and unreliable 
means of power in their homes. 
Socioeconomic 
and 
environmental 
impacts 
A priority that is linked with energy poverty in induced informal settlements has far reaching 
socioeconomic implication. Socio economically, energy affects household usage of income 
whereby a lot of money is utilized to cater for energy thus reducing the income available to pay 
for education health and other basic facilities. Dependence on kerosene and charcoal as source 
of energy is very expensive and since they depend on market forces their prices are also subject 
to change hence the economic vulnerability of the households (Ang’u, 2023). Also, very few 
people can afford a reliable source of energy and energy prices are very high which derails 
investment in income-generating activities hence slows the economic growth and poverty 
eradication. 
Energy poverty is a major issue having crippled health implications as well as adverse effects 
on the environment. The burning of biomass fuels to cook and heat causes emissions and fumes 
that endanger people’s indoor health by giving them diseases related to the respiratory system. 

########## PDF PAGE 120 ##########
Page | 114 
 
Compared to outdoor pollution, indoor pollutants resulting from the use of traditional ways of 
cooking were severely cited for having caused the loss of millions of lives every year, adversely 
affecting women and children since they spend most of their time indoors (Dida et al., 2022). 
Firewood and charcoal are an environmental nuisance because of the provision of raw materials 
for fuel, leading to deforestation and further land degradation, supporting structures 
undermining the ecosystems, and enhancing climate change (Leal Filho et al., 2021). These 
environmental impacts also pose further risks to communities' livelihoods, specifically those 
reliant on natural products. 
Thus, the effects of energy poverty vary across genders; this is especially the case with informal 
settlements. The impact of energy scarcity has an even more severe effect on women and girls 
in society because they are mostly involved in managing energy within households (Petrova 
and Simcock, 2019). This adds stress and time to looking for firewood and other traditional 
fuels and reduces their time for education, income, and social activities. Unlike in the past, this 
time, they replicated the gender disparities and spreaded their life chances for personal and 
economic transformation (Takamäki, 2020). In addition, they noted that health risks associated 
with substantial exposure to IAP are more pronounced among women, thereby making them 
even more vulnerable. 
As for gender, it is important to understand that women living in informal settlements have 
better yet different energy access and use prospects (Pearl-Martinez, 2020). It establishes them 
as responsible for providing energy to their homes, thereby making them over-reliant on 
inefficient and dangerous fuels. This means that the time and energy spent gathering the 
firewood or buying the fuel hinders them from their opportunity to get an education or earn an 
income. Also, women lack proper access to funds, credit, and decision-making, so they have 
poor bargaining power (Guvuriro and Booysen, 2019). Most of the time, they cannot buy 
modern energy solutions. This exclusion from the energy sector influences the entrenched 
gender inequalities while at the same time retarding efforts at attaining energy security. 
However, there are opportunities for women in energy solutions in the informal settlements, 
especially since the field has numerous challenges, as mentioned. Enabling and supporting 
women with a specific focus through energy programs will likely bring about a drastic change 
in the accessibility and utilisation of energy besides the female status. For instance, it is

2.5 
Conclusion and remarks 
This chapter has systematically reviewed the main areas and concepts of the research study 
(see figure 2.1), and identified systems approach as a powerful way of solving energy problems 
in informal settlement when adopted in the Living Labs with gender design. Consequently, the 
focus is on including multiple perspectives and user-driven approaches, thus increasing the 
energy solutions' efficacy and stability. The information on the South African and the general 
African experiences underscore the applicability of the SETILL model to advance energy 
development and promote energy access and utilisation sustainably. So, the systematic review 
should continue, and Living Lab methodologies and practices should be refined and extended 
to face the everyday challenges of the transformative discourse of the energy sector in Africa. 
Through such interactions, participants can identify viable ways through which different 
stakeholders can work together in a co-creation process to identify real solutions that can be 
implemented by all the parties to enhance the efficient use of energy by all the individuals 
within the society.

This review chapter has shown that by adopting systems approach in the Living Lab and 
incorporating biogas technology into the analysis, it is possible to come up with a sound 
conceptual framework to address problems in the energy sector of the African context of 
informal settlements. Combining these approaches leverages both strengths: biogas technology 
will offer an efficient and sustainable energy source (Ani, Aniokete and Agbo, 2024), and the 
ability to test the SETILL. At the same time, applications of systems approach will enable 
solutions to fit into the specific social context (York et al., 2019). Technologies such as biogas, 
where waste material is converted to biogas through a method known as anaerobic digestion, 
help solve waste elimination and energy production dilemmas (Obileke et al., 2020). While 
rational planning offers a more organised approach, systems approaches focus on hierarchy, 
networking, and interrelatedness of the socio-economic, environmental, and infrastructure put 
in place in the informal settlements (Okoye, 2020).

A sustainable intervention in informal settlement design addresses the multiple interactions in 
the physical context of the area (Christley et al., 2021). This means appreciating the fact that 
energy issues do not come singly but are just a slice of a number of issues touching on such 
aspects as waste disposal, health, economic advancement, and general sustainability of the 
environment. According to the community-oriented systems, representatives and decision-
makers can approach the case considering the situation from a more general point of view 
more focused on the achievement of several goals. For example, the technology used in 
biogas production is considered as a cleaner source of energy. It improves the management of 
wastes and diminishes the contact with indoor emissions besides offering an opportunity in 
generating income from other sources such as sale of excess biogas or quality fertilizers 
(Munga, 2020). 
According to Torres (2020), the steps involved in introducing the biogas technology in the 
informal settlements are: First of all, when establishing a program and setting goals and 
objectives one should consult and scan the community needs and assets along with possible 
and gaping barriers. With this assessment, the biogas systems designed to operate by fitting 
into the local environment as their goal is set at achieving the carbon offsets. Further, 
engaging in the mutually agreed upon decision making means the community is in charge of 
the interventions and ensures that the implemented solutions are socially relevant and 
optimal. The following involves pilot testing and further synchronisation of the biogas 
systems because of the feedback from the people living in the area. Last is the replication of 
successful initiatives in the extension of microfinance services through the attraction of 
funds, skills, and partnerships with the governmental and non-governmental organizations, 
businesses and industries. 
Moreover, the women play an important role in energy innovation activities because they 
increase the likelihood of positive change that is generally favorable and comprehensive to a 
majority of people. Several times, too, female voices when included translate to occupying a 
place with ideas and revealed insights that enhance the behind the scene workings of the energy 
sector (Habiyaremye, 2019). Thus, adopting gender mainstreaming could ensure more 
extensive engagement of women in Living Labs projects, apply their perspectives and expertise 
for co-creating energy innovations not only for the benefit of the participants but also for the 
improvement of the energy framework (Fortiş & Petcu, 2022).
