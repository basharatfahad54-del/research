[P24|Heading 1] 2. Abstract
[P25|Normal] South Africa's just energy transition is framed nationally by renewable procurement and grid decarbonisation, yet its outcomes are decided locally, in configurations where households, fuels, appliances, dwellings, tenure, infrastructure and institutions interact. In informal settlements these configurations often reproduce energy poverty, insecure supply and an unequal distribution of costs and benefits. Community-scale biogas is promoted as a decentralised alternative, but uptake is uneven, and existing acceptance models treat the technology as given and the user as an individual chooser, leaving the socio-material conditions of acceptance and justice unexamined.
[P26|Normal] This study develops SETILL, a Systemic Living Lab for Just Energy Transition, as a conceptual and methodological framework for examining how a community-scale biogas innovation can be designed, accepted and sustained justly in an informal settlement in Tzaneen, South Africa. The objectives are to frame the settlement as a socio-technical system, identify the factors that shape end-user participation, examine the cultural and technological conditions of acceptance, specify the mechanisms through which a Living Lab can support a just transition, and establish how such a Living Lab should be evaluated.
[P27|Normal] The study adopts a critical posthumanist paradigm, supported by systems thinking, energy justice, socio-technical transitions, Living Lab theory and complexity theory. It follows a qualitative, participatory case study design using a Living Lab approach, with co-design workshops, semi-structured interviews, observation and documentary analysis, analysed through reflexive thematic and framework analysis.
[P28|Normal] The expected contributions are a situated account of acceptance and justice in an informal settlement, a transferable Living Lab framework for community-scale energy innovation, practice guidance for participatory design, and methodological insight into reflexive, posthumanist energy research in engineering management.
[P30|Heading 1] 3. Introduction
[P31|Heading 2] 3.1 Background and context
[P32|Heading 3] 3.1.1 A grid that is changing, unevenly
[P33|Normal] South Africa generates most of its electricity from coal, and the costs of that dependence reach households directly. Scheduled load-shedding reached record levels in 2023 and has since been suspended for long periods, but the system remains vulnerable to breakdowns, and the price of electricity has risen faster than household incomes. For a middle-income household these are costs to be managed. For a household in an informal settlement they determine whether there is a hot meal, whether a child can study after dark, and whether the money set aside for electricity lasts the month.
[P34|Normal] The aggregate picture looks better than the household one. Around nine in ten South African households now have a grid connection (Statistics South Africa, 2023), yet connection is not the same as access. A connected household may still be unable to afford enough electricity, may receive an intermittent supply, may live in a dwelling whose wiring cannot carry a cooking load, or may buy through a landlord's meter at a marked-up price. Energy poverty in South Africa is therefore less a problem of missing infrastructure than of how infrastructure, income, tariffs, dwelling conditions and household practice fit together. That distinction shifts attention from the presence of a technology to the arrangement in which it works, or fails to work.
[P35|Normal] Policy has begun to reflect this. The Presidential Climate Commission's just transition framework accepts that decarbonisation and social equity cannot be separated (Presidential Climate Commission, 2022), and the Integrated Resource Plan sets out a shift away from coal alongside a commitment to security of supply (Department of Mineral Resources and Energy, 2019). The Just Energy Transition Partnership has added international finance and, with it, expectations of procedural fairness. Recent research shows, however, that the obstacles are as much institutional and social as financial or technical (Molepo et al., 2025; Mirzania et al., 2023): municipalities lack the capacity and revenue to fulfil their distribution duties, host communities have rarely been consulted meaningfully, and the households least able to pay are the least able to wait for benefits planned at national scale.
[P36|Heading 3] 3.1.2 Informal settlements as socio-material configurations
[P37|Normal] Informal settlements are not a single kind of place. They differ in age, size, location, tenure and access to services, and some have been upgraded while others rely on shared taps and informal connections. What they share is insecure tenure, self-built housing and incomplete municipal recognition, and that combination shapes both what is technically possible and who is able to make decisions about the household.
[P38|Normal] Three features are particularly relevant. The first is density: dwellings are close together, access routes are narrow and space for equipment is limited, so a technology that needs a dedicated yard, a water supply or a stable foundation may not fit. The second is the landlord-tenant relationship: many households rent a room or shack on someone else's plot, the meter may be in the landlord's name, and the tenant may have little say over wiring or appliances. The third is fuel stacking. Households rarely use one fuel for everything: electricity may serve lighting and phone charging while paraffin, firewood, charcoal or LPG serve cooking and heating. The choice is not habit alone; it reflects cost per unit of useful energy, reliability, pot size, weather, availability and the taste of the food (Musango, 2022; Musango et al., 2020).
[P39|Normal] Fuel stacking has consequences that a simple access metric cannot capture. Indoor smoke from solid fuels remains a significant health risk, particularly for women and young children; paraffin is a common cause of fires and of accidental ingestion; collecting wood takes time, usually from women; and a household that has “access” may still keep a paraffin stove for the evenings when the power is off. These are the substance of what an energy service is, and they explain why a technology that performs well in a laboratory can perform poorly in a settlement.
[P40|Heading 3] 3.1.3 Living Labs and decentralised energy technologies
[P41|Normal] The Living Lab has become a common way of organising innovation in real-world settings: users, researchers, firms and public bodies co-create, prototype and test solutions in the context where they will be used (Bergvall-Kåreborn and Ståhlbröst, 2009; Leminen et al., 2012). Applied in information technology, health and urban planning, the concept has more recently entered energy, where a systematic review found it used in several different ways — as a space, a methodology, an organisation or a network — and few applications in African informal settlements, with thin evidence on outcomes (Mbatha and Musango, 2022).
[P42|Normal] The South African research programme on gendered energy innovation is the most direct precedent. Its products include a gender analysis framework (Anditi et al., 2022), a systems model of energy service security (Batinge et al., 2022), a multi-stakeholder co-design toolkit (Petrulaityte et al., 2022) and a process for setting up energy Living Labs (Mukama et al., 2022). The programme established that gender is not an add-on to energy innovation and that service security depends on social as well as technical arrangements, and it supplies a practical vocabulary for working with communities. This study builds on that programme rather than claiming to start from nothing.
[P43|Normal] Biogas is selected from several candidate decentralised technologies for three reasons. First, anaerobic digestion converts organic waste into a gas for cooking, the service electricity most often fails to provide in informal settlements. Second, the technology makes the socio-material arrangement visible: a digester needs feedstock, water, space, stable temperature, regular attention and a route for the digestate, each connecting the artefact to the household, the neighbourhood, the municipality and the waste stream. Third, community-scale biogas has a mixed record in southern Africa — some installations work for years, many fail quickly through maintenance, feedstock, water or responsibility problems — and that variation is exactly what a study of acceptance needs.
[P44|Normal] Biogas is not presented as a universal solution; it is a test case. If the framework can explain why a biogas system is taken up in one part of a settlement and abandoned in another, it should transfer to other decentralised technologies, including solar water heating, improved cookstoves and small-scale PV.
[P45|Heading 3] 3.1.4 Why this is an Engineering Management problem
[P46|Normal] An engineering manager working on an energy project in an informal settlement has to make decisions that are technical, organisational and political at the same time. Which technology? At what scale? Who owns it? Who maintains it? How is it paid for? Who is trained? What happens when a part fails? These questions are not answered by a technical specification. They are answered through negotiations between households, municipal officials, suppliers, funders and community leaders, and they are answered under conditions of uncertainty.
[P47|Normal] Engineering Management has tools for such questions — requirements analysis, systems modelling, project and lifecycle management, risk assessment — but it lacks a theoretical account of the relationship between a technology and the social arrangement into which it is introduced. Without that account, the tools are applied to the artefact and the arrangement is left to chance. This study argues that the arrangement is the proper object of design, and that a theory is needed to describe it.
[P48|Heading 2] 3.2 Problem statement
[P49|Normal] Energy interventions in South African informal settlements tend to follow a familiar pattern. A technology is selected, a pilot is funded, equipment is installed and training provided. For a time it works. Then a part fails and cannot be sourced, feedstock becomes expensive, the maintainer moves away, the landlord raises the rent, or the promised service does not arrive. Use declines, the equipment is abandoned, and the project is recorded as a technical success that did not achieve its social objectives.
[P50|Normal] Three explanations for this pattern are taken seriously here. The first is analytical. The models that dominate applied energy research treat acceptance as an individual decision about a finished artefact: the Technology Acceptance Model and its successors ask whether a person finds a technology useful and easy to use (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Such questions can be answered in a survey, but they cannot explain why the same technology is used in one street and abandoned in the next. They abstract away the things that matter: the cost of fuel, the reliability of supply, the space available, the division of labour in the household, and the terms on which the dwelling is occupied.
[P51|Normal] The second explanation is methodological. Participation is now a standard requirement in energy projects, but it is often consultative rather than consequential: residents are invited to a meeting, their views are recorded, and the design is finalised elsewhere. This can legitimise a decision already taken, raise expectations the project cannot meet, and advantage those already comfortable speaking in public. The participation literature has described these failure modes for decades (Arnstein, 1969), yet they recur because the incentives to consult are stronger than the incentives to share control.
[P52|Normal] The third explanation is evaluative. Projects are usually judged by outputs — connections made, capacity installed, people trained — which say little about whether the intervention was just. A project can connect a settlement while the poorest pay more per unit than the wealthy, reduce smoke while transferring the burden of collecting feedstock to girls, or create a community asset that a local elite captures. Without criteria that make these distributions visible, an evaluation can report success while the injustice persists.
[P53|Normal] The problem, stated compactly, is this:
[TABLE 0] 1 rows x 1 cols
  | Problem statement. Energy technology interventions in South African informal settlements frequently fail to produce durable improvements in energy access because they are designed as transfers of finished artefacts rather than as reconfigurations of the socio-material arrangements through which energy services are produced. Engineering management lacks a systematic means of involving end-users as co-designers, of anticipating how materials, infrastructures, institutions and household practices will shape acceptance, and of judging whether the resulting arrangement is just. The consequence is a recurring pattern of misaligned technologies, abandoned pilots, unevenly distributed benefits and costs, and continued energy poverty.
[P55|Heading 2] 3.3 Research aim and objectives
[P56|Normal] The aim of the study is to develop, apply and evaluate a Systemic Living Lab for Just Energy Transition (SETILL) that supports the co-design of responsive energy technology innovations in a South African informal settlement, and to assess whether the resulting arrangement improves energy access and advances energy justice.
[P57|Normal] The aim is pursued through five objectives. Each objective corresponds to one research question and to a stage of the research design.
[P58|Normal] To establish how Systems Living Lab concepts frame the relationship between inequality, energy security, energy poverty, energy technology innovation and informal settlements, and to derive a theoretical account of that relationship from critical posthumanism.
[P59|Normal] To determine the socio-material factors that shape the involvement and participation of end-users in decision-making, co-design, co-creation, co-research, prototyping and testing of energy technology innovations in South African informal settlements.
[P60|Normal] To examine the cultural elements, norms, practices and technological mechanisms that influence the acceptance of end-user energy innovations in informal settlements.
[P61|Normal] To develop and evaluate a Systems Living Lab, operationalised through a community-scale biogas pilot, that engages end-users and other stakeholders in participatory design in a real-world setting.
[P62|Normal] To evaluate whether the SETILL framework can function as a coherent theoretical framework and a practical tool for improving energy security, reducing energy poverty and advancing energy justice.

They are deliberately open — they ask how and which, not whether a single variable has an effect — because the study is concerned with mechanisms and configurations rather than with estimating causal parameters across a population.

[P73|Heading 2] 3.5 Justification of the study
[P74|Heading 3] 3.5.1 Academic justification
[P75|Normal] The study addresses a gap at the intersection of four literatures: energy justice, Living Lab methodology, socio-technical systems thinking and critical posthumanism. Each is well developed in its own field yet falls short of this problem. Energy justice has a robust normative vocabulary but says little about how an engineer should design a prototype; Living Lab research describes co-creation in detail but evaluates outputs rather than justice; systems thinking maps complexity but often remains functionalist; and posthumanism offers a sophisticated ontology of human–technology relations that has rarely been applied to energy infrastructure in the Global South.
[P76|Normal] The contribution is not the invention of a new theory but the construction of a defensible bridge between these bodies of work, tested against a case. Bridge-building of this kind is a legitimate scholarly contribution, but each tradition must be represented on its own terms, the points of incompatibility stated rather than smoothed over, and the resulting framework must generate propositions that could fail.
[P77|Heading 3] 3.5.2 Practical justification
[P78|Normal] For practitioners, the study offers a design protocol that treats the socio-material configuration, rather than the artefact, as the unit of intervention; evaluation criteria that make distributional and procedural questions visible alongside technical performance; and evidence about what happens when a community-scale biogas system is co-designed in an informal settlement, including the conditions under which it is likely to be maintained. These are modest outputs, but they are the kind that project managers and municipal officials can use.
[P79|Heading 3] 3.5.3 Policy and sector justification
[P80|Normal] At the policy level, the study speaks to the implementation of the just transition framework at municipal scale. Municipalities are responsible for distribution, integrated development planning and, in many cases, for settlements that national policy treats as temporary. The study's attention to procedural justice, to the terms on which residents are included, and to how costs and benefits are distributed is directly relevant to the design of municipal energy programmes and the evaluation of JETP-funded projects.
[P81|Heading 3] 3.5.4 Methodological justification
[P82|Normal] The study contributes a methodological approach combining reflexive thematic analysis, situational mapping, causal loop diagramming and diffractive reading. Each method is established, but they are rarely used together, and the epistemological work of combining them has not been done. The study will document how the combination was applied, what it produced that a single method would have missed, and where it created tensions — a documentation that is itself a contribution to qualitative energy research.
[P83|Heading 2] 3.6 Scope and delimitations
[P84|Normal] The study is deliberately bounded. It examines one informal settlement in Greater Tzaneen, Limpopo Province, over three years, focusing on household cooking and a community-scale biogas system. It does not model the national electricity system, evaluate the life-cycle impacts of biogas, or estimate willingness to pay across a representative sample; those questions require different designs and resources.
[P85|Normal] The study is a proposal. No fieldwork has been conducted and no empirical data exist. The methods described in Section 7 are intended methods, to be implemented only after ethical clearance and after agreements with the municipality, community leadership and the households involved.
[P86|Normal] Four further delimitations should be stated. First, the study does not claim biogas is the best technology for the site, only that it is a useful case for examining the framework. Second, it aims for analytical rather than statistical generalisation, in the sense described by Yin (2009) and defended by Flyvbjerg (2006). Third, it records reported symptoms and observed conditions rather than clinical measures of indoor air pollution, treating them as one dimension of wellbeing. Fourth, it does not treat “the community” as a unified actor: it deliberately samples for difference, including tenants, landlords, women's groups, youth and municipal officials.

[P94|Heading 2] 4.1 Review approach
[P95|Normal] This is an integrative review rather than a systematic review, and the distinction is deliberate. A systematic review answers a narrow question by exhausting a defined body of evidence; an integrative review synthesises literature from several traditions in order to frame a problem and identify a gap (Torraco, 2016). The present question sits between fields that do not share a vocabulary — energy engineering, energy justice, Living Lab research and posthumanist philosophy each describe part of the problem, and none describes all of it.
[P96|Normal] The review follows a documented search strategy, set out in Table 2.1. It does not claim to be exhaustive and does not use the language of PRISMA; its claim is to be integrative and critical. If examiners later require a systematic review, a protocol can be registered and a second review conducted.

[P100|Heading 2] 4.2 Energy poverty and energy security in South African informal settlements
[P101|Normal] Energy poverty is usually defined as the inability to obtain the energy services needed for a decent life — a definition deliberately broader than “lack of electricity”, covering quantity and quality, affordability, reliability, safety and the time required to obtain energy. A household with a connection it cannot afford to use is energy poor; so is a household that cooks on paraffin because the supply fails in the evening.
[P102|Normal] South Africa's energy poverty is historically produced. Apartheid spatial planning located black households far from work and services, and the electricity system was built to serve industry and a minority of households. The post-1994 electrification programme raised the connection rate from around a third of households to around nine in ten (Statistics South Africa, 2023), an achievement that nonetheless left energy poverty intact: tariffs rose sharply, dwellings are often poorly insulated and inadequately wired, and interruptions became routine. Formal access coexists with informal practice — households are connected and still stack fuels, borrow electricity and cook on paraffin when the power fails.
[P103|Normal] Figure 2.1 represents that pattern as a chain from carrier to service, with the losses at each step: the right-hand column is where a technical account usually stops, and the bottom band is the part it omits.

[P106|Normal] The South African literature has documented this pattern in detail. Musango (2022) uses quantitative storytelling in the Groenheuwel settlement to show how gender, income and dwelling characteristics interact in household energy transitions. Musango et al. (2020) argue that the security of energy services, rather than of supply, is the appropriate organising concept for poor urban environments, and Batinge et al. (2022) formalise the insight in a systems model linking gendered innovation and service security through feedback. Anditi et al. (2022) provide a gender analysis framework that makes visible the different roles, burdens and knowledge of women and men, and Oosthuizen et al. (2020) identify, in a structured review, the factors that have hindered the mainstreaming of gendered energy interventions.
[P107|Normal] Three limitations recur. First, households are treated as homogeneous units, yet a household is a set of relationships in which age, gender, tenure and income shape who decides, who cooks, who pays and who bears the risk — a technology acceptable to the household head may be unacceptable to the person who has to clean it. Second, the urban settlement is sometimes conflated with the rural village, although the two differ in density, land markets, tenure, infrastructure and proximity to services. Third, the literature is stronger on description than on design: it explains why energy poverty persists but offers little guidance on what an engineer should change in a prototype, a tariff or a service model.
[P108|Normal] Energy security, in this literature, is best understood as a household-level accomplishment: security means obtaining the needed services across normal and disrupted conditions without unacceptable cost or risk. The definition connects the national conversation about generation capacity to the daily conversation about cooking, and it makes the distribution of security an ethical question, because security for one household can be purchased at the expense of another — a landlord's prepaid meter secures revenue and transfers the risk of arrears to the tenant.
[P109|Heading 2] 4.3 Just transition and energy justice
[P110|Normal] The idea of a just transition began in the labour and environmental movements of the 1980s and 1990s, which insisted that workers in polluting industries should not bear the cost of environmental protection. In its contemporary form, a just transition is fair in its outcomes, open in its procedures and respectful of the identities and knowledge of affected communities (Newell and Mulvaney, 2013; Swilling and Annecke, 2012). Winkler (2020) gives the concept a neo-Gramscian reading: transitions are shaped by the balance of social forces, and a transition that does not shift that balance will reproduce existing inequalities under a greener label.
[P111|Normal] In South Africa, the just transition has become official policy. The Presidential Climate Commission's framework sets out principles for distribution, procedure and restoration, and the Just Energy Transition Partnership has attached international finance to the coal phase-down (Presidential Climate Commission, 2022). Implementation research is less optimistic: Mirzania et al. (2023) identify structural and institutional barriers to powering past coal, including municipal capacity, vested interests and the social dependency of coal towns, and Molepo et al. (2025) find that the barriers are distributed across policy, finance, skills and governance and that they interact. The transition is not blocked by a single missing factor.
[P112|Normal] Energy justice supplies the normative vocabulary for evaluating these processes. The most widely used formulation identifies three tenets — distributive, procedural and recognition justice (McCauley et al., 2013; Jenkins et al., 2016). Sovacool and Dworkin (2015) add principles including availability, affordability, due process and intergenerational equity; Heffron and McCauley (2017) add restorative justice, the repair of past harm; and Walker and Day (2012) argue, for fuel poverty, that a failure of recognition is itself a form of distributive injustice rather than a separate dimension to be traded off.
[P113|Normal] The literature has two weaknesses for present purposes. The first is scale: energy justice is usually applied to policy, siting conflicts and large infrastructure, and its principles do not tell an engineer which material to specify or how to organise maintenance. The second is operationalisation: recognition is often reduced to “stakeholder engagement” and procedure to “consultation”, and both reductions lose the critical content of the concepts — a consultation can be procedurally correct and still legitimise a decision taken elsewhere.
[P114|Normal] What the study takes from this literature is a set of evaluative questions, not a ready-made design method: who receives what, through which procedure, with whose knowledge recognised, and with what repair for past harm? Section 6 turns these questions into indicators; Section 5 explains why they cannot be answered by a purely technical assessment.
[P115|Heading 2] 4.4 Living Labs and systems thinking
[P116|Normal] A Living Lab is a real-world arrangement for innovation in which users, researchers, firms and public bodies collaborate over time. Most definitions agree on four elements: a real-life setting, multiple stakeholders, co-creation with users, and iterative experimentation (Bergvall-Kåreborn and Ståhlbröst, 2009; Leminen et al., 2012). Labs have been classified by who drives them, by sector and by their physical or virtual constitution, but Hossain et al. (2019), in a systematic review, find the literature fragmented, with many studies describing individual labs without comparing them. Dell'Era and Landoni (2014) position the Living Lab between user-centred and participatory design, making clear that it is an organisational form rather than a consultation method.
[P117|Normal] The energy Living Lab literature is smaller. Mbatha and Musango (2022) find the concept used inconsistently — as a space, a method or a network — and propose a six-phase process for setting up an energy Living Lab in an urban informal settlement. Mukama et al. (2022) describe Living Labs for gendered energy innovation in poor urban environments, Petrulaityte et al. (2022) present the GENS co-design toolkit, and von Wirth et al. (2019) show that urban labs influence sustainability transitions through embedding, learning and network formation, but that their impact depends on how the lab is connected to the wider regime.
[P118|Normal] Systems thinking supplies a different set of resources. Checkland (1981) argues that systems are ways of organising inquiry, so that a system's boundary is a choice with political consequences. Meadows (2008) shows how feedback, stocks and flows produce behaviour that cannot be inferred from the parts. Midgley (2000) develops boundary critique as a central move, and Sterman (2000) provides the vocabulary of causal loops and delays. Cilliers (1998) cautions that complex systems cannot be fully represented, so any model is a reduction to be used with care.
[P119|Normal] The combination of Living Lab and systems thinking is often asserted rather than derived. A “systemic Living Lab” is usually a lab that uses systems tools, not one whose boundaries, stakeholder set, feedback architecture and evaluation criteria are derived from systems theory. That distinction is the subject of Section 5.8, and it matters because a lab that is systemic only in vocabulary will reproduce the conventional boundary of the project, with the wider settlement treated as context.
[P120|Normal] The empirical evidence on energy Living Labs in African informal settlements is thin: the South African work is recent and mostly descriptive, and there are few longitudinal, comparative or counterfactual evaluations. This is a statement about the state of the evidence rather than about the researchers, and it shapes the claims this study can make: it can contribute a carefully documented case, but it cannot establish general effectiveness.
[P121|Heading 2] 4.5 End-user participation and acceptance
[P122|Normal] Participation has a long and sceptical literature. Arnstein's (1969) ladder distinguishes manipulation and therapy from consultation, partnership and citizen control, and its central insight survives: the difference between consultation and control is the difference between legitimising a decision and sharing it. Sanders and Stappers (2008) describe the shift from designing for users to designing with them, and von Hippel (2005) shows that users can be important sources of innovation when their needs are unusual and their knowledge tacit.
[P123|Normal] Energy research has adopted participation as a norm while examining its conditions more slowly. Wüstenhagen et al. (2007) distinguish social acceptance at three levels — socio-political, community and market — showing that a technology can be acceptable in principle and resisted in a particular place; but the model remains attitudinal, asking what people think rather than what the technology, the infrastructure and household practice do together.
[P124|Normal] The dominant model of acceptance in engineering and information systems is the Technology Acceptance Model, in which perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use predict intention and use (Davis, 1989), extended by UTAUT with social influence and facilitating conditions (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Both models have been enormously productive, and both assume an individual chooser, a finished artefact, a measurable intention and a context that can be treated as background. They have little to say about the cost of fuel, the reliability of supply, the division of labour, the terms of tenure or the material properties of a dwelling. A TAM study can report that residents find a biogas system useful; it cannot explain why the system is abandoned when the water supply fails.
[P125|Normal] Practice theory and the energy cultures framework offer an alternative. Shove (2003) shows that consumption is shaped by collective, material conventions of comfort, cleanliness and convenience, and Stephenson et al. (2010) propose that energy behaviour emerges from the interaction of norms, practices and material culture. These approaches treat technology and practice as co-constitutive, but they are stronger on explanation than on design.
[P126|Normal] Participation also has failure modes the literature documents and projects ignore: consultation can be tokenistic, meetings dominated by those with time and status, expectations raised and disappointed, benefits captured by elites, and communities fatigued by repeated participation in projects that do not endure. These are not arguments against participation but for designing it with the same care as the technology, and for evaluating it rather than assuming its virtue.
[P127|Heading 2] 4.6 Decentralised energy and biogas
[P128|Normal] Anaerobic digestion is a mature technology: bacteria break down organic matter without oxygen, producing a gas that is roughly 50 to 70 per cent methane and a residue usable as a soil amendment. The process needs a consistent feedstock, water, a temperature range that supports microbial activity, and an adequate retention time. In practice, designing a digester means designing a supply chain and a maintenance arrangement as much as a vessel.
[P129|Normal] Biogas has been promoted across Africa for decades with mixed results. Successful installations share a reliable feedstock supply, a user who understands the system, a maintenance arrangement with access to parts, and a use for the gas that fits existing cooking practice; failures share the opposite. The technical literature calls these “non-technical barriers”. A posthumanist reading treats them as part of the technology: the digester is the vessel plus the feedstock, the water, the labour, the stove, the spare parts and the agreement about who maintains it.
[P130|Normal] In South African informal settlements, the conditions for biogas are both promising and difficult. Organic waste is plentiful in some areas, particularly where there is a market or food trade; water is often shared and sometimes costly; space is limited and tenure is insecure; cooking practices vary, and gas may not be acceptable for all dishes; and municipal waste services are uneven. These conditions do not make biogas impossible. They make the configuration the central design problem, which is precisely why biogas is a useful case for this study.
[P131|Heading 2] 4.7 Posthumanism in energy and engineering
[P132|Normal] Posthumanism has been applied extensively in education, geography, feminist technoscience and design, but rarely in energy engineering. Forlano (2017) argues that taking non-human agency seriously changes what counts as a design problem. Ulmer (2017) sets out posthumanism as a research methodology. Aboalez (2025) makes the case for a posthumanist social approach to energy transition in engineering education — the closest precedent for this study, discussed in Section 5.4.
[P133|Normal] The gap is not that posthumanism has never been mentioned in energy research, but that there is no developed account of how distributed agency shapes the design, acceptance and use of energy technologies in informal settlements. Energy justice says what justice requires, Living Lab research says how to organise collaboration, and posthumanism says how to think about human–technology relations. What is missing is a framework that connects the three at the scale of a settlement project.

[P142|Heading 1] 5. Theoretical Framework
[P143|Heading 2] 5.1 Why a theoretical framework matters in doctoral research
[P144|Normal] A theoretical framework is a set of assumptions and concepts, connected by an argument, that determines what a study observes, how it interprets what it observes and what it will accept as an explanation. It does three kinds of work at once: it specifies an ontology, a view of what exists and what can act; an epistemology, a view of how knowledge about those things can be produced; and an axiology, a view of what the research is for and whose interests it serves.
[P145|Normal] The literature on doctoral research is consistent about this function. Grant and Osanloo (2014) describe the theoretical framework as the blueprint of a dissertation: it determines the rooms that can be built and the connections between them. Ravitch and Riggan (2017) argue that a conceptual framework is not a diagram appended to a literature review but the researcher's reasoned account of why the study is designed as it is. Sandberg and Alvesson (2011) show that constructing a research question is itself a theoretical act, and Alvesson and Kärreman (2007) add that empirical material becomes interesting when it resists the theory used to generate it.
[P146|Normal] Four functions follow. A framework prevents description from masquerading as analysis: a study that reports what people said, without a theory of why they said it, produces data rather than knowledge. It makes assumptions contestable, since an implicit assumption cannot be examined at all. It connects the study to a tradition, so that the reader can see which conversation is being joined and what would count as a contribution. And it disciplines interpretation: a researcher committed to a theory can be held to its implications, whereas a researcher who has not can explain any finding after the fact.
[P147|Normal] The relationship between theory and empirical work runs in both directions: theory shapes questions, design and interpretation, while surprising evidence can revise theory — the logic of abduction (Alvesson and Kärreman, 2007). The surprise is not a nuisance; it is the point at which the study has something to say.
[P148|Normal] A theoretical framework is not the same as a conceptual framework, and the distinction matters for the structure of this proposal. The theoretical framework is the general body of theory the study adopts, abstract and portable; the conceptual framework is the researcher's own model of the specific problem, concrete and local. Section 5 establishes the theory; Section 6 constructs the model. A common weakness in applied research is to collapse the two, so that the model is presented as if it were a theory and the theory is never tested against anything.
[P149|Normal] The consequences of a weak or absent framework are predictable: variables selected ad hoc, unfalsifiable claims, findings that cannot be generalised because no theory says what they are a case of, and recommendations that follow the researcher's preferences rather than the analysis. In engineering management the pressure to be useful is strong, and a framework is sometimes dismissed as an academic ornament that delays practical work. The argument of this section is the opposite: without a framework, the practical work has no way of knowing what it is looking at.
[P150|Heading 2] 5.2 Theoretical frameworks in Engineering Management research
[P151|Normal] Engineering Management sits at the intersection of technical systems, organisational behaviour, economics, innovation studies and public policy, and its problems are rarely confined to one level: a decision about a technology is also a decision about skills, contracts, maintenance, regulation and trust. A technically sound project can still fail because the operating organisation lacks capacity, the tariff makes the service unaffordable, or the community has learned from previous projects that promises are not kept.
[P152|Normal] The field has borrowed theories from several disciplines: TAM and its successors from psychology and information systems, Lean Thinking and the resource-based view from operations, complexity and systems thinking from complexity science, and socio-technical systems theory and the multi-level perspective from sociology and innovation studies. Each has been productive in its home setting. The difficulty arises when a theory developed for a firm, an individual or a market is applied to an infrastructural problem in a low-income settlement: the theory does not merely lose precision, it mis-specifies the object.
[P153|Normal] Three mis-specifications recur. Level confusion: a theory of individual intention is used to explain a collective outcome, or of firm strategy to explain a household practice. Black-boxing: the artefact is treated as a fixed input whose properties are known, when its properties are part of what has to be explained. Context as background: what the model controls for rather than what it is about. In a study of energy in an informal settlement, context is the substance — tenure, density, infrastructure, income, gender and municipal capacity are not variables to be held constant but the conditions that make a technology work or fail.
[P154|Normal] A framework adequate to this problem therefore needs to be relational and multi-level, to hold human and non-human elements in one frame without reducing one to the other, to be normative because the problem is about justice, and to be empirically tractable because the findings must be defensible. These requirements are the criteria applied in Section 5.11.
[P155|Normal] There is also the question of disciplinary fit. Engineering Management values usefulness: a theory that cannot inform a decision will not survive in the field however elegant it is. The framework is therefore assessed not only for philosophical coherence but for whether it changes what a design team would do on a Monday morning.
[P156|Heading 2] 5.3 Philosophical foundations of critical posthumanism
[P157|Heading 3] 5.3.1 Humanism and its limits
[P158|Normal] Humanism is less a single doctrine than a family of commitments: the human being as the central subject of knowledge and value; culture separated from nature and mind from body; a universal human essence as the measure of other beings. These commitments enabled universal rights, scientific inquiry and the critique of arbitrary authority, but they also produced exclusions: the universal human was in practice male, European, propertied and able-bodied, and those who did not fit were treated as less than fully human — as nature rather than culture, as objects of study rather than participants in knowledge.
[P159|Normal] Posthumanism begins from this diagnosis. It does not reject humanism's achievements; it rejects the pretence that the human is a self-contained, universal subject, and asks what becomes visible when the pretence is dropped. The question is not “what comes after the human?” but “how has the human been produced, and what else is involved in that production?”
[P160|Heading 3] 5.3.2 Three formations that must not be conflated
[P161|Normal] The term posthumanism covers several projects, and the differences matter.
[P162|Normal] The first is posthumanism as a historical condition: humans are increasingly entangled with technologies, data, infrastructures and ecologies, and the boundaries between body and machine, nature and culture, are harder to draw. Hayles (1999) and Braidotti (2013) describe the condition, although they evaluate it differently.
[P163|Normal] The second is critical posthumanism, a normative and philosophical project drawing on anti-humanism, feminism, postcolonial thought and science and technology studies, which affirms difference rather than universalism and treats knowledge as situated. Braidotti (2013, 2019) is its most systematic contemporary exponent; Wolfe (2010) distinguishes it carefully from the third formation.
[P164|Normal] The third is transhumanism, which seeks to enhance human capacities through technology — longer life, greater intelligence, control over the body. Its values remain humanist: perfectibility, mastery, transcendence. This study rejects transhumanism, and not as a matter of taste: transhumanism would direct attention to enhancing individual capacities and away from the collective, material and institutional arrangements that are the subject of the study.
[P165|Normal] A fourth position, technological determinism, is also rejected. The alternative adopted here is not that society is autonomous but that technology and society are co-constituted: a prepaid meter does not cause a particular relation between landlord and tenant; it materialises a relation already being negotiated, and in doing so changes the terms of the negotiation.
[P166|Heading 3] 5.3.3 Relational ontology
[P167|Normal] The central ontological claim of critical posthumanism is that entities do not precede their relations. In Barad's (2007) agential realism the primary unit is the phenomenon, not the object: an entanglement of matter and meaning in which boundaries are enacted. The relata within a phenomenon acquire their properties through intra-action; they do not exist first and then interact. Intra-action is the relation through which they emerge.
[P168|Normal] The claim is difficult, and easy to repeat without understanding. A biogas digester is not a thing with fixed properties introduced into a household. What the digester is, in practice, depends on the feedstock available, the water that can be spared, the space allocated, the stove used, the person who maintains it and the agreements that govern it. Change the water supply and the digester becomes a different object, possibly a burden rather than a service. The properties that matter are not intrinsic to the vessel; they are produced in the relation.
[P169|Normal] Haraway's (1988) epistemology is a companion to this ontology. Knowledge is always from somewhere: the “view from nowhere” conceals a position rather than escaping one. Situated knowledge is objective in a different way, because it can be located and checked. The implication is direct: a resident's knowledge of how a stove behaves in a small kitchen is not anecdote awaiting expert validation; it is knowledge produced at a particular site, indispensable to a design that has to work there.
[P170|Heading 3] 5.3.4 Distributed agency
[P171|Normal] If entities emerge through relations, agency cannot belong to humans alone. Barad (2007) describes agency as an enactment rather than an attribute, distributed across the apparatus through which a phenomenon is observed and the materials that participate in it. The agential cut is the boundary drawn within a phenomenon between what is observed and what observes; it is not arbitrary, but it is a cut — it includes and excludes, and it has consequences.
[P172|Normal] Latour's (2005) actor-network theory is adjacent but not identical: humans and non-humans act symmetrically as actants, and the networks through which action is assembled can be traced. Actor-network theory is empirically useful because it shows how a technology enrols allies and how it can be undone; it is less useful for normative questions, because it is deliberately indifferent to the justice of the network it describes. The study uses Latour's sensibility without adopting his ontology wholesale.
[P173|Normal] Bennett's (2010) thing-power is also relevant: matter has a vitality of its own, and things can resist, surprise and exceed human intention. A digester that produces too little gas, a clogged pipe, a stove that will not sit flat on a pot — these are moments in which the material world asserts its own agency and changes what the household can do.
[P174|Normal] The consequence for research is significant: a study that asks only what people think has already excluded most of what is going on. The meter, the fuel, the dwelling, the water supply and the maintenance arrangement are not context; they are participants.
[P175|Heading 3] 5.3.5 Materiality and technological agency
[P176|Normal] Posthumanism takes matter seriously, which distinguishes it from constructionism's willingness to treat the material world as a product of discourse. Matter is not passive: a prepaid meter is not a neutral measuring device but enacts a relation between household, utility and money — it requires purchase before use, it can disconnect a household automatically, and it shifts cash-flow management onto the household. Those material effects shape practice, and they are not captured by asking whether the meter is easy to use.
[P177|Normal] A digester is another example. Its material requirements — water, feedstock, temperature, space — produce social requirements: someone must collect the waste, carry the water, be available when the system needs attention, and be able to buy a spare part. The technology distributes labour, usually along gendered lines. That is a property of the artefact-in-relation, not a social consequence appended to a technical artefact.
[P178|Heading 3] 5.3.6 Ecological interdependence
[P179|Normal] Posthumanism also insists on the interdependence of human and ecological systems: the nature–culture distinction is a way of organising the world, not a description of it, and the organisation has costs. The Anthropocene names a condition in which human activity has become a geological force and its consequences are unevenly distributed — those who contributed least to emissions are often most exposed to their effects.
[P180|Normal] Ecological interdependence matters here in two ways. Energy interventions have environmental effects that are not externalities to be added at the end: a biogas system that consumes water in a water-scarce settlement is not environmentally benign simply because it is renewable. And environmental and social justice are not separate agendas: the arrangements that expose residents to smoke and fire also expose them to floods, heat and pollution. A framework that treats the social and the environmental as separate domains will miss the connection.
[P181|Heading 3] 5.3.7 Ethics and power
[P182|Normal] Posthumanist ethics is relational and affirmative. Braidotti (2006, 2013) contrasts potestas, the power that negates and constrains, with potentia, the capacity that enables and creates — a distinction that avoids the assumption that power is always repressive. A community can have the capacity to organise and build even without formal authority; the task of a just intervention is to increase potentia without ignoring the potestas that constrains it.
[P183|Normal] Haraway (2016) offers response-ability: the practised, accountable capacity to respond to others, human and non-human. Barad (2007) describes ethics as ethico-onto-epistemology: what we choose to observe, and how, is already an ethical decision, because questions of being and knowing cannot be separated from questions of value.
[P184|Normal] These ideas have practical implications for a Living Lab. Who is invited to the first meeting, which data are recorded, who is credited for an innovation, and who bears the risk of failure are all ethical decisions. A framework that treats ethics as a consent form signed at the start will miss most of them.

[P193|Normal] Braidotti supplies the normative horizon. [P194|Normal] Haraway supplies the epistemology. Situated knowledge is the strongest available alternative to the assumption that the designer knows best, and response-ability describes the ethical relation between researchers and residents more demandingly than informed consent. Her later concept of sympoiesis, making-with (Haraway, 2016), is directly relevant to a Living Lab because it treats collaboration as a condition of life rather than a technique. The limitation is that Haraway offers figures and provocations, not procedures.
[P195|Normal] Barad supplies the ontology and the analytical unit. Intra-action is the reason the study treats the phenomenon, not the user or the artefact, as the unit of analysis; the apparatus is the reason a Living Lab can be understood as producing the phenomenon it studies; and diffraction — reading insights through one another — holds the interview, observational and workshop material together in the analysis. The risk is obscurity, since Barad's vocabulary is drawn from quantum physics; every technical term in this proposal is therefore defined in plain English and illustrated with an energy example.
[P196|Normal] Hayles supplies the corrective to abstraction. Her account of embodiment insists that information is always instantiated in a body, a device and a material arrangement, and her concept of technogenesis — humans and technologies co-evolving — supports the refusal to treat technology as an external object. Her work is less useful for justice and participation, and is not asked to carry that weight.
[P197|Normal] The four thinkers converge on a rejection of the autonomous humanist subject, on relationality and on the critique of the nature–culture divide; they diverge on almost everything else — Braidotti a vitalist monist, Barad a realist about relations, Haraway a situated pragmatist, Hayles a media theorist of embodiment. The study does not present them as one theory. It assembles a small number of concepts from each, states why each is needed, and accepts the assembly as a construction rather than a discovery.
[P198|Normal] This is also where the study connects to the supervisor's work. Aboalez (2025) draws on Braidotti and Barad to argue for a posthumanist social approach to energy transition. The present study extends that argument from engineering education to settlement-level design, adding Haraway's epistemology and Hayles's materialism to address questions the earlier paper did not have to answer.
[P199|Heading 2] 5.5 Posthumanism and socio-technical energy systems
[P200|Normal] An energy system is usually described as a chain: generation, transmission, distribution, metering, appliances, end use. The description is accurate as far as it goes, but it separates the technical from the social and treats the social as where the technical is received. A posthumanist account describes the same system as an assemblage: a configuration of people, materials, infrastructures, institutions, fuels, dwellings, meters, tariffs and practices whose relations produce the capacity to deliver an energy service.
[P201|Normal] The difference is not semantic. In the chain description the household is the end point; in the assemblage description it is one element among many, and the service is produced by the relations between them. A cooking service is produced by the stove, the fuel, the pot, the kitchen, the cook, the money that buys the fuel, the tariff, the reliability of supply and the norms governing what counts as a proper meal. Change any one of these and the service changes.
[P202|Normal] This way of thinking has become more common in energy social science. Sovacool (2014) calls for an agenda that takes infrastructure, practice and politics seriously, and the multi-level perspective describes how niches, regimes and landscapes interact over time (Geels, 2002, 2011). That literature explains why transitions are slow and why a promising niche can fail to displace an incumbent regime, but it operates at a high level of aggregation: it can explain the fate of a technology category, less well why one digester works and the next does not.
[P203|Normal] Posthumanism's value is that it works at the scale of the configuration without losing the wider structures: the tariff, the by-law, the landlord's rule, the stove and the pot are elements of the same assemblage even though they operate at different scales.
[P204|Heading 3] 5.5.1 A worked illustration: the biogas digester
[P205|Normal] Consider a community-scale digester proposed for an informal settlement. A conventional engineering assessment would specify feedstock, hydraulic retention time, volume, gas yield and capital cost; a TAM study would add measures of perceived usefulness and ease of use. Both produce useful information; neither explains what happens next. A posthumanist analysis asks a different set of questions.
[P206|Normal] Feedstock and water. Who produces the organic waste, who collects it, and what else do they do with their time? If the waste is sold or fed to animals, the digester competes with an existing use; if collection falls to women or children, the labour cost falls on them; if the supply is seasonal, the output will be seasonal. Digestion also needs water in a fixed ratio to feedstock, and in a settlement where water is shared, purchased or carried, the digester competes with cooking, cleaning and drinking. A design that assumes a tap in the yard has already excluded the households most in need.
[P207|Normal] Space, temperature and cooking. A digester needs a footprint, a slope, access for maintenance and a safe distance from dwellings, and in a dense settlement space is claimed and contested, so siting is a decision about whose space is taken and who is compensated. Microbial activity depends on temperature, so a system that performs in a warm climate may underperform in winter — a material property that shapes the service that can be promised, not a detail for a safety factor. And gas burns differently from wood or paraffin: the flame is less visible, the heat more concentrated, the pot may need to change. Ignore the pot and the dish, and the cook will judge the system unusable whatever the gas yield.
[P208|Normal] Maintenance, labour and institutions. A digester needs someone with time, tools, training and a reason to keep going to check the feed, remove the digestate, clear a blockage and replace a valve; if the arrangement depends on a volunteer who moves away, the technology has a design fault, not a social problem. The labour is usually assigned to women, so a system that increases that labour while reducing smoke may be good on one dimension and bad on another — the frame makes the distribution visible by treating labour as part of the assemblage. A permit, a waste agreement, a municipal connection or a credit arrangement may also be required: these are not “enabling environment” factors outside the project; their presence or absence changes what the technology is.
[P209|Normal] None of this is invisible to a good engineer: practitioners know that feedstock, water, space and maintenance decide whether a system survives. The theory's contribution is not the list of factors but the claim that they are constitutive of the technology rather than external to it, so that the unit of design is the configuration. If the claim is accepted, prototyping must include the meter, the tariff, the dwelling, the maintenance contract and the waste supply — the arrangement, not only the device, is what gets evaluated.
[P210|Normal] A TAM study would report that residents find the idea of biogas useful. A posthumanist study would report that the system will fail where water is carried, succeed where a market provides a steady waste stream, and add a burden for the women expected to feed it. The second account is less tidy, but more useful for design.
[P211|Heading 2] 5.6 Posthumanism, social justice and just energy transition
[P212|Normal] The normative core of this study is not posthumanism alone. It is a conversation between posthumanism and energy justice, in which each supplies something the other lacks.
[P213|Normal] Posthumanism adds four things to the justice literature. It extends the moral community beyond humans, so that materials, ecologies and future generations are participants in the arrangement rather than resources; it reframes justice as a property of composition as well as distribution — which actors and materials are admitted to the arrangement in the first place; it makes visible exclusions that human-centred recognition analysis misses, such as a dwelling that cannot accommodate a technology or infrastructure that excludes by design; and it supplies a critique of the technocratic view from nowhere, whose neutrality usually marks a position others do not hold.
[P214|Normal] Posthumanism also has limits, stated plainly. It does not specify distributive criteria, generate policy instruments or offer a theory of institutions, and it can be vague about how decisions are actually made. Applied without a justice vocabulary it can dissolve human suffering into ontology: a woman who cannot afford paraffin is not a “phenomenon”; she is a person in a specific relation of deprivation, and the analysis must be able to say so.
[P215|Normal] Energy justice supplies the missing operational content. The four dimensions used here — distribution, procedure, recognition and restoration — provide assessable criteria: who receives the service and who bears the cost; who participates and on what terms; whose knowledge and practices count; what past harm is repaired and what future capacity is built. They are not derived from posthumanism but from the justice literature (McCauley et al., 2013; Jenkins et al., 2016; Heffron and McCauley, 2017), and they are retained as a normative-operational complement.
[P216|Normal] Justice also has a scalar dimension. Fraser (2009) argues that the frame — who is included in the “who” of justice — is itself a question of justice. In an energy project the frame may be the household, the street, the settlement, the municipality or the planet, and each frame makes some claims visible and others invisible: a project that is just at settlement scale may be unjust regionally if it displaces pollution or extracts water. The study does not resolve this problem, but names it and builds a scalar check into the evaluation.
[P217|Normal] The combination is deliberate: posthumanism provides the ontology and the critique of technocracy, energy justice the criteria and the policy vocabulary, systems thinking the tools for mapping relations and feedback. None is sufficient alone, and the framework is designed so that each can be held accountable by the others.
[P218|Normal] One further point is necessary. The just transition literature has been criticised for treating “the community” as a homogeneous beneficiary, and the criticism applies to a posthumanist framework too. Settlements are stratified by tenure, income, gender, age and political affiliation; a project that benefits “the community” may benefit one group and burden another. The framework therefore requires disaggregated evidence and does not assume that a community-level outcome is a household-level outcome.
[P219|Heading 2] 5.7 Posthumanism and end-user participation
[P220|Heading 3] 5.7.1 The user in humanist design
[P221|Normal] User-centred design begins from the needs and experience of the user; participatory design involves users in the process; co-creation and co-research extend participation to generating ideas and producing knowledge (Sanders and Stappers, 2008; von Hippel, 2005). These traditions have improved design practice and given users a voice they did not have before.
[P222|Normal] But they carry humanist assumptions: the user as a centre of intention, the artefact as a tool, participation as extracting knowledge from users and transferring it into a design. The designer decides what counts as knowledge and what counts as a good design; the user is consulted, but the frame of the consultation is set elsewhere.
[P223|Normal] The assumptions are inadequate for a technology whose performance depends on the arrangement around it. If the problem is the water supply, the tenure arrangement or the maintenance contract, consulting users about stove preferences will not solve it. Participation has to extend to the elements the design tradition treats as context.
[P224|Heading 3] 5.7.2 The end-user as a participant in an assemblage
[P225|Normal] A posthumanist reconstruction treats the end-user as a position within an assemblage rather than a centre of intention: the person whose practices, body, dwelling and relationships are enrolled in the technology. Their agency is real but not the only agency at work — the artefact, the fuel, the infrastructure and the institution also act. This does not diminish the user; it makes the user's knowledge more important, because the person who lives in the relation has knowledge the designer does not: situated, embodied, practical, not always articulate or consistent, but indispensable.
[P226|Heading 3] 5.7.3 What kind of knowledge do end-users hold?
[P227|Normal] The literature often asserts that users hold valuable knowledge without saying what kind. Five types are relevant here.
[P228|Normal] Tacit knowledge is embedded in practice and hard to articulate: a cook knows when a pot is right by sound and feel; a household knows how much paraffin to buy without calculating it. Situated knowledge is produced from a position (Haraway, 1988): a tenant knows the landlord's rules as an owner does not, and a woman knows the risks of cooking in a shared kitchen in ways a male researcher may not. Embodied knowledge is held in the body — the effort of carrying water, the smoke in the eyes — and cannot be reduced to information (Hayles, 1999).
[P229|Normal] Practice-based knowledge is how a task is actually done — workarounds, repairs, substitutions, informal rules — as distinct from how it is supposed to be done. Cultural knowledge is what a technology means: the appropriateness of a fuel for a dish, the symbolism of a flame, the norms governing who cooks and when. A design process that recognises these types will elicit them differently: interviews for articulate knowledge; observation and transects for tacit and practice-based knowledge; diaries and photovoice for embodied and situated knowledge; and workshops for cultural knowledge, if organised to allow disagreement.
[P230|Heading 3] 5.7.4 Culture as constitutive, not as a barrier
[P231|Normal] Treating culture as a barrier to acceptance is misleading, and this study does not do so. A cultural practice is part of the standard against which a technology is judged: if a dish requires a visible flame, a technology with an invisible flame is inadequate for that dish. The question is not how to overcome culture but how to design a technology that can be enrolled in it, or how to negotiate a change in practice the household considers acceptable.
[P232|Normal] This does not mean accepting all practices uncritically. The analysis must distinguish a preference from a constraint and from a compromise: a household using paraffin because electricity is unaffordable is managing a constraint, not expressing a cultural preference. The design response differs in each case.
[P233|Heading 3] 5.7.5 Participation and its failure conditions
[P234|Normal] Participation is not automatically good, and a framework that ignores its failure modes will romanticise the community it studies. Tokenism means residents are invited but unheard, their presence legitimising a decision already made (Arnstein, 1969). Elite capture lets the already-advantaged dominate the process and direct the benefits to themselves. Expectation inflation breaks trust when promises exceed delivery.
[P235|Normal] Consultation fatigue sets in when communities are asked repeatedly to participate in projects that do not endure, and the cost falls on those with the least time. Framing power means the agenda and vocabulary are set by the project, so residents can respond within the frame but not change it. The labour of participation is counted in meetings attended, not in effort contributed, and is often borne by women. Exit means the project ends and the arrangements built around it collapse. A posthumanist analysis adds material exclusion: a resident willing to participate may be unable to attend because the meeting is at a time she is cooking, in a place she cannot reach, or in a language she does not speak — a barrier that is material and institutional, and can be designed against.
[P236|Heading 3] 5.7.6 Acceptance re-theorised
[P237|Normal] The study replaces the attitudinal construct of acceptance with a relational one. Acceptance is a pattern of sustained use that emerges when technology, infrastructure, household practice, cost and social norms align. It is not a decision made once; it is an achievement that has to be reproduced every day.
[P238|Normal] The definition has four implications. Acceptance is observable in practice, not only in stated intention: a household that says it likes the system but does not use it has not accepted it. It is provisional, lost when a part fails or a price rises. It is distributed, depending on the maintenance arrangement, the fuel supply and the institution as much as on the household. And it is not the same as justice: a technology can be accepted because the household has no alternative.
[P239|Normal] The definition also makes acceptance measurable in a way that is useful for design: the study can observe use over time, record the points at which use declines, and trace the relations that changed. That is more demanding than a satisfaction survey, but it produces evidence that can be acted on.
[P240|Heading 2] 5.8 Posthumanism and the Systems Living Lab
[P241|Normal] Section 4.4 introduced the Living Lab as a real-world arrangement for co-creation, differentiated by who drives it and whether it is primarily a space, a method or a network (Bergvall-Kåreborn and Ståhlbröst, 2009; Leminen et al., 2012; Mbatha and Musango, 2022). The question here is what would make such a lab systemic rather than systemic in name.
[P242|Normal] Systems thinking adds three resources. Boundary critique: Checkland (1981) argues that a system's boundary is a choice and Midgley (2000) that the choice has political consequences, determining whose interests are considered. Feedback: Meadows (2008) shows that system behaviour is produced by loops, delays and stocks, not by the intentions of its members. Emergence: Cilliers (1998) argues that complex systems have properties not predictable from their components, and that every model is a reduction.
[P243|Normal] A Living Lab becomes systemic, in this study, when three things are true. Its boundary is chosen through explicit critique rather than convention, and what is excluded is recorded. Its feedback architecture is designed: the lab collects data on the effects of its own interventions and uses them to revise them. And its evaluation criteria derive from the system's purpose rather than the project's outputs: it asks whether the arrangement became more just and more secure, not only whether the prototype worked.
[P244|Normal] There is a further, distinctly posthumanist point. A Living Lab is an apparatus in Barad's sense: it does not observe a phenomenon from outside but participates in producing it. The questions asked, the instruments used, the people included and the data recorded all enact a cut, making some things visible and others invisible. Reflexivity about the cut is a condition of validity, not an optional virtue: a lab that presents its findings as neutral observations has misunderstood its own role.
[P245|Normal] The practical consequences follow. The lab documents its own decisions — why this site, these participants, this technology, these indicators. Dissenting views are recorded, not smoothed into consensus. The lab is accountable to the people it studies as well as to the funder. And the evaluation asks what the lab itself changed, including unintended changes.
[P246|Normal] From this argument the study derives six design principles for a systemic Living Lab, stated here and operationalised in Section 6.
[P247|Normal] Boundary transparency: the lab states whose interests are included and excluded, and revisits the boundary as the project develops.
[P248|Normal] Distributed participation: participation is designed around the material conditions of residents' lives, including time, location, language, care responsibilities and safety.
[P249|Normal] Configuration as prototype: the lab prototypes the arrangement, including the artefact, the fuel supply, the maintenance arrangement, the tariff and the governance rules.
[P250|Normal] Reflexive apparatus: the lab records its own interventions and treats them as part of the data.
[P251|Normal] Justice as an outcome and a process: the lab evaluates distribution, procedure, recognition and restoration, and applies the same criteria to its own operation.
[P252|Normal] Exit and durability: the lab plans for its own withdrawal from the start, including who will own, maintain and finance the arrangement after the project ends.
[P253|Normal] These principles are not a recipe. They are commitments that shape how the lab is organised, and they can be assessed.

[P268|Heading 2] 5.11 Justification for selecting posthumanism
[P269|Normal] Six criteria are used to select the theory: explanatory power, ontological fit, capacity to handle human and non-human elements, normative adequacy, empirical tractability and institutional fit.
[P270|Normal] Explanatory power. Posthumanism explains why identical technologies produce different outcomes in different households and streets, why participation can fail even when it is well organised, and why justice is a property of the arrangement rather than an add-on.
[P271|Normal] Ontological fit. The research questions are about relations, not about individuals or variables, and posthumanism is the only candidate that takes the relation as its primary unit: TAM takes the individual, diffusion theory the population, practice theory the practice. Each captures something, but none captures the full configuration.
[P272|Normal] Human and non-human elements. The study has to hold people, artefacts, infrastructures, fuels, institutions and ecologies in one frame, and posthumanism does this without reducing one to the other. Systems thinking can also do it, but it tends to treat the elements as components of a functional whole rather than as participants with unequal power.
[P273|Normal] Normative adequacy. The study is about justice, and posthumanism supplies a normative vocabulary that is not reducible to efficiency, complemented by energy justice, which supplies the criteria; the combination is stronger than either alone.
[P274|Normal] Empirical tractability. Posthumanism is often criticised as abstract, and the study addresses the criticism by operationalising the concepts as observable relations: intra-action becomes the relation between artefact, household and infrastructure; distributed agency the set of actors that must be enrolled for the system to work; situated knowledge the knowledge residents hold and specific methods elicit. The test of the framework is whether it can be used in the field, not whether it is elegant.
[P275|Normal] Institutional fit. The theory was introduced by the supervisor and is supported by the research programme in which the study is located — a reason to take it seriously, not a sufficient reason to adopt it. The argument of this section is that the theory is defensible on its merits; if it were not, the institutional fit would be a reason to negotiate rather than to proceed.
[P276|Normal] Several alternatives were considered.
[P277|Normal] TAM and UTAUT were rejected for the reasons set out in Section 5.9: they are useful for measuring attitudes but not for explaining configurations. Diffusion of innovations explains how an innovation spreads through a population, which is useful for understanding adoption curves and the role of early adopters, but it is less useful for understanding why a technology that has spread can be abandoned, or why the benefits of diffusion are unevenly distributed.
[P278|Normal] Social practice theory is close to this study in its attention to routine, materiality and convention (Shove, 2003; Stephenson et al., 2010) and informs the analysis of cooking and fuel stacking; its limitation is that it explains persistence better than intervention. Actor-network theory is the closest alternative: it shares the commitment to symmetry between humans and non-humans and offers a rich vocabulary for tracing associations, but it is deliberately indifferent to normative questions, and this study needs a theory that can say something about justice as well as association.
[P279|Normal] The capability approach (Nussbaum, 2011) provides a normative framework for wellbeing and freedom, compatible with this study's justice dimension, but it does not, by itself, account for technology or material agency, so it is a possible complement rather than a foundation. Political ecology and institutional theory explain the political and organisational conditions of energy transitions and inform the analysis of municipal capacity, but they operate at a different level from the design of a local arrangement. Systems thinking alone was rejected because it can describe a configuration without asking whether it is just; it is retained as a methodological complement.
[P280|Normal] The verdict is that posthumanism is retained as the primary foundation because it is the only candidate that supplies an ontology adequate to the human–technology–infrastructure relation at the heart of the study. It is not sufficient on its own: systems thinking supplies the tools, energy justice the criteria, Living Lab theory the method, and socio-technical theory the wider transition context.
[P281|Normal] The theory also has limits that should be stated. It explains individual motivation poorly, it does not produce measurable acceptance scores, it offers limited guidance on policy design, and it can be difficult to write clearly. These limits are real, and the study manages them by using mixed qualitative methods and by keeping the empirical claims modest.
[P282|Heading 2] 5.12 Synthesis: theoretical propositions
[P283|Normal] The framework can be stated as six propositions. They are not hypotheses in the statistical sense; they are claims that the empirical work will examine, qualify and, where necessary, revise.
[P284|Normal] P1. Acceptance is an emergent property of a socio-material configuration: interventions that change only the artefact will produce unstable acceptance, and acceptance will vary with water, space, fuel supply and maintenance arrangements, not only with attitudes.
[P285|Normal] P2. End-user agency is distributed across people, artefacts and institutions: participation mechanisms that address only human voice will fail to shift configurations, and projects that consult residents without changing the material arrangement will not produce durable change.
[P286|Normal] P3. Justice outcomes depend on which actors and materials are admitted to the intra-action: the boundary of the project — who is in the room, what counts as evidence, which infrastructure is treated as fixed — is a justice decision, and narrow boundaries will produce benefits that are captured by those inside them.
[P287|Normal] P4. A Living Lab is an apparatus that produces the phenomena it studies: reflexivity about the lab's own cuts is a condition of validity, and a lab that does not document its own interventions will misattribute its findings to the community.
[P288|Normal] P5. Acceptance and justice are related but not identical: high acceptance can coexist with injustice, as when tenants pay more than owners or women bear additional labour.
[P289|Normal] P6. Participation has material conditions: the failure of participation is often a failure of design rather than a lack of community interest, and attendance and engagement will vary with the time, place, language and care arrangements of the meeting.

[P293|Heading 2] 5.13 Chapter summary
[P294|Normal] This section has argued that energy interventions in informal settlements fail for theoretical as well as technical reasons. Dominant models treat acceptance as an individual decision about a finished artefact, and in doing so exclude the relations that determine whether a technology works. Critical posthumanism supplies the alternative ontology — entities emerging through relations, agency distributed, matter generative, knowledge situated — complemented by energy justice for normative criteria, systems thinking for mapping relations and feedback, and Living Lab theory for organising collaboration.
[P295|Normal] The framework has limits: it does not specify distributive criteria, does not produce policy instruments, and can be difficult to operationalise. The study manages these limits by treating posthumanism as the foundation rather than the whole, energy justice as the operational complement, and by stating its claims in examinable form. The framework yields six propositions about acceptance, agency, boundary, apparatus, justice and participation, which Section 6 converts into components, relationships and indicators. The division of labour is deliberate: Section 5 explains why the study thinks the way it does; Section 6 shows what it will look at.
[P296|Heading 1] 6. Conceptual Framework
[P297|Heading 2] 6.1 From theory to model
[P298|Normal] Section 5 ended with six propositions about acceptance, agency, boundary, apparatus, justice and participation. They are abstract: none of them tells a researcher what to do on the first day of fieldwork.
[P299|Normal] This section converts the propositions into a working model called SETILL: a Systemic Living Lab for Just Energy Transition. It has ten components, each naming something the lab has to organise, decide or measure. The components are not stages; they run in parallel and feed one another, and the numbering is a convenience for reference. The distinction between the sections is that between a theoretical and a conceptual framework: Section 5 justifies the ontology, this section builds the model it implies, with enough specificity that another researcher could apply it and enough openness that the case can surprise it. A model that cannot be surprised is not a research instrument.
[P300|Heading 2] 6.2 What the framework is and is not
[P301|Normal] The framework is a structured account of the elements that must be aligned for a community-scale energy innovation to be taken up, sustained and judged fair in an informal settlement. It is used in three ways: as a design heuristic for the project team, as an analytical frame for the data, and as an evaluation instrument.
[P302|Normal] It is not a theory of everything. It does not explain national energy politics, model the electricity market or predict adoption rates. It is deliberately local, because the local configuration is where the fate of a decentralised technology is decided.
[P303|Normal] It is also not a checklist to be completed mechanically. Each component raises a question that has to be answered in the specific case, and the answers will differ between settlements with different markets, space and organised structures. The framework's contribution is the set of questions, not a set of answers. Three commitments from Section 5 shape every component: the unit of analysis is the configuration, not the artefact and not the individual; justice is assessed throughout, not added at the end; and the lab is part of what it studies, so its own operation has to be documented.

[P318|Heading 3] 6.4.1 C1 Boundary critique and stakeholder mapping
[P319|Normal] Every project draws a boundary, and the boundary decides who is consulted, whose costs are counted and which infrastructure is treated as fixed. In most projects the boundary is drawn by convention and therefore cannot be questioned. This component makes it an explicit decision: the lab produces a dated boundary statement naming who is included, who is excluded and why, noting the actors who could affect the outcome but are not in the room — the landlord who controls the meter, the councillor who controls access, the department that owns the waste route, the supplier who stocks the part. The statement is revisited each cycle, because the boundary that made sense at the start may not make sense once fieldwork begins.
[P320|Normal] The component derives from Checkland's (1981) argument that a system is a way of organising inquiry rather than an object in the world, and from Midgley's (2000) boundary critique, which asks whose interests a framing serves; Arnstein (1969) informs the link between boundary and power. The test is simple: can the team produce a dated boundary statement with reasons, and show that it changed at least once in response to something learned in the field? If not, the boundary was probably inherited rather than chosen.
[P321|Heading 3] 6.4.2 C2 Situated diagnosis
[P322|Normal] A diagnosis is an account of what the problem is, and in energy projects it is usually technical: the settlement lacks a reliable cooking fuel, the waste is not collected, the grid connection is inadequate. Such statements may be true, but they are made from a position. A woman cooking for eight, a tenant whose rent includes electricity, a landlord, an artisan and a municipal official will describe the same settlement differently. This component treats those differences as data: the lab collects accounts from multiple positions and uses them together, rather than selecting one as the accurate description. The methods capture practice and embodiment as well as opinion: transect walks, household diaries, photovoice, kitchen observation and key-informant interviews.
[P323|Normal] The component draws on Haraway's (1988) argument that knowledge is always situated, and on the South African literature showing that household energy practices are organised by gender, so that a diagnosis which ignores this mis-specifies the problem (Musango et al., 2020; Anditi et al., 2022; Musango, 2022). The test is whether the diagnosis contains accounts that differ by position, and whether those differences changed something in the design. A diagnosis that produces a single coherent story is suspicious; settlements are not coherent.
[P324|Heading 3] 6.4.3 C3 Participation architecture
[P325|Normal] Participation is often organised as an event: a meeting is announced, people attend, and attendance is recorded as participation. That measures presence, not influence, and it excludes those whose time is not their own. This component treats participation as a design problem: what would have to be true for a tenant who works all day, a woman with small children, a person with a disability or an elderly resident to take part on equal terms? The answers are practical: meeting times that fit the working day, venues within walking distance, childcare, translation into the languages people actually use, separate sessions where mixed groups would silence some voices, and payment for time where the project can afford it.
[P326|Normal] The component derives from Arnstein's (1969) distinction between consultation, partnership and citizen control, and from the co-design literature, which treats users as participants in generating ideas rather than sources of requirements (Sanders and Stappers, 2008; Leminen et al., 2012). The test is whether attendance is broad and stable across the cycles, and whether the people who attend can change decisions. A participation architecture that produces good attendance and no change has failed.
[P327|Heading 3] 6.4.4 C4 Configuration design
[P328|Normal] The fourth component most directly distinguishes SETILL from a conventional Living Lab. There the artefact is the object of design and the context the setting; here the configuration is the object of design, and the process must specify the other elements — fuel supply, water source, space, maintenance arrangement, spare-parts route, skills, tariff and rules of use. For biogas this means the specification includes the feedstock supply chain, the water budget, siting, gas storage, stove type, digestate disposal, the maintenance schedule and the person responsible for each task. A specification covering only digester volume and gas yield is incomplete, however technically sound.
[P329|Normal] The component rests on Barad's (2007) argument that properties emerge through intra-action, on Bennett's (2010) account of thing-power, and on practice theory, which shows that technologies are enrolled in existing routines (Shove, 2003; Stephenson et al., 2010). The test is whether the specification names the non-technical requirements explicitly, and whether the prototype is a prototype of the arrangement or only of the vessel.
[P330|Heading 3] 6.4.5 C5 Governance and ownership
[P331|Normal] A community-scale system needs a decision-making structure. Who sits on the management committee, how are members chosen and removed, who owns the asset if the funder withdraws, who authorises and pays for a repair, who resolves a dispute over access? These questions are usually answered by default at the end of a project: the asset is registered to an organisation that may not function, the committee is whoever attended the final meeting, and the dispute route is a phone number that stops working when the project officer leaves. This component decides them explicitly and early, producing a written agreement in the local language naming the owner, the managing body, the decision rules, the tariff principle and the dispute route — a document residents can describe and hold each other to.
[P332|Normal] The component draws on the commons literature, particularly Ostrom's (1990) principle that boundaries, rules and conflict-resolution mechanisms should be defined by the users themselves, and on von Wirth et al. (2019), who show that a lab's durability depends on its embedding in local institutions. The test is whether residents can describe, in their own words, who owns the system and what happens when it breaks. If the answer is “the project”, the component is incomplete.
[P333|Heading 3] 6.4.6 C6 Cost incidence and financing
[P334|Normal] Every intervention distributes cost. Some is financial: capital, operating expenses, fuel, spare parts. Some is labour: collecting feedstock, carrying water, cleaning the digester, attending meetings. Some is risk: the possibility that the system fails and the household falls back on a costlier option. This component traces the distribution — who pays, in what form, at which point in the service chain — and disaggregates by tenure, income and gender, because the same nominal cost can be trivial for an owner and decisive for a tenant. It draws on the energy justice literature on affordability and the distribution of burdens (Sovacool and Dworkin, 2015; Walker and Day, 2012), and on the argument that unpaid labour is a cost even when no money changes hands.
[P335|Normal] The test is a cost profile by household type that includes unpaid labour. A project reporting a low cost per household without disaggregating has not answered the question.
[P336|Heading 3] 6.4.7 C7 Capability and local enterprise
[P337|Normal] A Living Lab that leaves a working system but no capacity to maintain it has not succeeded. This component treats capability as an outcome in its own right: skills in construction, operation, maintenance and repair; a local supplier of spare parts; a small enterprise able to service other installations; people who can train others. It is informed by Braidotti's (2013) distinction between potestas and potentia, framing capacity-building as an increase in the community's power to act, and is compatible with the capability approach (Nussbaum, 2011). The test is whether the skills persist after the project team leaves; a workshop that produces certificates but no practice has not built capability.
[P338|Heading 3] 6.4.8 C8 Justice evaluation
[P339|Normal] This component is the operational heart of the framework's normative claim. It turns each justice dimension into indicators with a data source and a point of comparison, set out in Table 4.4 and discussed in Section 6.6. It is not a scoring exercise: it produces a profile showing where the arrangement performs well and where it does not, shared with participants before it is finalised, with disagreements recorded.
[P340|Heading 3] 6.4.9 C9 Reflexive monitoring
[P341|Normal] A Living Lab is an apparatus in Barad's (2007) sense: it participates in producing the phenomenon it studies, and its questions, participants and records enact a cut. This component makes the cut visible through three records. A decision log: what was decided, by whom, on what basis, and which alternatives were rejected. An intervention register: every action that could have affected the outcome, from installing the digester to choosing a meeting venue. And a reflection record: the research team's notes on its own assumptions, surprises and discomforts.
[P342|Normal] These records allow the analysis to distinguish project effects from other changes in the settlement, and allow the reader to assess the lab's own role in what it found. Without them, a finding about residents' preferences may be a finding about the questions the project asked.
[P343|Heading 3] 6.4.10 C10 Exit, durability and scaling
[P344|Normal] The final component plans for the end from the beginning. It asks what will still be working two years after the project closes, who will maintain it, how it will be financed, and what happens to the relationships. The exit plan specifies the handover (asset, records, spare parts, training materials, contacts), the finance (a maintenance fund, a tariff, a municipal budget line or a combination), and the review: a date at which the arrangement is assessed by the people who depend on it.
[P345|Normal] The component draws on von Wirth et al. (2019), who show that a lab's impact depends on how it is embedded in wider structures, and on Mbatha and Musango's (2022) observation that many energy Living Labs in Africa are not designed for continuation. The test is a named owner, a budget line and a maintenance route that do not depend on the research team.
[P346|Heading 2] 6.5 The relationships between the components
[P347|Normal] The components are not independent, and four relationships matter. A dependency: C4 depends on C2, because a configuration cannot be designed without a diagnosis of what it currently is — yet projects routinely design technology before diagnosing the arrangement, and discover during implementation that water, space or maintenance were never assessed. A tension: C3 and C10 pull against each other, because broad participation takes time and later cycles are under pressure to close; the framework requires the lab to record how the tension was managed. A feedback: C8 feeds C4, because the justice evaluation produces findings that should change the design — if tenants bear a disproportionate cost, the tariff or the siting must respond. A precondition: C1 and C5 make the other components possible, since a lab without a boundary statement does not know what it is assessing, and one without a governance agreement cannot hand anything over.

[P357|Heading 2] 6.7 How the framework will be used in the analysis
[P358|Normal] The framework does three jobs in the analysis; stating them guards against the framework becoming a set of headings under which anything can be filed. The first is deductive: the ten components provide an initial coding frame for interview and observational data — a passage about the timing of meetings is coded to C3, one about ownership of the digester to C5 — which gives the analysis a starting structure and comparability across sources.
[P359|Normal] The second is abductive. The framework is expected to be incomplete, and where the data do not fit a component, or a component proves unimportant, that is a finding. The analysis records the misfits and uses them to revise the model — the moment at which the study can contribute to theory rather than merely apply it (Alvesson and Kärreman, 2007).
[P360|Normal] The third is evaluative. The justice criteria are applied at baseline and follow-up, and the profile is one of the main outputs: a description of how a particular arrangement distributed benefits, voice, recognition and repair at a particular time, not a verdict on the community or the team. The risk — an evaluation conducted by the project team about its own work — is managed in three ways: criteria specified in advance, data that the team did not generate (diaries, observation by an assistant not involved in delivery), and discussion of the draft profile with participants before it is finalised.
[P361|Heading 2] 6.8 Boundaries of the framework
[P362|Normal] The framework has four limits. It is designed for community-scale, decentralised technology in a low-income urban settlement and may not transfer to household devices, networked infrastructure or rural settings without modification. It assumes a research team present in the field over an extended period; a three-month consultancy could use it as a checklist but could not complete the cycles or observe durability. It depends on access: a lab that cannot reach tenants will produce a partial picture, and the boundary statement should say so. And it is a framework for design and evaluation, not causal inference: it can show that an arrangement changed and describe how, but cannot isolate the lab's contribution from other changes. Section 7 addresses this through case logic and the reflexive record, not statistical control.
[P363|Heading 2] 6.9 Chapter summary
[P364|Normal] This section has converted the theoretical propositions of Section 5 into a ten-component model covering the frame (boundary, diagnosis, participation), the intervention (configuration, governance, cost, capability) and the assessment (justice, reflexivity, exit), each with a core question, activities and a test. The justice dimension is operationalised in twelve indicators with data sources. The framework is offered as an instrument that can be used, criticised and revised; its value will be judged by whether it produces findings a conventional technology-transfer approach would have missed, and by whether those findings are useful to the people who live in the settlement. That judgement can only be made after the fieldwork, and the design in Section 7 is intended to make it possible.
[P365|Heading 1] 7. Research Design and Methodology
[P366|Heading 2] 7.1 Research paradigm
[P367|Normal] The study works within a critical posthumanist paradigm, and this has four consequences for the design. The first is ontological. Reality is treated as relational: entities, including technologies and households, acquire their properties through the relations in which they are enrolled. This does not mean the digester is not real; it means that what the digester is, for the purposes of the study, includes the water, the feedstock, the space, the labour and the rules that sustain it.
[P368|Normal] The second is epistemological: knowledge is situated and partial. The researcher is part of the arrangement, and the knowledge produced is knowledge from a particular position — a condition to be documented, not a weakness to be corrected by detachment. The third is methodological: if the object is a configuration, the methods must capture relations rather than variables, pointing to qualitative methods that follow actors, materials and practices, and to analytical tools that represent feedback. The fourth is axiological: the study is not neutral about justice, and it reports whether the arrangement improved or worsened the position of the people who live in it, even when the answer is unflattering.
[P369|Normal] These commitments place the study in the interpretive and critical traditions rather than the positivist one, with implications for how quality is judged (Section 7.7). They do not imply a rejection of technical evidence: the study records gas yields, water use, cost and maintenance events, treating them as data about the configuration.
[P370|Heading 2] 7.2 Research design
[P371|Normal] The study uses a single embedded case study, organised through the four stages of Design Research Methodology (DRM). DRM provides the internal logic of a design-oriented project — what must be clarified, described, prescribed and evaluated; the case study provides the empirical logic — how evidence is collected from a bounded instance and how claims are generalised.
[P372|Normal] Figure 5.1 shows the design: the top band carries the two lenses that shape every stage; the four DRM stages follow with their activities; the orange line marks the iterative return from evaluation to description and prescription, the point of a design study; and the bottom band locates the sequence within the case.

[P375|Heading 3] 7.2.1 Design Research Methodology
[P376|Normal] DRM was developed by Blessing and Chakrabarti (2009) for research in engineering design, and it has been widely used in engineering management. It distinguishes four stages.
[P377|Normal] Stage 1: Clarification of requirements. The problem, the current situation, the desired situation and the criteria for success are established. In this study Stage 1 covers the literature review, the theoretical and conceptual frameworks, site scoping and the ethics application; its output is a set of requirements for the SETILL framework and the biogas configuration, with the criteria against which the pilot will be evaluated.
[P378|Normal] Stage 2: Descriptive study I. The current situation is examined in depth: key-informant interviews, resident focus groups, transect walks, household energy diaries and photovoice. The output is an evidence-based account of how energy services are currently produced in the settlement, who does the work, and what fails and why.
[P379|Normal] Stage 3: Prescriptive study. An intervention is developed and specified to move the situation toward the desired state: co-design workshops, the site-specific specification of the SETILL model, the specification of the biogas configuration and construction of the prototype. The output is a documented design with explicit assumptions and requirements.
[P380|Normal] Stage 4: Descriptive study II. The intervention is evaluated against the Stage 1 criteria and the understanding of the problem revised: pilot evaluation, justice assessment, revision of the framework and feedback of findings to the community. The stages are not a waterfall — Stage 4 feeds back to Stages 2 and 3, and a Stage 2 finding can send the researcher back to Stage 1. The design allows two full cycles and a partial third, subject to time and access.
[P381|Heading 3] 7.2.2 Why a case study
[P382|Normal] A case study is appropriate when the research asks how or why a phenomenon works in its real-life context, when the researcher cannot manipulate behaviour, and when phenomenon and context are not clearly separable (Yin, 2009). All three conditions hold. The case is embedded rather than holistic: within the settlement there are four units of analysis — households, women's groups, the municipal service and the technology team — analysed separately and then together, because the configuration is produced at their intersection.
[P383|Normal] Flyvbjerg (2006) argues that a well-chosen case can produce knowledge unavailable from statistical samples, particularly where the aim is to understand mechanisms rather than estimate frequencies — the aim here. The study does not claim its findings describe all South African informal settlements; it claims they describe a configuration of a type that recurs, and it provides enough detail about the conditions for readers to judge whether the mechanism would operate elsewhere.

[P399|Heading 3] 7.5.1 Interviews
[P400|Normal] Semi-structured interviews will be used with key informants and a subset of households. The guide is organised around the ten components but does not follow them mechanically: it begins with the person's own account of how energy is obtained and used, moves to the points at which it fails, then to the surrounding arrangements, and finally to decision-making and fairness. Interviews will be conducted in the participant's preferred language, audio-recorded with consent, transcribed and, where necessary, translated with the translation checked by a second speaker and documented as the analytical act it is.
[P401|Heading 3] 7.5.2 Focus groups
[P402|Normal] Focus groups will be held separately for women, men, youth and tenants, because mixed groups in this context tend to reproduce existing authority, with older men speaking first and at length; a final mixed session will test whether the separate accounts survive when the groups are brought together. Facilitation and note-taking will be by trained fieldworkers of the same gender where possible, recorded with consent.
[P403|Heading 3] 7.5.3 Transect walks
[P404|Normal] A transect walk is a structured walk through the settlement with a resident guide, recording what is observed and what the guide says. It produces spatial evidence interviews miss: where the water points are, how far the wood is, which plots have space for a digester, where waste accumulates, which paths flood, how the settlement's edges are policed. Walks will be conducted at different times of day, and notes and photographs recorded with consent, with locations described so that individuals cannot be identified.
[P405|Heading 3] 7.5.4 Household energy diaries
[P406|Normal] Diaries capture the ordinary sequence of a day. Participating households will keep a simple diary in the local language, with pictograms for fuel types and a page for each day, supported by twice-weekly fieldworker visits because written diaries exclude participants with limited literacy. The diary records what was cooked, on what, for how long and at what cost; when the electricity was off; what was used instead; who did the work; and what went wrong. The analysis will treat it as an account produced with the researcher, not as an objective log.
[P407|Heading 3] 7.5.5 Photovoice
[P408|Normal] Photovoice asks participants to photograph what matters to them and explain the images in a group discussion, shifting the framing from the researcher's questions to the participant's attention. It produces evidence about material conditions residents consider important, and a record that can be shown to the municipality. Ethical care is required: participants will be trained in consent for photography, asked not to photograph identifiable individuals without permission, and retain control over which images are used in publications.
[P409|Heading 3] 7.5.6 Co-design workshops
[P410|Normal] Four workshops will be held in Stage 3: the first frames the problem and tests whether the Stage 2 diagnosis is recognised; the second develops the service model — who operates the system, who pays, who decides, what happens when it fails; the third specifies the technical configuration; the fourth reviews the prototype and agrees the evaluation criteria. The workshops will use visual and physical materials rather than text, because text-based workshops exclude participants who are not confident readers, and disagreement will be recorded rather than resolved by the facilitator.
[P411|Heading 3] 7.5.7 Documents and secondary data
[P412|Normal] The study will also collect documents: municipal Integrated Development Plans, waste and energy policies, records from previous interventions, equipment specifications and maintenance logs, used to establish institutional conditions and to check accounts given in interviews.

[P417|Heading 3] 7.6.1 Reflexive thematic analysis
[P418|Normal] Thematic analysis is used in its reflexive form (Braun and Clarke, 2006, 2021), which treats themes as produced by the analyst's engagement with the data rather than as latent objects waiting to be found. Coding is deductive, using the ten components, and inductive, allowing codes the framework did not anticipate, through the six phases: familiarisation, initial coding, generating, reviewing, defining and naming themes, and writing. Coding will be conducted on transcripts in the original language where possible. A second coder will code ten transcripts independently; the purpose is not positivist inter-rater reliability but a check that the coding frame is applied consistently and a surfacing of the analyst's assumptions. A reflexive journal will run through the analysis, and extracts will appear in the methodology chapter to show how interpretations changed.
[P419|Heading 3] 7.6.2 Situational mapping
[P420|Normal] Situational mapping (Clarke, 2005) produces three kinds of map: situational maps of the major human and non-human elements and their relations; social worlds and arena maps of collective actors; and positional maps of discursive positions on contested issues. The method suits the study because it can represent non-human elements without reducing them to context — the digester, the water point, the meter, the landfill and the ward committee in one frame — and positional maps are particularly useful for the disagreements the study expects: on formalisation, on whether women should manage the system, on whether the municipality can be trusted.
[P421|Heading 3] 7.6.3 Causal loop diagramming
[P422|Normal] Causal loop diagrams represent feedback: how a change in one variable affects another, and how the effect returns. They will be built with participants, to represent their own accounts of why systems succeed or fail, and by the researcher, to synthesise the evidence; one for the settlement's current arrangements and one for the proposed configuration. The comparison is itself a finding, showing which loops the intervention is intended to change. The diagrams are qualitative representations, not simulation models.
[P423|Heading 3] 7.6.4 Diffractive reading
[P424|Normal] Diffraction, in Barad's (2007) sense, reads insights through one another rather than against one another. Here it compares data sets that describe the same phenomenon from different positions: a household diary and an official's account of the same service; a woman's description of cooking and a designer's specification of the stove. The analysis asks what each account makes visible, what each obscures, and what difference the difference makes. The output is not a synthesis that resolves disagreement but an account of how the phenomenon is produced differently in different apparatuses — directly relevant to RQ3, because it shows acceptance is not a single fact about a single object.
[P425|Heading 3] 7.6.5 From analysis to framework revision
[P426|Normal] The analysis is also a test of the framework. For each component there are three outcomes: confirmed as important and adequately specified; confirmed as important but under-specified, and revised; or found unimportant in this case, and the framework revised to say so. The revision is documented in a table showing the original component, the evidence and the revised version — itself a contribution, because it shows what a framework looks like after it has been used.

[P441|Heading 2] 7.10 Limitations of the design
[P442|Normal] The design has four limitations, stated in advance. A single case cannot support claims about prevalence; the researcher's presence changes the configuration, and the reflexive record addresses this without eliminating it; the study depends on a technology that may underperform, in which case the underperformance will be reported and treated as evidence about the configuration; and three years limits what can be said about durability, so the exit plan includes arrangements for follow-up beyond the doctoral period.
[P443|Heading 2] 7.11 Summary of the methodology
[P444|Normal] The study is a single embedded case study conducted through four DRM stages in an informal settlement in Greater Tzaneen. Data are collected through interviews, focus groups, transect walks, diaries, photovoice, co-design workshops and documents, and analysed through reflexive thematic analysis, situational mapping, causal loop diagramming and diffractive reading. Quality is addressed through the eight big-tent criteria and a documented audit trail. Ethics are addressed through procedural clearance and through a relational practice that includes an exit plan and shared findings. The next section describes the case site.
[P445|Heading 1] 8. Context and Case Study
[P446|Heading 2] 8.1 Why Tzaneen
[P447|Normal] The choice of Greater Tzaneen is not arbitrary, and it is not a convenience sample in the ordinary sense. The municipality combines the conditions the study needs: a large population with high service connection and low service quality; an agricultural and agro-processing economy with the organic waste streams that follow; a climate warm enough for anaerobic digestion; and a municipal administration under capacity but not absent. It is a place where the contradictions of the South African transition are visible in a small area — a formal town core surrounded by settlements of varying formality, tenure and services, a few kilometres apart and decades apart in investment. Finally, Limpopo receives less attention in the energy transition literature than Gauteng or the Western Cape despite its population and renewable potential, so a study there adds to a thin evidence base.
[P448|Heading 2] 8.2 The municipality
[P449|Normal] Greater Tzaneen Local Municipality (LIM333), one of four local municipalities in the Mopani District, covers approximately 2,896 square kilometres and had a population of 478,254 at the 2022 census, up from 389,623 in 2011 — growth of about two per cent a year (Statistics South Africa, 2023). There are roughly 129,600 households, averaging 3.7 people, about half headed by women. Just over 16 per cent of adults have no schooling and under nine per cent a higher education qualification. Table 6.1 sets out the indicators that matter for this study.

A household here is likely to be grid-connected while managing its own water, waste and cooking fuel — exactly the configuration the study is about.
[P453|Normal] Two caveats are necessary. The dwelling classification counts a structure as formal if it is a house or flat on a formal erf, and backyard rooms or rented shacks on a formal plot may be counted within it, so the 97.1 per cent formal-dwelling figure understates informal or insecure arrangements; the study will not rely on it and will establish tenure through its own scoping. And “electricity for lighting” records use, not the legality, reliability or capacity of the connection: a household with an informal connection from a neighbour's meter is counted as using electricity. The indicator measures presence, not service.
[P454|Heading 2] 8.3 Energy in the municipality
[P455|Normal] The municipality's energy profile is shaped by three factors. The national grid: distribution is carried out by Eskom rather than a municipal distributor, so outages, tariffs and connection processes are administered at a distance, and municipal officials can advocate but cannot decide. The local economy: agro-processing generates fruit waste, sawdust, poultry litter, abattoir waste and market waste, some already used for feed or compost, some dumped; a digester would compete for a fraction of this stream, and the competition is part of what the study must understand. Household fuel use: as in much of Limpopo, households combine electricity with wood, paraffin and LPG — wood often collected rather than bought, paraffin bought in small quantities at a high cost per unit of energy — and the combination changes with season, price and reliability.
[P456|Normal] The study will not attempt to quantify the municipal energy balance. It will document the household-level configuration and the institutional conditions that shape it.
[P457|Heading 2] 8.4 Waste and water
[P458|Normal] Biogas depends on a feedstock stream and a water supply, both constrained here. Refuse removal reaches about a quarter of households; the rest burn, bury or dump their waste, leaving organic waste dispersed rather than concentrated — a problem for a digester needing a steady supply. The design response is to aggregate waste from a few sources — a market, a poultry operation, a group of households — or to size the digester to the supply that can be guaranteed; the co-design workshops will decide. Water reaches most households through a network, but inside connections are limited and supply intermittent in places; in a settlement where water is carried, a technology needing several hundred litres a week is a different proposition from one needing fifty. The study will measure water use and treat it as a design constraint.
[P459|Heading 2] 8.5 The settlement
[P460|Normal] The specific settlement has not been selected. This is deliberate, a statement about the state of the work rather than an omission. The scoping phase in months one to six will assess candidate settlements against the criteria in Table 5.1 through a site visit, conversations with community structures and a meeting with the municipality, and the outcome will be documented in a site selection memorandum stating which criteria each candidate met and why the chosen site was preferred.
[P461|Normal] What can be said now is what the study expects to find, based on the literature and preliminary conversations: a mix of owner-occupied and rented dwellings, an electricity supply that is connected but unreliable and costly, multiple cooking fuels, uncollected organic waste, water at a shared point. The settlement is likely to have at least one organised structure — a residents' committee, a women's group, a church-based organisation — and to have experience of previous projects that shapes how a new one is received. A settlement that has been promised services before will not take a new promise at face value, and the study should not expect it to.
[P462|Heading 2] 8.6 Relevance to Engineering Management
[P463|Normal] The case is an Engineering Management case, not only an energy case. The field is concerned with designing, operating and improving systems that combine technical and organisational elements, and a community-scale digester is exactly such a system: a technical core with a supply chain, a maintenance regime, a cost structure, a governance arrangement and users whose practices determine whether it works. The decisions that determine its fate — siting, sizing, contracting, staffing, pricing, training, scheduling, risk allocation — are management decisions.
[P464|Normal] The study contributes to the field in three ways: a method for specifying the non-technical requirements of a technical system in a low-income context; an evaluation approach that treats justice as a system performance criterion rather than an external constraint; and evidence about the organisational conditions under which a decentralised energy system is sustained. The same questions arise for solar water heating, container-based sanitation, water ATMs and small-scale PV, and the framework is intended to be portable across them.
[P465|Heading 2] 8.7 Access, permissions and relationships
[P466|Normal] Access will be negotiated in three steps. Institutionally, the study will seek written permission from the municipality and engage the relevant departments — waste management, electricity, planning, local economic development — as participants rather than gatekeepers, with a commitment to share findings. At community level, it will approach the recognised structures, explain the purpose and limits of the project, and ask permission to work in the area; it will not offer benefits in exchange for access, and concerns will be recorded and addressed before fieldwork begins. At household level, participation is individual, refusal carries no consequence for service entitlement, and participants may withdraw with data removed on request where practicable. The study will also establish a small advisory group of residents, municipal officials and a technical expert, meeting twice a year: not a decision-making body, but a channel for critique that does not depend on the researcher's judgement.
[P467|Heading 2] 8.8 Summary of the case
[P468|Normal] Greater Tzaneen offers a setting in which nearly every household is grid-connected while fewer than a third have water inside the dwelling, refuse collection reaches a quarter of households and organic waste is dispersed, and a large share of households are headed by women and depend on multiple cooking fuels. Those conditions make the configuration, rather than the artefact, the central problem. Section 9 states what the study expects to contribute.

[P474|Heading 2] 9.2 Theoretical contribution
[P475|Normal] The theoretical contribution is the argument that acceptance is an emergent property of a socio-material configuration, with consequences for how energy innovation should be designed and evaluated. The claim is not new in its general form — practice theory, socio-technical systems theory and actor-network theory have all argued that technology and social order are co-produced. What is new is the specific combination: a posthumanist ontology applied to the design of a community-scale energy system in a South African informal settlement, with the justice literature supplying the normative criteria, yielding propositions that can be examined and, importantly, can fail.
[P476|Normal] The test of the contribution is whether the framework explains something a TAM study would have missed. The study expects to find at least three such things: households reporting a positive attitude that stop using the system because a material condition changed; households reporting a negative attitude that continue because there is no alternative; and identical technologies producing different outcomes in adjacent households of similar income because the configuration differs in ways an attitude survey cannot detect. If none is found, the contribution is weakened, and the study will report that.
[P477|Heading 2] 9.3 Methodological contribution
[P478|Normal] The methodological contribution is the combination of four analytical practices and the documentation of what each contributes. Reflexive thematic analysis is well established but usually used alone; situational mapping is used in grounded theory but rarely in energy research; causal loop diagramming is used in systems research but rarely with qualitative community data; and diffractive reading is used in posthumanist research but rarely in engineering. The study will report how the four were combined, what each produced, and where they produced incompatible accounts. The combination is not offered as a template: it is a documented experiment in method, with an honest account of its costs — diffractive reading in particular is slow and difficult to explain, and the study will show what it added and what it cost.
[P479|Normal] The second methodological contribution is the operationalisation of justice. The energy justice literature is rich in principles and thin in indicators; the twelve indicators in Table 4.4 attempt to close that gap for a project-scale intervention. They are not the only possible set, and the study will report which proved usable.
[P480|Heading 2] 9.4 Practical contribution
[P481|Normal] For practitioners, the study offers a design protocol and a body of evidence. The protocol treats the configuration as the unit of intervention: a project team would specify the feedstock supply, water budget, siting, maintenance arrangement, spare-parts route and governance rules at the same time as the digester volume and gas yield, and the study will produce a template for that specification, derived from and tested against the case. The evidence concerns the conditions under which a community-scale biogas system is sustained: the literature contains many accounts of why systems fail and few longitudinal accounts of how one was maintained, by whom, at what cost. These outputs are modest, but they are the kind a project manager can use; a framework that is elegant but unusable is of limited value in engineering management.
[P482|Heading 2] 9.5 Policy contribution
[P483|Normal] At the policy level, the study speaks to two questions municipalities currently answer by default. How to evaluate an energy programme against justice criteria: Integrated Development Plans routinely state commitments to redress and inclusion without specifying how they will be assessed, and the indicator set in Table 4.4 offers a way of doing so at project scale with data a municipality could collect. And how to organise participation so that it is more than consultation: the study will document the arrangements that made participation possible for tenants, women and youth, and those that did not — directly relevant to municipal engagement design and to the conditions attached to Just Energy Transition Partnership funding.
[P484|Normal] The study does not claim that its findings will change national policy. It claims that they provide evidence that is currently missing from a policy conversation that is conducted mostly at national scale.
[P485|Heading 2] 9.6 Contribution to the supervisor's research programme
[P486|Normal] The study is located within a research programme on gendered energy innovation in poor urban environments led by the supervisor and colleagues, which has produced a gender analysis framework (Anditi et al., 2022), a systems model of energy service security (Batinge et al., 2022), a co-design toolkit (Petrulaityte et al., 2022), a process for developing energy Living Labs (Mukama et al., 2022) and a posthumanist argument for a social approach to energy transition in engineering education (Aboalez, 2025). This study extends that programme by moving the posthumanist argument from engineering education to settlement-level design, adding a justice evaluation the earlier work identified as necessary but did not operationalise, and providing a detailed case in a new site.
[P487|Normal] The relationship is a strength and a risk: a build on established concepts and access, but also the danger that a study too close to its supervisor's framework will not test it hard enough. The design addresses this through negative case analysis, a critical friend on the advisory group, and an explicit statement of what would count as disconfirming evidence.
[P488|Heading 2] 9.7 Contribution to the researcher's development
[P489|Normal] A doctoral study is also a process of formation, and it is reasonable to state what the researcher expects to learn: competence in qualitative research in a context of poverty and inequality; in applying posthumanist theory to engineering problems; and in managing a multi-stakeholder design process. The fieldwork requires working in a language and setting that are not the researcher's own; the theoretical work requires fluency in concepts not native to engineering; and the design work requires the management skills the study is, in part, about.
[P490|Normal] None of this belongs in a claim about contribution to knowledge; it belongs in the statement of what the study is for, and it shapes the choices in Section 10.

[P502|Normal] The first phase establishes the conditions for the study: a literature update, the development of the interview and workshop instruments, the ethics application and site scoping. The ethics application will be submitted in month 3 assuming a three-month review; if clearance takes longer, scoping can continue because it involves no data collection, but the fieldwork start will slip. The site selection memorandum, completed by month 6, will assess at least three candidate settlements against the criteria in Table 5.1 and state the reasons for the choice.
[P503|Heading 3] 10.2.2 Phase 2: Descriptive study I (months 7–14)
[P504|Normal] The second phase produces the diagnostic account. Key-informant interviews establish the institutional context and identify households and groups to approach; focus groups follow, then transect walks, diaries and photovoice — a deliberate sequence in which earlier methods inform later ones. Twelve to fifteen households will keep diaries for four weeks, ten to twelve residents will take part in photovoice, and twenty to twenty-five household interviews plus eight to ten interviews with non-users will be conducted, adjusted as saturation is assessed. Transcription begins in month 9 and coding in month 10.
[P505|Heading 3] 10.2.3 Phase 3: Analysis and modelling (months 15–21)
[P506|Normal] The third phase consolidates the analysis: the situational maps, the causal loop diagrams, the first revision of the framework and a draft of the findings chapter, submitted to the supervisor for review. A member-checking session presents the emerging findings to participants; it is not a validation exercise in the positivist sense but an opportunity to disagree, with the disagreements recorded.
[P507|Heading 3] 10.2.4 Phase 4: Co-design and prototyping (months 22–27)
[P508|Normal] The fourth phase is the design intervention. Four workshops produce the service model, the governance agreement, the technical specification and the evaluation plan; construction follows by a local contractor with the technology team, using local labour where possible, with commissioning including operator training and a maintenance schedule. This phase carries the highest risk — construction can be delayed by weather, supply, permission or disagreement — and a two-month contingency is built into the schedule.
[P509|Heading 3] 10.2.5 Phase 5: Evaluation and drafting (months 28–33)
[P510|Normal] The fifth phase assesses the intervention and writes the thesis. Monitoring data are collected over six months — gas output, water use, feedstock input, maintenance events, cost and use by household — with follow-up interviews of original participants and a sample of non-participating households. The justice profile is constructed, discussed with participants before finalisation, and the thesis drafted alongside a conference or journal paper.
[P511|Heading 3] 10.2.6 Phase 6: Finalisation (months 34–36)
[P512|Normal] The final phase completes and submits the thesis, and conducts the exit handover: a community feedback session, the transfer of records and contacts, and the establishment of the maintenance arrangement agreed in Phase 4.
[P513|Heading 2] 10.3 Milestones and decision points
[P514|Normal] Five decision points are built into the plan. Month 6, site confirmation: if no site meets the criteria, either a criterion is relaxed with documented reasons or the fieldwork is delayed three months while a new site is scoped. Month 14, framework revision: if the diagnostic evidence does not support the framework, it is revised before the co-design phase — the point at which a major revision is cheapest. Month 22, go/no-go on construction: construction proceeds only if a governance agreement and maintenance arrangement are in place; otherwise the study runs a design-only intervention and reports the reasons, which is itself a finding about the conditions for community-scale technology.
[P515|Normal] Month 27, prototype acceptance: if the system does not perform to specification, the shortfall is documented and analysed as evidence about the configuration. Month 33, submission readiness: if the draft is incomplete, a three-month extension will be requested.
[P516|Heading 2] 10.4 Resources
[P517|Normal] Beyond the researcher's time and the supervisor's guidance, the study needs three kinds of resources. Fieldwork: transport between Johannesburg and Tzaneen, accommodation during fieldwork blocks, participant compensation, facilitator fees, transcription and translation, and printing of instruments. Equipment: biogas system components, a gas stove, plumbing and safety fittings, and monitoring instruments. Software: NVivo or an equivalent qualitative package, and Vensim or an equivalent for causal loop diagrams. Funding will be sought from the university's postgraduate research budget, the host research programme and external funders; a detailed budget is provided separately to the school.
[P518|Heading 2] 10.5 Supervision and progress management
[P519|Normal] The study will be supervised by Dr K. Aboalez, with co-supervision where the school requires it. Progress will be managed through monthly supervision meetings, a written progress report every six months, and an annual review with the school's higher degrees committee. A research journal, recording decisions, problems and reflections, is both a management tool and evidence for the reflexive component. Risk will be reviewed against the register in Table 5.7.

[P526|Heading 2] 11.2 Notes on the chapters
[P527|Normal] Three chapters carry the weight of the argument. Chapter 6 is the diagnostic chapter, doing what the literature review cannot: establishing what the configuration actually is in this place. Chapter 8 is the evaluation chapter, the one most likely to produce uncomfortable findings: it will report the justice profile in full, including the dimensions on which the intervention performed poorly, and the lab's own performance against the same criteria.

[P653|Normal] What participation involves. An interview of about an hour, or a place in a focus group of about ninety minutes, or keeping a diary for four weeks, or taking part in a workshop. You will not be asked to do all of these unless you agree.
[P654|Normal] Risks. There are no physical risks beyond ordinary daily life. Some questions concern money, health and conflict, and may be uncomfortable. You may decline any question.
[P655|Normal] Benefits. There is no payment for participation except where compensation for time is stated. The study may produce a pilot system, but participation does not entitle you to a service.
[P656|Normal] Confidentiality. Your name will not appear in any publication. Recordings and notes will be stored securely and destroyed after five years. Photographs will be used only with your separate permission.
[P657|Normal] Voluntary participation. You may withdraw at any time, and you may ask for your data to be removed.
[P658|Normal] Ethics approval. Ethical clearance will be sought from the Faculty of Engineering and the Built Environment Research Ethics Committee before any fieldwork begins. The approval number will be inserted when it is issued.
[P659|Normal] Contact. The researcher and the supervisor's contact details are given on the form.

[P663|Heading 2] Appendix E: Workshop protocol
[P664|Normal] Each workshop runs for three hours and follows the same structure.
[P665|Normal] Welcome and purpose (15 minutes). Restate what the workshop can and cannot decide.
[P666|Normal] Ground rules (10 minutes). Agreed by the group, including how disagreement will be handled.
[P667|Normal] Evidence presentation (30 minutes). Findings from the diagnostic phase, presented visually, with time for challenge.
[P668|Normal] Small-group task (60 minutes). Groups of five to seven, mixed by gender and tenure where possible, each with a facilitator and a visual template.
[P669|Normal] Plenary (45 minutes). Each group presents; disagreements are recorded on a flip chart rather than resolved.
[P670|Normal] Decision record (20 minutes). What was decided, what was deferred, who will do what.
[P671|Normal] A decision log is completed after each workshop. It records the decision, the alternatives considered, who supported and who opposed, and the reasons given. The log is a primary data source for C9 and for the procedural justice indicators.
[P672|Heading 2] Appendix F: Glossary of theoretical terms
[TABLE 30] 13 rows x 2 cols
  | Term | Plain-language definition
  | Agential cut | The boundary drawn within a phenomenon that separates what is observed from what observes; it is a decision with consequences.
  | Agential realism | Barad's ontology in which entities emerge from relations rather than preceding them.
  | Assemblage | A configuration of human and non-human elements whose relations produce capacities that none has alone.
  | Diffraction | Reading one account through another to see what each makes visible, rather than judging one against the other.
  | Distributed agency | The claim that the capacity to act is spread across people, artefacts, infrastructures and institutions.
  | Intra-action | The relation through which entities acquire their boundaries and properties; distinct from interaction between pre-existing entities.
  | Potestas | Braidotti's term for constraining, negating power.
  | Potentia | Braidotti's term for enabling, capacity-building power.
  | Response-ability | Haraway's term for the practised capacity to respond to others accountably.
  | Situated knowledge | Knowledge produced from a particular position, which can therefore be located and checked.
  | Sympoiesis | Making-with; collaboration as a condition of life rather than a technique.
  | Apparatus | The arrangement of practices, instruments and concepts through which a phenomenon is produced and observed.
