1.1 Introduction 
This chapter explores the research overview of the historical development of the study, the 
background and research real-world problems leading to what the research is all about, a brief 
discussion on study concepts like the systems reinforcing model (SRM), energy culture (EC) 
and sustainable energy access (SEA) framework’s that is contextual to the development of the 
new Systems Energy Technology Innovation Living Lab (SETILL) theoretical model focused 
on gender design for the research study. The research aims, objectives, and research questions 
were also presented for suitable guidance on what the research study intended to accomplish. 
A brief overview of the study's relevance and justification, as well as its methodology, is 
included to help guide the research design. Thereafter, the chosen technology to be used for 
the testing or implementation of the SETILL is discussed briefly. Generally, this chapter 
explores this study for contextual applicability to the topic to develop a new Systems Energy 
Technology Innovation Living Lab (SETILL) focused on gender design and to identify the 
processes that can guide the exploratory theory.  
 
1.2 The overview of the historical development of the research study 
Energy poverty conditions are mostly prevalent in poor rural and urban African informal 
settlement households, most informal settlement households make use of localised energy 
sources but still lack access to clean energy sources and modern energy services (Okoye, 2020). 
Therefore, ‘energy insecurity’ which can also be called ‘energy poverty’ in the context of this 
research study, is challenging and the issues have been generally supported in literature and 
policies in the public domain (Balmer, 2007; McCollum et al, 2017; Sadath and Acharya, 
2017). The highest rate of energy insecurity is in Africa with more than 80% of fuel 
consumption being paraffin (high carbon emitting fuel), biomass, and municipal industrial and 
animal wastes (UNEP, 2017). In developing countries, billions of individuals are unable to 
access clean cooking sources and electricity (Sokona, Mulugetta and Gujba, 2012), and the 
United Nations (2014) indicated that by 2030, over 2.5 million individuals could be unable to 
access clean modern sources of energy for cooking. Therefore, this research study looked in 
the direction of developing a modern energy source for cooking with women as the end-users 
with the Living Lab through a bottom-up approach which will be validated and tested.

Generally, energy poverty challenges are socio-economical, and usually found in poor 
households in urban or rural environments mostly in developing countries. Simply put, energy 
poverty could be referred to as the unavailability of clean energy and energy services in 
households and beyond. Okoye (2020:1) stated that “one of the factors of promoting energy 
poverty mostly comes from socio-cultural environments, which are likely to shape energy use 
patterns and behaviours in households”. However, energy poverty is not only the deficiency of 
access to energy and modern energy services. Thus, there should be no need to attempt to 
reduce energy poverty by means of mere technical concepts and numerical metrics, socially 
and culturally, but of rights and justice, of transformation as a capacity and for gender as an 
economics. This is because the complexities of addressing energy insecurity within the 
contested geography of the informal settlements in Africa remains both gendered and 
persistently understudied and underdeveloped (Musango et al., 2020; Anditi et al., 2022). In 
addition, the following is an argument that decreasing energy insecurity can decrease the 
poverty rate through economic development, sustainability, and human involvement in the 
informal settlement (Kohler, Rhodes & Vermaak, 2014). First of all, concerning the 
enhancement of modern energy services with an emphasis on cooking, the decisions of the 
designs and promotion of environmentally friendly energy technology are off policy making 
and should be linked to the energy users or end-users in order to increase the systems and 
mechanisms regarding energy access and the consumption/uses of energy resources. For a fact, 
and as much as the South African government has attempted to introduce energy pro-poor 
policies since 1998 into the households of the informal settlements, the nation still lack clean 
energy. Nakhooda (2012) states that fifty percent of the nation has no access to clean energy 
sources (SEA, 2014). 
In one capacity or the other, research studies have either individually or in groups considered 
policies on energy and have made great signs of progress and or proffer recommendations 
(Mohlakoana, 2014; Kelly and Geyer, 2018; Okoye, 2020). Other studies have also provided 
an overview of energy services achievements concerning gender mainstreaming and creating 
living labs towards energy security (Musango et al., 2020; Mukama et al., 2022). However, to 
our knowledge, no research study in South Africa has been identified to have promoted energy 
security in the Living Lab by developing energy technology innovation to a pilot scale to 
improve energy access and clean cooking energy services with a gender design using the 
systems approach. Therefore, this research study would study policies, conceptualise the 
Living Lab, gender inequality, energy technology, energy security, how human factors affect

1.3 The background and problem statement to the context of the 
research study 
The energy sector has produced substantial attention in both research and industries due to the 
observed striking technological advancements (Mastelic, 2014), like renewable energy 
technology, solar energy technology and clean cooking technology. However, the promising 
technological innovations seen are not guaranteed to be accepted by customers and prospective 
end users (Mastelic, 2014). This might not be because of the willingness of the energy sector 
to implement these innovations or if the energy innovations truly exist as applicable to 
technology, but because some of these glaring innovations lack the direct involvement of end-
users in the development processes of the energy products and services (Pełka, 2013), and 
because they are mostly weakened by potential user’s fear for change, inability to comprehend 
the technologies, and by legislative or political hindrances (Mastelic, 2014). This means that 
there should be a collaboration between the stakeholders and end-users in co-researching, co-
designing, co-creating, prototyping, and testing energy innovation technology. This will help 
to meet the expectations and needs of the end-users (Pełka, 2013). 
Gender and energy have grown to be one of the analytical pathways for connecting the 
intervention of energy (Mazzeo, Lazer and Zieky, 2006), to the Sustainable Development 
Goals, and an important achievement of the SDGs by 2030 is the reduction of energy poverty 
as presented by the United Nations (2014). Regardless of many attempts, gender inequality still 
exists at all stages of energy, power, management, and all economic sectors, and energy poverty 
is still prevalent in society. There is no doubt that energy insecurity is a huge challenge in the 
households of the informal settlements leading to about 50% of the dwellers in South Africa 
(Anditi et al., 2022). The issue of gender affects every part of society, especially in households. 
Therefore, when it comes to energy management, distribution of energy resources, and 
deciding who uses energy products, there is quite a gender imbalance, especially at the 
household level in informal settlements, although the industries and workplaces could be 
different (Köhlin et al., 2011). 
As stated above, the issue of energy insecurity in the informal settlements in South Africa is 
gendered (Musango et al., 2020). Literature has investigated and primarily articulated the links

between gender and energy (Oparaocha and Dutta, 2011). Women perform a more significant 
number of labour-rigorous tasks in households. Therefore, the advancements in the energy 
market or innovations around this sector need to benefit women uniquely. The recommendation 
is to support the gender equality by including women in the processes of decision making of 
the design of innovation of energy (Sohna, 2018). This is the same way as prevention of 
comprehensive market opportunities for financial services due to gender discrimination 
(George et al., 2016). 
Energy technology needs sustainable development goal 5, gender equality, poverty, and 
inequality as identified in the United Nations’ Agenda 2030, (United Nations, 2016). 
According to Elena Bou, (2019), gender equality is individually relevant to the energy industry 
for three key reasons: 
1. Gender diversity enables employees to deliver more enhanced performances than any 
other gender secured teams. 
2. The energy industry has remained as an innovative industry that inspires creativity; 
hence, for organizational creativity, the energy industry requires diversity. 
3. In line with a human approach, energy designs and solutions can therefore be viewed 
from the user perspective. This is due to the fact that in energy efficiency sector and 
some other sectors such as smart building, the African businesses and domestic 
markets in rural and urban settings (Temu, 1999; Mwaura et al, 2012), women are a 
meager market that needs to be understood qualitatively as the end-users and 
consumers. 
More women live in the poor informal settlements that constitute more than half of the 
population in an informal settlement, implying that gender defines the poor informal 
settlements (Kovacic et al., 2019). Therefore, gender equality matters in policy formulation 
and design of energy technology or the energy sector at all levels. But it is mandatory to find 
out the issues and make women involved in the initial stages to discuss and search for the 
options that might be defined as possible. 
Anditi et al. (2022) have indicated that women are the most vulnerable and also hugely affected 
by the issue of energy insecurity, and although plenty of women (poor or wealthy) suffer greatly 
in poor informal settlements. (Barsoum, 2006; European Institute of Gender Equality, 2020), 
indicates that there is a need to investigate and understand the importance of gendered energy 
in the informal settlements in the process of developing energy innovations. Thus, gender 
equality issues are essential at all levels of energy sectors, management, distribution of energy

resources, policymaking, and designs in energy technology. Also, the argument for greater

Living Lab, throughout the last years, has been even more developed and defined; its 
subsequent concern is based on the creative engagement of the end-users as co-designers or 
co-producers of technologies and innovations (as Higgins, 2011 highlighted). The Living Lab 
can therefore be defined as the private/public domain where people; specifically the 
stakeholders and users bring the new innovations or technology introductions that are required 
to be designed in the Innovation Design stage and provide input in the process (Evans, et al, 
2017). For example, the phones are either touch screen phones or button screen phones; the 
manufactures of the mobile phones set a forum where the end users can participate to establish 
which between the touch screen and the button is more favorable. This is important in that the 
end users are involved in manufacturing decisions thus enabling the manufacturers save costs 
and results into social acceptance of the mobile phone. This process is called a “User-Centred 
Design (UCD)”. 
The user-centred design (UCD) is also a factor within this research study as the design is also 
a process considered within this study to obtain the intended objectives. The Living Lab at the 
UCD shows us why the focus of this study is on the end-users, in this case women only to 
accomplish the goals of this type of research. Therefore, according to the report of Interaction 
Design Foundation (2022:1), the UCD is “an iterative design process in which designers focus 
on the users and their needs in each phase of the design processes”. It is also stated that user-
centred research was used to “support new product/system design and development to promote 
more rapid adoption and carbon-saving use of energy efficient and renewable technologies in 
homes” (Caird and Roy, 2008:1). The diagram below, shows the process of user-centric design:

Additionally, the majority of analyses on energy poverty and the Living Labs focus on rural

the testing stage of the products (that is let the innovations be brought by more women). 
In light of this, the study suggests that women should always be included in the design process 
if we want to address the problems of energy insecurity, market transitions and investment in 
the energy sector in Africa, poverty and inequality, rural and urban livelihood, and the 
development of sustainable energy technology to meet the energy products. Therefore, the 
problem statement is: ‘the big challenge and hindrance towards stipulating the needs and

1.4 Research aims and objectives 
In response, the South African government has devoted its attention to addressing Energy 
poverty endeavours in poor informal settlements or households. Unfortunately, much of this 
gendered energy is missing in the poverty research analysis and poverty assessments by most 
researchers (Mazzeo, Lazer and Zieky, 2006). They have culminated into the argument that 
energy poverty within the Africa continent is gendered. The issue of using gender as an 
insignificant factor in the energy innovation processes of the informal settlement is that it 
hampers the recognition of energy innovations (Anditi et al., 2022). The informal settlements 
in Africa are gendered (Kovacic et al., 2019) and thus require gender equality as provided in 
SDG5 by the year 2030 and to encourage gender mainstreaming to realize energy security in 
the context of informal settlements. 
Hence, this study seeks to ‘advance theoretical and empirical knowledge on the processes of 
developing energy technology innovations within a systems thinking Living Lab that at least 
addresses positioning of gender or women at the core of designing, creating, researching, 
prototyping, and testing of energy innovations to ensure the end-users’ utility requirements can 
be met in ways that would eventually help address energy poverty. To attain the research aim, 
the following research objectives will be addressed: 
1. To conceptualise and understand the Living Lab concepts, gender inequality, energy 
security, energy technology innovations and informal settlements. Determining the 
present functions and impact of women in energy technology innovation. Evaluating 
the consequences of gender inequality on results of energy innovation. Suggesting 
strategies and frameworks for mainstreaming gender disparities in energy technology 
innovation. 
2. To understand the cultural, norms, practices or technological mechanisms influencing 
the level of adoption or acceptance of energy innovations, gender design or end-users 
within energy innovation processes in the informal settlements.

3. To investigate the influence of end-user’s contributions on energy technology 
innovation in a systems Living Lab context and how human factors affect 
technologies. 
4. To develop a Systems Living Lab that focuses on gender designs for the development 
of energy technology innovation up to a pilot scale through the involvement of end-
users (women) and other stakeholders in real-life scenarios to achieve the security of 
energy services. In the typical case study, the new SETILL model incorporates the 
crucial elements of objectives 1-3 to advance energy security. 
 
1.5 Research questions 
To address the research objectives, the following research questions related to each of the 
research objectives were answered: 
1. What is the conceptualization and understanding of the Systems Living Lab concept, 
gender inequality, energy security, energy technology innovations, material cultures, 
cognitive norms and energy practices of the informal settlements and how do human 
factors affect technologies in literature? a) What positions do women hold in energy 
technology innovation in Sub-Saharan Africa today? b) To what extent do women's 
and men's life experiences of energy inequality affect outcomes for energy 
innovation, such as access, sustainability, and technological progress? c) Which 
strategies and frameworks can be designed or put into action to address the gender 
imbalance and promote fair participation in energy technology innovations? 
2. Which are the possible factors determining the involvement of knowledge and 
participation of women in the decision-making process of co-designing, co-creating, 
co-researching, prototyping and testing the energy technology innovations in the 
informal settlement to satisfy the intended recipients? 
3. How can the mechanism(s) be improved to enhance the level of adoption or 
acceptance of energy innovations and end-user (women) contributions and 
participation to address the security of energy services in poor informal settlements? 
4. Is it possible that the proposed Systems Living Lab specifically on gender design can 
act as a theoretical model or frame work for energy technology innovation, to enhance 
the take up of gender, fight energy poverty and enhance access to energy services in 
the poor informal settlements and the energy sectors?

This reinforces the previous remarks on gender structure of energy production and distribution 
and gender definition of the customer of energy products. Ordinarily, firms develop objects

Women and men are not society and or energy sector equal and women are in different ‘spaces’ 
from those designed by men (Gillard and Okonjo-Iweala, 2020) hinder equal distribution of 
energy services or secure energy services in Africa. That is because the structure which has 
already been framed and constructed as male; the structure cannot mold itself in that framework

of electricity is considered. (United Nations, 2014). Also, it is recorded that 2.6 million people 
carry out household cooking relying on biomass which has several effects like economic and 
social problems in the informal settlement (United Nations, 2014).  
The South African government has paid more attention in addressing energy poverty 
challenges in the poor informal settlements, or parts of the community. However, the improved 
own-constructed indicator of the government shows limited progress in the provision of 
electricity and gender inequality in the poor informal settlements (Musango et al., 2020). By 
the same token, most researchers and poverty assessments overlook or even dismiss gendered 
energy (Mazzeo et al. 2006). In other words, failure to consider gender in the analysis of energy 
innovation processes in the informal settlement means that the issue of when to accept energy 
innovations will never be addressed (Anditi et al., 2022). This is because the energy innovation 
processes have been seen, to consist of the generation of knowledge, then conversion of the 
knowledge into the energy innovation products and the consumption of the energy innovation 
products (Fri and Savitz, 2014).  
Nonetheless, there is a low uptake of adoption of energy innovations in the informal settlements 
(Soman 2014; Nasra et al. 2021), because the end-users within the informal settlements do not 
participate in the design processes, because the designers pay little attention to the users and 
their requirements throughout the design processes. However, given that the energy policies 
and energy decision-making are not bottom up policies and decision-making, but rather top up 
policies and decision-making Ambole et al., 2021), this means that there will be a low rate of 
uptake or acceptance of the innovation products by the targeted end-users.
