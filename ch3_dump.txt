Chapter 1 
Conceptual Framework of the SETILL model 
1.1 Introduction 
This chapter discusses the transitional theory of the processes of the phenomena being studied 
and why. It is the development of the procedures for the phenomena being investigated. The 
conceptual framework for this study is not essentially a literature review because it can be 
created in the study by the insights on the phenomena and uses several pieces of information 
to illustrate the phenomenon being studied (Maxwell, 1996). The conceptual framework was 
linked to Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 of this thesis where the background, overview, and review 
of the literature of the study and topic were analysed and discussed. This implies that this 
chapter will partially not be repeating the background, overview and review of works of 
literature on the topic, but will discuss further the topic while leading to the development of 
the SETILL theoretical model with gender design to support the energy innovation to be 
produced and tested to a pilot scale at the selected case study. 
Therefore, this chapter represents a conceptual framework for a Systemic Living Lab intending 
to promote energy technology innovation from the perspective of gender in South African 
informal settlements. The conceptual framework for this study is a synthesis of the theory of a 
Living Lab concept, which highlights the dynamic participation of stakeholders, SsT/SE and 
co-creating solutions, intending to empower women and tackle gender inequalities in the 
context of energy security and energy innovation. These theories including the characteristics 
of the informal settlements, the social, and cultural norms and economic mechanisms, and the 
SRM for EAS, EC and SEA frameworks contributing to gender equality within the South 
African informal settlements with access to energy services, women's participation and 
decision making were discussed and reviewed in chapter 1 and chapter 2 of this thesis. 
Therefore, by linking Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 to Chapter 3 of this thesis, the conceptual 
framework is developed, leading to the SETILL model. 
Furthermore, to share ideas in a connected manner and generate a substantial ‘space’ for co-
designing and co-innovation to achieve a particular goal, the end users (women in this context) 
and other stakeholders in the public and private sectors should cooperatively work together 
(Mukama et al., 2022). The space referred to in this context is called the Living Lab. This 
chapter also explores the approach of the Living Lab as reviewed in the Literature chapter for 
energy technology innovation in an informal settlement with a gendered perspective. The 
Living Lab, if properly governed, can be seen as a vital innovation environment which focuses

on problem-solving and the development of technological innovations with the consideration 
of compound methods like social, environmental, usability, technical, etc (Mukama et al., 
2022). 
Therefore, the importance of involving end-users in co-designing innovations has been often 
spoken about in literature (Ballon, Pierson and Delaere, 2005; Benoît Dutilleul, 2010; Almirall, 
Lee and Wareham, 2012; Dell’Era and Landoni, 2014). This collaboration allows for the end-
users in the energy households/sectors, health sectors, etc, to participate in the designing and 
testing of technology innovations which leads to an adoption or acceptance of the innovation. 
This participation with the end-users can either be done as end-users becoming the head 
developers (Von Hippel, 2001), like the women in the communities deciding on the innovation 
to be developed or the users participating together as partners with the stakeholders to co-create 
innovations (Brown, 2008). Thus, the Living Lab can then be seen as the equal participation of 
the end-users and stakeholders to co-create and test innovations in real-world scenarios 
(Almirall, Lee and Wareham, 2012). In recent years, the Living Labs has been used in different 
fields even in the social science fields for technological improvements (Ahmadi et al., 2020), 
which can be seen as the ‘social innovation environment’ (Edwards‐Schachter, 2012). 
Therefore, this research study agrees with the statement above that Living Lab should also be 
seen as a ‘social innovation environment.’  
Furthermore, the Living Lab can be of great importance and has some aspects of distinctiveness 
to possible co-creation, making use of real-life scenarios and make use of end-users (Dell’Era 
and Landoni, 2014), to achieve technological advancement. The main peculiar area of the 
Living Lab is the involvement of end-users from the design phases down to the testing phases 
of the innovations, which indicates that the main focus of the technological innovations is the 
end-users (Almirall, Lee and Wareham, 2012). This allows for the conceptualisation of 
knowledge and opinions of the end-users (women) in the case to be studied. The Living Lab 
can also be used as a mediator between different stakeholders like the researchers, 
communities, governments, etc, to evaluate technological innovations (Almirall, Lee and 
Wareham, 2012; Dupont et al., 2017), for proper adoption of the innovations, since the Living 
Lab environment can only be conducted by the stakeholders to give value to the technological 
innovation. The Living Lab can also serve as an example to the private and public sectors 
towards the importance of participatory technological innovations and co-designing 
innovations to bring solutions (Burbridge, 2017; Dupont et al., 2017), to the energy needs of 
the informal settlements. The Living Lab as an innovation environment allows for the

generation of new and more detailed ideas by the end-users on the particular technology 
innovation to be developed, allows for the adoption of the innovation due to the testing of the 
innovation and allows for the impact of the technology innovation to be easily experienced by 
all stakeholders involved (Dell’Era and Landoni, 2014).  
Therefore, according to this research study, the development of the systems Living Lab is to 
support energy technology innovation with a gendered perspective, and also give more 
understanding to the Living Lab technology. A study has advanced the understanding and best 
application of the Living Labs to support gendered energy technology innovation (Mukama et 
al., 2022), however, this study did not make use of the SsT/SE approaches in the Living Lab 
to develop the technology innovation and also did not go further to develop or test any 
particular innovation in an informal settlement in South Africa. Therefore, this research study 
will contribute to the understanding and application of the Living Lab technology to support 
energy technology innovation with a gender design and also go further to develop and test an 
energy innovation to a pilot scale (Biogas technology) using the SsT and SE approaches, hence 
the model to be developed in this research study is called the systems energy technology 
innovation Living Lab (SETILL) with gender design. The SETILL will most importantly 
contribute to ‘gendered-energy informal settlement nexus research’ with the Living Lab in 
South Africa. 
Therefore, there is a need for more contributions and understanding of the Living Lab to 
support gendered energy technology innovation in South Africa. This is because, at the 
moment, the application of the Living Lab in the energy environment and Africa is very lean 
(Mukama et al., 2022), especially in South Africa. Furthermore, this chapter attempts or gives 
a leading role to answer research question 4 and validates research question 1 of this research 
study which made use of the literature review approach in chapter 2 and linked it to this chapter 
3. The literature search was done and analysed from google scholar, library genesis, university 
databases and websites of existing Living Labs focused on the context of the study and the 
systems thinking approaches to further lead to the SETILL and the research questions of this 
study. The search words used were: ‘systems thinking’, ‘systems Living Lab’, ‘Living Labs’, 
‘gendered Living Labs’, ‘energy stakeholders and end-users, ‘energy Living Labs’, ‘poor 
informal settlements’, ‘energy security’, ‘women empowerment’, etc.

1.3 The Living Lab concepts as applied to the SETILL model 
The energy efficiency market is controlled by an innovative techno-centric model, which 
indicates that users are the logical decision-makers who will effectively accept and use the 
energy technologies once they are informed of the gain the system has to the economic and 
environmental aspects (Caird and Roy, 2008). The Living Lab concepts have been discussed 
in Chapter 2 of this research study and will be briefly discussed in this chapter, leading to the 
SETILL model.  
Therefore, it has been noticed in this research study that the definitions of the Living Labs as a 
concept are peculiarly dependent on the field it is used for. Generally, the Living Lab involves 
studying and developing innovations in a real-world environment like the communities 
(Mukama et al., 2022). This allows for the visibility and installation of the technology 
innovations in the communities, thereby impacting them (Ballon, Pierson and Delaere, 2005; 
Hossain, Leminen and Westerlund, 2019) and leading to the adoption and acceptance of the 
innovations. Furthermore, due to the participation of end-users in co-creating technology 
innovations in the Living Labs and the real-world environment, Dell’Era and Landoni (2014) 
argued that the definitions of the Living Lab research lacked the inclusion of producing novel 
innovations in the Living Lab, which is why Living Lab was defined as “a design research 
methodology aimed at co-creating innovation through the involvement of aware users in a real-
life setting” Dell’Era and Landoni (2014:139). 
Furthermore, during the study carried out by Hossain, Leminen and Westerlund (2019:2), the 
Living Lab was defined as “a physical or virtual space in which to solve societal challenges, 
especially for urban areas by bringing together various stakeholders for collaboration and 
collective ideation”. These definitions identified above are all in agreement with this research 
study, however, this study disagrees with the Living Lab being specifically for the urban 
environment, but the Living Lab should be developed anywhere (rural and urban) for the 
benefit of the communities or stakeholders involved (Almirall, Lee and Wareham, 2012). The 
Living Lab as a resilient methodology is fully focused on the ideas or insights obtained from 
the end-users (Almirall, Lee and Wareham, 2012), to achieve a specific objective (Ahmadi et 
al., 2020), which allows for various designs in the Living Lab suited for the technology 
innovations to be developed (Mukama et al., 2022). Therefore, in the case of the women in the 
informal settlement who are not empowered, are marginalised and do not have the 
opportunities to co-design the innovations, the Living Lab will allow them to contribute and 

########## PDF PAGE 129 ##########
Page | 123 
 
co-design the energy innovations in the communities. This is referred to as the Living Lab as 
a ‘safe space’ (Ahmadi et al., 2020). 
The Living Lab as a methodology is classified into the following according to Almirall, Lee 
and Wareham (2012): User-Centric Design (UCD), design-driven, and participatory-driven. 
The UCD has been introduced in Chapter 1 of this study and this is the process where the main 
passive collaborators are the end-users. The UCD is introduced in this research study as the 
research study intends to develop a new technology innovation to be tested and to analyse how 
technologies are affected by human factors (Mukama et al., 2022). This is because, in a 
research area, the Living Lab was defined by (Schaer, Schaible and Müller, 2020) as “a user-
centric study methodology for researchers to evaluate the performance of retrieval systems 
within real-world applications”. This implies that the end-users are to be involved in the 
research as co-researchers to achieve a common goal. Therefore, the definitions above and the 
Living Lab concepts as applied to SETILL can be suitable to the phenomena being studied 
because it involves the ‘end-users’ and the ‘real-world testing environment’. These major 
theories will be applied to the SETILL with gender designs. 
Therefore, after looking at the concepts of different Living Lab in Chapter 2 and Chapter 3 
respectively, the critical elements of the Living Labs that support innovation are: “Living Lab 
methodology”, “ICT and infrastructure”, “Living Lab management”, “partners and users”, and 
also “research” (Jere et al., 2014). The interactions necessary for innovation are depicted by

1.4 Structuring and adapting the Living Lab leading to SETILL model 
The Living Labs and the gender studies have been discussed in Chapter 2 of this study. The 
Living Labs are seen as methodologies, real-world scenarios and landscapes where various 
approaches, techniques and concepts are used by several stakeholders for a common goal 
(Hossain, Leminen and Westerlund, 2019). However, the successful development of Living 
Labs and their results are mostly affected by several challenges like the maintenance of the 
innovation, and the engagement of the end-users and stakeholders. There are several Living 
Labs in the world with a lens of systems thinking in them. Such as the Sustainable Urban 
Neighbourhood (SUN) research projects which took place in Europe, the Living Lab for Smart 
Mobility situated in Eindhoven Netherlands, the Systems Living Lab situated in Oulu Finland 
and at the University of Trento in Italy.  However, at the moment it is not confirmed if these 
Living Labs still exist, but the point is that they used the systems thinking lens to develop and 
test products in collaboration with different stakeholders, but, with no gender lens. 
Furthermore, in South Africa, although there are several Innovation hubs in the form of the 
Living Lab focused on energy technology innovation, most of them lacked the focus on gender 
and systems thinking perspectives. Generally, these innovation hubs may not focus on gender 
design in energy technology innovation in South Africa, but they work to improve gender 
equality in other sectors. There are also research studies that have used the soft systems 
methodology lens towards a Living Lab methodology for smart cities, innovation management, 
concept designs, transitioning smart campus to cities, to develop a community Living Lab, 
framework, forming future IT to show the Living Lab processes for the involvement of users, 
for user-centric co-creation, and for the evaluation of Living Labs to innovation (Mirijamdotter 
et al., 2006; Kong and Li, 2007; Ståhlbröst, 2008; Bergvall-Kåreborn, Holst and Ståhlbröst, 
2009; van der Walt et al., 2009; Cosgrave, Arbuthnot and Tryfonas, 2013; van Geenhuizen, 

########## PDF PAGE 131 ##########
Page | 125 
 
2018; Omotayo et al., 2021). However, these studies did not involve the gender perspective. 
The closest study was the study by Mukama et al (2022), which involved the gendered 
perspective on energy technology innovation. However, this study did not include systems 
thinking approaches. Therefore, this research study will involve the systems thinking approach 
and gendered design of the Living Lab to be developed towards the development of the energy 
technology innovation in the case study. 
The selected case study of the SETILL will show how the Living Lab adapted to energy 
technology innovation using a gender design. The SETILL will carry out transdisciplinary 
energy technology research which will involve various stakeholders; however, the major focus 
will be on the needs of the women in the community which will also impact the sustainable 
development of the community. The SETILL model will also be focused on reducing energy 
poverty in the households of the informal settlement. The SETILL will also help the researchers 
and the government learn more about the processes and how to further intervene to achieve the 
security of energy services. Furthermore, the main focus of the SETILL model is the 
involvement of women as end users and the informal settlement to drive their opinions towards 
the development of the energy technology innovation to be developed and tested, which could 
be later used by the government to improve energy services. 
Therefore, considering the Living Lab concepts, the conceptualization and understanding of 
the Living Labs, the Living Lab can be defined as “a research and innovation concept for 
experimental and experiential learning in real-life environment, involving users and multiple 
private and public stakeholders, and aimed at (1) co-designing, prototyping, testing and 
observing new solutions and novel organisational structure in an iterative fashion, and (2) 
stimulating changes in the socio-technical regime to create the most favourable conditions for 
scaling-up innovations (Mukama et al., 2022:4). Therefore, considering the definition of the 
Living Lab above and the scope of this research study, the SETILL will now be defined in this 
research study as the ‘physical or virtual and mobile research and systems thinking approach 
space for design methodology focused on co-creating and testing of energy innovations through 
the involvement of end-users in a real-life scenario towards mitigating energy poverty for the 
informal settlements by collaborating with various stakeholders to (1) understand the material 
cultural, cognitive norms and energy practices or technological mechanisms influencing the 
level of adoption of energy innovations, (2) to investigate the influence of end-users 
contributions on energy technology innovation in a systems Living Lab, (3) develop and test

1.5 Stakeholders and their roles as applied to the SETILL model 
Stakeholders in the Living Lab are very important to achieving the aim of this research study. 
The importance of the stakeholders cannot be underestimated. This is because, the 
collaboration of the stakeholders in the Living Lab will go a long way to improve the 
acceptance and adoption of innovations and increase knowledge and transparency to the 
development of the technology innovations (Breukers and Wolsink, 2007; Reed, 2008; 
Johnson, Jansujwicz and Zydlewski, 2015; Mastelic et al., 2018; Gabriel et al., 2020). 
Therefore, the stakeholders in this context can be defined as the public group of people and 
institutions that are involved in a process of development or otherwise (Reed, 2008). To build 
trust for the success of innovations and promote acceptance, it is necessary to work with various 
stakeholders. Also, people should not be left behind when making decisions on the design 
phases and processes of innovations to avoid a lack of adoption and acceptance of the 
innovation (Dachtera, Randall and Wulf, 2014), thus the involvement of various stakeholders 
from the design phases to the testing phases is very important for a successful technology 
innovation development (Mastelic et al., 2018).  
Moreover, the concept of the Living Lab technology is enhanced by the participations of many 
stakeholders and especially from the different field of discipline. These may include the people 
in the government sectors, academic sectors, communities and in the private and public sectors 
of other fields with same vision towards technology success for the Living Lab as mentioned 
in Ahmadi et al., 2020. A study on gender equality with energy studies indicated the importance 
of the collaboration of various stakeholders to be a gain or a driver in the success of gender 
equality (Oosthuizen, de Kock and Musango, 2020). The huge challenge of collaborating with 
stakeholders in this context is usually maintaining different perspectives of the stakeholders in 
a practical study (Logghe, Baccarne and Schuurman, 2014; Ley et al., 2015) because 
individuals with their interests might clash with the research study because of different goals 
relating to their interests (Dachtera, Randall and Wulf, 2014). Therefore, to overcome these 
challenges, the identification of trustworthy groups or individuals, who are ready to work and 

########## PDF PAGE 135 ##########
Page | 129 
 
share the environment is necessary (Dupont et al., 2019; Ahmadi et al., 2020) and the 
management of time, motivating the individuals and accommodating their interest is necessary 
to avoid them from disconnecting (Mukama et al., 2022). Brouwer et al. (2013) advised that 
the stakeholders should lead together or have equal power to avoid one of the stakeholders’ 
overpowering others in the environment. However, there should be a facilitator who should 
coordinate the environment with the teams of stakeholders for a balance in the process which 
allows for more focus (Mukama et al., 2022). 
Furthermore, new stakeholders can be brought in during the process of the Living Lab to allow 
for more opinions and greater experience (Ahmadi et al., 2020). This would help to constantly 
add or subtract, and bring versatility to the roles of various stakeholders in the Living Lab 
(Ståhlbröst and Holst, 2017). Therefore, this research study collaborated with several 
stakeholders towards a successful development of the energy technology innovation where 
women (end-users) were allowed to contribute to how the energy innovation should be 
developed to benefit them and the community. The Living Lab collaboration has been ongoing 
with various stakeholders from the start of this research study, and this allows for the addition 
of experience methodological bonding between the various stakeholders involved (Ahmadi et 
al., 2020). This continued until the end of this research project for the successful adoption of 
the innovation.  
The management of the Living Lab technology is easy when the stakeholders are categorised 
accordingly, to enable successful stakeholders’ opinions into the processes (Nyström et al., 
2014; Johnson, Jansujwicz and Zydlewski, 2015; Ståhlbröst, Bergvall-Kåreborn and Eriksson, 
2015). The connection between the stakeholders and the Living Labs is usually focused more 
on the stakeholders’ control and not on the Living Lab space (Ståhlbröst, Bergvall-Kåreborn 
and Eriksson, 2015). This is because the stakeholders drive the Living Lab and also dominate 
the Living Lab. This also means that the successful continuation or longevity of the Living Lab 
depends on the various stakeholders who agree to work together to achieve a common goal 
(Mukama et al., 2022). Therefore, the SETILL will comprise the collaborations of various 
stakeholders who are willing to achieve the desired objectives of this research study. These

1.5.1 
The informal settlement/community stakeholders 
The research study collaborated with Mater Pluviam Holdings, a science technology company 
to partner with the Tzaneen, Limpopo of South Africa, as the selected case study to test the 
gendered energy technology innovation and the Umuahia, Abia State of Nigeria informal 
settlements. Mater Pluviam Holdings PTY LTD is trusted around the community and beyond 
and allows for easy access to the community due to the scientific innovations it has brought to 
the community in the past. The Mather Pluviam Holding made it possible for the community 
supporters/civil society who could serve as co-researchers to be identified and educated on the 
process involved in carrying out their duties. Furthermore, a non-random sample of the 
informal settlement women in households in the community was identified. These women in 
the household were a representation of the variety of women in the households in the informal 
settlements. They also helped to identify the schools in the community where the innovation to 
be developed will be tested for clean cooking to feed the school children in the case study. A 
semi-structured interview and surveys were conducted by the researchers and co-researchers 
in the informal settlements to identify the material cultures, cognitive norms and energy 
practices in the community in ten areas of the communities in August and September of 2022 
which ethnographic research was also done in 30% of the households in July 2023 to discuss 
the challenges of the energy systems.  These were done in collaboration with other stakeholders 
in the SETILL.  
This survey was done to have an insight into how the households in the community are 
influenced by modern energy needs, level of energy demand, availability of energy demand 
and general energy uses and culture, using the available energy sources in the informal 
settlements. Therefore, this research study will then require the ideas and opinions of the 
informal settlements and end-users (women) and other stakeholders to carry out the design 
processes of the energy technology innovation to be developed towards a reduction in energy 
poverty and bringing about the security of energy services in the informal settlements. This 
process is referred to as open social innovation (Yun et al., 2017). These informal settlements

1.5.2 
The government/policy maker stakeholders 
The government is one of the stakeholders in the Living Lab and its connection with the 
community is very important because of its authority and power to decide the continuation of 
the energy innovation, if not carried along properly (Johnson, Jansujwicz and Zydlewski, 
2015). The government will assist to educate others in the Living Lab, about the extent to the 
sustainability of the innovation, other laws and corrections to the policies where necessary. The 
duties of the government in the Living Lab might be to bring up laws that will assist in the 
advancement and adoption of energy innovation and security to the energy services from the 
Living Lab (Almirall, Lee and Wareham, 2012).  
The government (central or local governments) will also assist with the formulation and 
enactment of policies and legislations for the energy industry, advance the sustainable and 
effective energy use, supervise closely the energy project for the benefit of the community, 
demand more participations in the design and management of the energy technology (Ceriano, 
Lalk and Thopil, 2023), and create further expansion of the energy technology innovation to 
other informal settlements. Lastly, the social system guiding the innovation laws to the social 
challenges can be strongly supported by the government to make decisions on the challenges 
faced by the communities. They can also influence the funding of the activities in the Living 
Lab, the Living Lab itself and the maintenance of energy technology.

1.5.4 
The researcher/academia stakeholders 
The researchers are the facilitators of the activities in the Living Lab. They will manage and 
direct the activities, invent new knowledge to assist the other stakeholders and apply for 
funding for the progress of the activities to be carried out in the Living Lab, and they will also 
avoid research bias, and be true to the other stakeholders to build trust (Mukama et al., 2022). 
The researchers should also endeavour to keep to the time frame to complete the research 
objectives. The researchers are to be resilient (Ahmadi et al., 2020), and contemplative 
(Ståhlbröst and Holst, 2017) while carrying out their duties in the Living Lab. According to 
Ceriano, Lalk and Thopil (2023:7), the technical researchers “develop energy solutions that 
address country’s specific energy needs and sustainably exploit the usage of available 
resources; keep abreast of research developments regionally and internationally; adopt better 
practices customised for the local scenarios while utilising local resources; training and skilling 
of energy workforce; act as information sources and decision makers’ consultants; are 
compelled to observe national and local rules and norms”. 
The researchers or academia’s in collaboration with the developers are to also provide a 
suitable venue and required tools needed for the activities of the stakeholders and research 
(Mukama et al., 2022). The main role of the researcher is to use empirical knowledge to give 
insights into the technicalities and sciences behind the development of energy technology 
innovation. They also do not possess the dynamic ability to force the design of innovations on 
the communities (Mukama et al., 2022).

1.5.6 
The end-user stakeholders 
As discussed initially, for this research study, women are the target end-users. The energy 
technology to be developed is mostly used by women in the Tzaneen and Umuahia informal 
settlements and other likely informal settlements. Although almost everyone (including men) 
with access to the energy system are users. This research study has a focus on women because 
they happen to be affected more by the disadvantages of restricted access to the modern form 
of energy and from contributing to the designs of the energy technology innovations (Cecelski, 
2012). The focus on the vulnerability of women in poor informal settlements have been used 
by Kovacic et al., (2019); Musango et al., (2020); Mukama et al., (2022). These studies, which 
are related to health, energy, Living Labs and gender respectively, also result in the view that 
there is a link between women vulnerable to poverty and the “symmetric” dissemination of 
household responsibilities. 
The women can also be in the same category as the community stakeholders, because they 
share similar responsibilities as the community stakeholders and are all beneficiaries of the 
energy technology innovation. However, women are categorised as separate stakeholders in 
the context of the SETILL because of their importance in achieving the research objectives. A 
non-random probability sample of women representing various women was used to identify

1.6 Conceptualising the SETILL theoretical model 
The SETI Living Lab model as conceptualised will be implemented as part of any hub of 
energy technology innovation with gender design and systems thinking approach in poor 
informal settlements. The SETILL model could be a virtual place if there is no space due to 
social distancing. This means that it could be done virtually by bringing together those involved 
online, and it can also be mobile, which means that it can be moved to any convenient space 
regardless of where the energy innovation is to be developed. Also, the SETILL model is not 
complete if there is no energy technology innovation developed to benefit a particular end-
users chosen by the researcher in collaboration with other stakeholders. 
Therefore, considering the project of the proposed SETILL context, the Living Lab is defined 
as physical or virtual and mobile research and systems thinking approach space for design 
methodology focused on co-creating and testing of energy innovations through the involvement 
of end-users in a real-life scenario towards mitigating energy poverty for the informal 
settlements by collaborating with various stakeholders to (1) understand the material cultures, 
cognitive norms and energy practices or mechanisms influencing the level of adoption of 
energy innovations, (2) to investigate the influence of end-users contributions on energy 
technology innovation in a systems Living Lab, (3) develop and test an energy technology 

########## PDF PAGE 143 ##########
Page | 137 
 
innovation up to a pilot scale through the involvement of end-users, (4) promote energy 
security, gender equality and women empowerment. 
It is envisaged that the SETILL theoretical model will look like the Living Lab that focuses on 
the involvement of users, with a focus on women in the decision-making processes and 
knowledge contribution of the innovative design. The users would be involved in the co-
creation of the energy services solutions, co-design of energy technology innovations, 
prototyping, and testing. Although men are also part of the users, for the context of this research 
study, the focus is on women. A different study that wants to use the SETILL model can decide 
to choose a different user if possible. 
The processes of developing the SETILL model from a conceptualisation point of view can be 
illustrated by borrowing from the ICT, health, gender and education sectors. Although these 
have been developed in the ICT, health, gender and education sectors, they will become the 
basis for testing the energy technology innovation to be developed. This has given the idea of 
how the proposed SETILL model will be conceptualised. See Figure 3.5 below, figure 3.3 and

1.7 Conclusions 
This chapter has contributed to the theoretical framework of the Living Lab concept in the 
energy industry and shows how the Living Lab concept is used leading to the context of the 
SETILL to achieve security of energy services. The SETILL can be mobile and the movement 
can always start from the GSTM of the University of Pretoria to collaborate with various 
stakeholders to ‘co-research’, ‘co-design’, ‘co-create’, ‘prototype’ and ‘test’ any form of 
energy technology innovation to a pilot scale and most importantly with gender perspectives 
and systems thinking approaches. Any kind of systems thinking or systems engineering can be 
used for the SETILL. 
The chapter has specifically or partially addressed objective 1 and 4 by developing a roadmap 
to the SETILL (figure 3.3). This chapter also conceptualised the Living Lab technology starting 
with building the conceptual framework, showing the antecedents, attributes and consequences 
relating to the research concept. Finally, other detailed information and literature reviews
