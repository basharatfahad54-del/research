[P25|Heading 1] 2. Abstract
[P26|Normal] South Africa's just energy transition is framed nationally by renewable procurement and grid decarbonisation, yet its outcomes are decided locally, in configurations where households, fuels, appliances, dwellings, tenure, infrastructure and institutions interact. In informal settlements these configurations often reproduce energy poverty, insecure supply and an unequal distribution of costs and benefits. Community-scale biogas is promoted as a decentralised alternative, but uptake is uneven, and existing acceptance models treat the technology as given and the user as an individual chooser, leaving the socio-material conditions of acceptance and justice unexamined.
[P27|Normal] This study develops SETILL, a Systemic Living Lab for Just Energy Transition, as a conceptual and methodological framework for examining how a community-scale biogas innovation can be designed, accepted and sustained justly in an informal settlement in Tzaneen, South Africa. The objectives are to frame the settlement as a socio-technical system, identify the factors that shape end-user participation, examine the cultural and technological conditions of acceptance, specify the mechanisms through which a Living Lab can support a just transition, and establish how such a Living Lab should be evaluated.
[P28|Normal] The study adopts a critical posthumanist paradigm, supported by systems thinking, energy justice, socio-technical transitions, Living Lab theory and complexity theory. It follows a qualitative, participatory case study design using a Living Lab approach, with co-design workshops, semi-structured interviews, observation and documentary analysis, analysed through reflexive thematic and framework analysis.
[P29|Normal] The expected contributions are a situated account of acceptance and justice in an informal settlement, a transferable Living Lab framework for community-scale energy innovation, practice guidance for participatory design, and methodological insight into reflexive, posthumanist energy research in engineering management.

[P33|Heading 1] 3. Introduction
[P34|Heading 2] 3.1 Background and context
[P35|Heading 3] 3.1.1 A grid that is changing, unevenly
[P36|Normal] South Africa generates most of its electricity from coal, and the consequences of that dependence are now felt in three places at once: in the account that households pay, in the reliability of the supply they receive, and in the carbon intensity of the economy. The national utility, Eskom, supplies the greater part of the country's power from a fleet of ageing stations. Scheduled load-shedding reached record levels in 2023. It has since been suspended for long periods, but the system remains vulnerable to breakdowns, and the price of electricity has risen faster than household incomes. For a middle-income household, these are inconveniences and costs to be managed. For a household in an informal settlement, they determine whether there is a hot meal, whether a child can study after dark, and whether the money set aside for electricity runs out before the month does.
[P37|Normal] The aggregate picture looks better than the household picture. Around nine in ten South African households now have a grid connection (Statistics South Africa, 2023), a substantial achievement compared with the early 1990s. Yet connection is not the same as access. A connected household may still be unable to afford enough electricity, may receive an intermittent supply, may live in a dwelling where the wiring cannot carry a cooking load, or may buy electricity through a landlord's meter at a price that includes an informal markup. Energy poverty in South Africa is therefore less a problem of missing infrastructure than a problem of how infrastructure, income, tariffs, dwelling conditions and household practice fit together. That distinction matters for this study because it shifts attention from the presence of a technology to the arrangement in which the technology works, or fails to work.
[P38|Normal] Policy has begun to reflect this. The Presidential Climate Commission's just transition framework accepts that decarbonisation and social equity cannot be separated (Presidential Climate Commission, 2022), and the Integrated Resource Plan sets out a shift away from coal alongside a commitment to maintain security of supply (Department of Mineral Resources and Energy, 2019). The Just Energy Transition Partnership has brought international finance and, with it, expectations of a transition that is procedurally fair as well as technically rapid. Recent research on the barriers to the transition in South Africa shows, however, that the obstacles are as much institutional and social as they are financial or technical (Molepo et al., 2025; Mirzania et al., 2023). Municipalities carry distribution responsibilities without the capacity or revenue to fulfil them. Communities that host generation or transmission infrastructure have rarely been consulted in a meaningful way. And the households that are least able to pay are the least able to wait for the benefits of a transition that is planned at national scale.
[P39|Heading 3] 3.1.2 Informal settlements as socio-material configurations
[P40|Normal] Informal settlements are not a single kind of place. They differ in age, size, location, tenure arrangements, access to services and internal organisation. Some are on well-located land close to work; others are peripheral. Some have been upgraded with water, sanitation and electricity; others rely on shared taps, communal toilets and informal connections. What they share is a combination of insecure tenure, self-built housing and incomplete municipal recognition. That combination shapes what is possible technologically, and it shapes who is able to make decisions about the household.
[P41|Normal] Three features are particularly relevant here. The first is density. Dwellings are close together, access routes are narrow, and space for equipment is limited. A technology that needs a dedicated yard, a water supply or a stable foundation may not fit. The second is the relationship between landlord and tenant. Many households rent a room or a shack on someone else's plot. The meter may be in the landlord's name, the rent may include electricity, and the tenant may have little say over wiring or appliances. The third is fuel stacking. Households rarely use one fuel for everything. Electricity may be used for lighting, television and phone charging, while paraffin, firewood, charcoal or LPG are used for cooking and space heating. The choice is not a matter of habit alone. It reflects cost per unit of useful energy, the reliability of the supply, the size of the pot, the weather, the availability of fuel and the taste of the food (Musango, 2022; Musango et al., 2020).
[P42|Normal] Fuel stacking has consequences that a simple access metric cannot capture. Indoor smoke from solid fuels remains a significant health risk, particularly for women and young children. Paraffin is a common cause of fires and of accidental ingestion. Collecting wood takes time, and the time is usually taken from women. Cooking on an electric hotplate during load-shedding is impossible, so a household that has "access" may still keep a paraffin stove for the evenings when the power is off. These are not peripheral details. They are the substance of what an energy service is, and they are the reason why a technology that performs well in a laboratory can perform poorly in a settlement.
[P43|Heading 3] 3.1.3 Living Labs and decentralised energy technologies
[P44|Normal] The Living Lab has become a common way of organising innovation in real-world settings. The term covers a family of arrangements in which users, researchers, firms and public bodies co-create, prototype and test solutions in the context where those solutions will be used (Bergvall-Kåreborn and Ståhlbröst, 2009; Leminen et al., 2012). Living Labs have been applied in information and communication technology, health, urban planning and, more recently, energy. A systematic review of the energy literature found that the concept is used in several different ways: as a physical space, as a methodology, as an organisation and as a network (Mbatha and Musango, 2022). The same review notes that energy Living Labs in African informal settlements are few, and that the evidence on their outcomes is thin.
[P45|Normal] The South African research programme on gendered energy innovation is the most direct precedent for this study. Over several years, a group of researchers working in poor urban environments developed a gender analysis framework (Anditi et al., 2022), a systems model of energy service security (Batinge et al., 2022), a multi-stakeholder co-design toolkit (Petrulaityte et al., 2022) and a process for setting up energy Living Labs (Mukama et al., 2022). Their work established that gender is not an add-on to energy innovation and that the security of energy services depends on social as well as technical arrangements. It also established a practical vocabulary for working with communities. This study builds on that programme rather than claiming to start from nothing.
[P46|Normal] Biogas is one of several decentralised technologies that could be examined. It is selected here for three reasons. First, anaerobic digestion converts organic waste into a combustible gas that can be used for cooking, which is the energy service that electricity most often fails to provide in informal settlements. Second, the technology makes the socio-material arrangement visible. A digester needs feedstock, water, space, a stable temperature, regular attention and somewhere to put the digestate. Each of those requirements connects the artefact to the household, the neighbourhood, the municipality and the waste stream. Third, community-scale biogas has a mixed record in southern Africa. Some installations work for years; many fail within a short period because of maintenance, feedstock shortages, water constraints or a lack of agreement about who is responsible for the system. That mixed record is analytically useful. It provides the variation that a study of acceptance needs.
[P47|Normal] Biogas is not presented here as a universal solution. It is a test case for a framework. If the framework can explain why a biogas system is taken up in one part of a settlement and abandoned in another, it should be transferable to other decentralised technologies, including solar water heating, improved cookstoves and small-scale PV.
[P48|Heading 3] 3.1.4 Why this is an Engineering Management problem
[P49|Normal] An engineering manager working on an energy project in an informal settlement has to make decisions that are technical, organisational and political at the same time. Which technology? At what scale? Who owns it? Who maintains it? How is it paid for? Who is trained? What happens when a part fails? These questions are not answered by a technical specification. They are answered through negotiations between households, municipal officials, suppliers, funders and community leaders, and they are answered under conditions of uncertainty.
[P50|Normal] The discipline of Engineering Management has tools for these questions: requirements analysis, systems modelling, project and lifecycle management, risk assessment, technology assessment and operations management. What it has lacked is a theoretical account of the relationship between the technology and the social arrangement into which it is introduced. Without such an account, the tools are applied to the artefact and the arrangement is left to chance. This study argues that the arrangement is the proper object of design, and that a theory is needed to describe it.
[P51|Heading 2] 3.2 Problem statement
[P52|Normal] Energy interventions in South African informal settlements tend to follow a similar pattern. A technology is selected, often for good technical reasons. A pilot is funded. The equipment is installed. Training is provided. For a period, the technology works. Then something changes: a part fails and cannot be sourced, the feedstock becomes too expensive, the person who maintained the system moves away, the landlord raises the rent, or the promised service does not arrive. Use declines, the equipment is abandoned, and the project is recorded as a technical success that did not achieve its social objectives.
[P53|Normal] There are three explanations for this pattern, and this study takes all three seriously. The first is analytical. The models that dominate applied energy research treat acceptance as an individual decision made about a finished artefact. The Technology Acceptance Model and its successors ask whether a person perceives a technology as useful and easy to use (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Those questions can be answered in a survey, but they cannot explain why the same technology is used in one street and abandoned in the next, or why a household that reports a positive attitude stops using a system after three months. The models abstract away the very things that matter: the cost of fuel, the reliability of the supply, the space available, the division of labour in the household, and the terms on which the dwelling is occupied.
[P54|Normal] The second explanation is methodological. Participation is now a standard requirement in energy projects, but it is often consultative rather than consequential. Residents are invited to a meeting, their views are recorded, and the design is finalised elsewhere. Participation of this kind can legitimise a decision that has already been taken. It can also raise expectations that the project cannot meet, and it can give an advantage to those who are already comfortable speaking in public. The literature on participation has described these failure modes for decades (Arnstein, 1969), yet they recur in energy projects because the incentives to consult are stronger than the incentives to share control.
[P55|Normal] The third explanation is evaluative. Projects are usually judged by outputs: the number of connections, the installed capacity, the number of people trained. These are legitimate measures, but they say little about whether the intervention was just. A project can connect a settlement and still leave the poorest households paying more per unit than wealthier ones. It can reduce smoke in the kitchen and transfer the burden of collecting feedstock to girls. It can create a community asset that is captured by a local elite. Without criteria that make these distributions visible, an evaluation can report success while the underlying injustice persists.
[P56|Normal] The problem, stated compactly, is this:
[TABLE 1] 1 rows x 1 cols
  | Problem statement. Energy technology interventions in South African informal settlements frequently fail to produce durable improvements in energy access because they are designed as transfers of finished artefacts rather than as reconfigurations of the socio-material arrangements through which energy services are produced. Engineering management lacks a systematic means of involving end-users as co-designers, of anticipating how materials, infrastructures, institutions and household practices will shape acceptance, and of judging whether the resulting arrangement is just. The consequence is a recurring pattern of misaligned technologies, abandoned pilots, unevenly distributed benefits and costs, and continued energy poverty.
[P58|Heading 2] 3.3 Research aim and objectives
[P59|Normal] The aim of the study is to develop, apply and evaluate a Systemic Living Lab for Just Energy Transition (SETILL) that supports the co-design of responsive energy technology innovations in a South African informal settlement, and to assess whether the resulting arrangement improves energy access and advances energy justice.
[P60|Normal] The aim is pursued through five objectives. Each objective corresponds to one research question and to a stage of the research design.
[P61|Normal] To establish how Systems Living Lab concepts frame the relationship between inequality, energy security, energy poverty, energy technology innovation and informal settlements, and to derive a theoretical account of that relationship from critical posthumanism.
[P62|Normal] To determine the socio-material factors that shape the involvement and participation of end-users in decision-making, co-design, co-creation, co-research, prototyping and testing of energy technology innovations in South African informal settlements.
[P63|Normal] To examine the cultural elements, norms, practices and technological mechanisms that influence the acceptance of end-user energy innovations in informal settlements.
[P64|Normal] To develop and evaluate a Systems Living Lab, operationalised through a community-scale biogas pilot, that engages end-users and other stakeholders in participatory design in a real-world setting.
[P65|Normal] To evaluate whether the SETILL framework can function as a coherent theoretical framework and a practical tool for improving energy security, reducing energy poverty and advancing energy justice.

[P76|Heading 2] 3.5 Justification of the study
[P77|Heading 3] 3.5.1 Academic justification
[P78|Normal] The study addresses a gap at the intersection of four literatures: energy justice, Living Lab methodology, socio-technical systems thinking and critical posthumanism. Each literature is well developed in its own field. Energy justice has produced a robust normative vocabulary but has said little about how an engineer should design a prototype. Living Lab research has produced detailed accounts of co-creation but has tended to evaluate outputs rather than justice. Systems thinking has supplied tools for mapping complexity but has often remained functionalist in its assumptions. Posthumanism has produced a sophisticated ontology of human–technology relations but has rarely been applied to energy infrastructure in the Global South.
[P79|Normal] The contribution is not the invention of a new theory. It is the construction of a defensible bridge between these bodies of work, tested against a case. That kind of bridge-building is a legitimate scholarly contribution, but it has to be done carefully. It requires that each tradition be represented on its own terms, that the points of incompatibility be stated rather than smoothed over, and that the resulting framework generate propositions that could fail.
[P80|Heading 3] 3.5.2 Practical justification
[P81|Normal] For practitioners, the study offers three things. The first is a design protocol that treats the socio-material configuration, rather than the artefact, as the unit of intervention. The second is a set of evaluation criteria that make distributional and procedural questions visible alongside technical performance. The third is evidence about what happens when a community-scale biogas system is co-designed in an informal settlement, including the conditions under which it is likely to be maintained. These are modest outputs, but they are the kind that project managers and municipal officials can use.
[P82|Heading 3] 3.5.3 Policy and sector justification
[P83|Normal] At the policy level, the study speaks to the implementation of the just transition framework at municipal scale. Municipalities are responsible for distribution, for integrated development planning and, in many cases, for the informal settlements that national policy treats as temporary. The study's attention to procedural justice, to the terms on which residents are included, and to the distribution of costs and benefits is directly relevant to the design of municipal energy programmes and to the evaluation of JETP-funded projects.
[P84|Heading 3] 3.5.4 Methodological justification
[P85|Normal] The study contributes a methodological approach that combines reflexive thematic analysis, situational mapping, causal loop diagramming and diffractive reading. The combination is unusual. Each method is established, but they are rarely used together, and the epistemological work of combining them has not been done. The study will document how the combination was applied, what it produced that a single method would have missed, and where it created tensions. That documentation is itself a contribution to qualitative energy research.
[P86|Heading 2] 3.6 Scope and delimitations
[P87|Normal] The study is deliberately bounded. It examines one informal settlement in Greater Tzaneen, Limpopo Province, over a period of three years. The empirical work focuses on household cooking and on a community-scale biogas system. It does not attempt to model the national electricity system, to evaluate the full life-cycle impacts of biogas, or to estimate willingness to pay across a representative sample. Those questions are important, but they require different designs and different resources.
[P88|Normal] The study is a proposal. No fieldwork has been conducted and no empirical data exist. The methods described in Section 7 are intended methods. They will be implemented only after ethical clearance and after agreements have been reached with the municipality, the community leadership and the households involved.
[P89|Normal] Four further delimitations should be stated. First, the study does not claim that biogas is the best technology for the site. It claims that biogas is a useful case through which to examine the framework. Second, the study does not aim for statistical generalisation. It aims for analytical generalisation to theory, in the sense described by Yin (2009) and defended by Flyvbjerg (2006). Third, the study does not evaluate the health impacts of indoor air pollution using clinical measures. It records reported symptoms and observed conditions, and it treats them as one dimension of wellbeing rather than as a medical endpoint. Fourth, the study does not treat "the community" as a unified actor. It deliberately samples for difference, including tenants, landlords, women's groups, youth and municipal officials.

[P97|Heading 2] 4.1 Review approach
[P98|Normal] This is an integrative review rather than a systematic review. The distinction is deliberate. A systematic review answers a narrow question by exhausting a defined body of evidence. An integrative review synthesises literature from several traditions in order to frame a problem and identify a gap (Torraco, 2016). This study needs the second, because the question sits between fields that do not share a vocabulary. Energy engineering, energy justice, Living Lab research and posthumanist philosophy each describe part of the problem, and none describes the whole of it.
[P99|Normal] The review follows a documented search strategy, set out in Table 2.1. It does not claim to be exhaustive, and it does not use the language of PRISMA. The earlier draft of this proposal described the review as a "stand-alone qualitative systematic literature review (realist review)" but reported no protocol, search strings, screening counts or synthesis method. That claim was not defensible. It is replaced here by an accurate description of what was done, together with a search log that can be inspected. If the examiners require a systematic review at a later stage, a protocol can be registered and a second review conducted.

[P103|Heading 2] 4.2 Energy poverty and energy security in South African informal settlements
[P104|Normal] Energy poverty is usually defined as the inability to obtain the energy services needed for a decent life. The definition is deliberately broader than "lack of electricity". It includes the quantity and quality of energy, its affordability, the reliability of supply, the safety of its use and the time required to obtain it. A household that has a connection but cannot afford to use it is energy poor. So is a household that cooks with paraffin because the electricity supply fails in the evening, or one that spends hours collecting wood because the local vendor has run out of gas.
[P105|Normal] South Africa's energy poverty is historically produced. Apartheid spatial planning located black households far from work and services, and the electricity system was built to serve industry and a minority of households. The post-1994 electrification programme was one of the largest in the world, and it raised the connection rate from around a third of households to around nine in ten (Statistics South Africa, 2023). That achievement is real. But it has not eliminated energy poverty, because connection was only one of several conditions. Tariffs have risen sharply, dwellings are often poorly insulated and inadequately wired, and supply interruptions have become routine. The result is a pattern in which formal access coexists with informal practice: households are connected and still stack fuels, still borrow electricity, still cook on paraffin when the power fails.
[P106|Normal] Figure 2.1 represents that pattern as a chain from carrier to service, with the losses at each step. The right-hand column is the part that a technical account usually stops at. The band at the bottom is the part it usually omits.

[P109|Normal] The literature on South African informal settlements has documented this pattern in detail. Musango (2022) uses a quantitative storytelling approach in the Groenheuwel settlement to show how gender, income and dwelling characteristics interact in household energy transitions. Musango et al. (2020) argue that the security of energy services, rather than the security of supply, is the appropriate organising concept for poor urban environments, because households experience insecurity through the services they fail to obtain rather than through the national balance of generation and demand. Batinge et al. (2022) formalise this insight in a systems model, showing how gendered innovation and service security are linked through feedback. Anditi et al. (2022) provide a gender analysis framework that makes visible the different roles, burdens and knowledge of women and men in energy innovation. Oosthuizen et al. (2020), in a structured review, identify the factors that have hindered the mainstreaming of gendered energy interventions in poor urban environments.
[P110|Normal] Three limitations recur. First, households are often treated as homogeneous units. The household is not a single decision-maker with a single preference. It is a set of relationships in which age, gender, tenure and income shape who decides, who cooks, who pays and who bears the risk. A technology that is acceptable to the household head may be unacceptable to the person who has to clean it. Second, the urban informal settlement is sometimes conflated with the rural village. The two share poverty and state neglect, but they differ in density, land markets, tenure, infrastructure and proximity to services. A framework built for rural energy poverty does not travel unchanged to an urban settlement. Third, the literature is stronger on description than on design. It explains why energy poverty persists, but it offers limited guidance on what an engineer should change in a prototype, a tariff or a service model.
[P111|Normal] Energy security, in this literature, is best understood as a household-level accomplishment. A household is secure when it can obtain the services it needs across normal and disrupted conditions, without unacceptable cost or risk. That definition connects the national conversation about generation capacity to the daily conversation about cooking. It also makes the distribution of security an ethical question, because security for one household can be purchased at the expense of another: a landlord who installs a prepaid meter secures his revenue and transfers the risk of arrears to the tenant.
[P112|Heading 2] 4.3 Just transition and energy justice
[P113|Normal] The idea of a just transition began in the labour and environmental movements of the 1980s and 1990s, where it named the claim that workers in polluting industries should not bear the cost of environmental protection. It has since expanded. In its contemporary form, a just transition is one that is fair in its outcomes, open in its procedures and respectful of the identities and knowledge of affected communities (Newell and Mulvaney, 2013; Swilling and Annecke, 2012). Winkler (2020) gives the concept a neo-Gramscian reading, arguing that transitions are shaped by the balance of social forces and that a transition that does not shift that balance will reproduce existing inequalities under a greener label.
[P114|Normal] In South Africa, the just transition has become official policy. The Presidential Climate Commission's framework sets out principles for distribution, procedure and restoration, and the Just Energy Transition Partnership has attached international finance to the coal phase-down (Presidential Climate Commission, 2022). Research on implementation has been less optimistic. Mirzania et al. (2023) identify structural and institutional barriers to powering past coal, including municipal capacity, vested interests and the social dependency of coal-dependent towns. Molepo et al. (2025) interview industry experts and find that the barriers are distributed across policy, finance, skills and governance, and that they interact. The transition is not blocked by a single missing factor that could be supplied by a single intervention.
[P115|Normal] Energy justice supplies the normative vocabulary for evaluating these processes. The most widely used formulation identifies three tenets: distributive justice, which concerns the distribution of benefits and burdens; procedural justice, which concerns who participates and on what terms; and recognition justice, which concerns whose knowledge, identities and practices are acknowledged (McCauley et al., 2013; Jenkins et al., 2016). Sovacool and Dworkin (2015) add a set of principles, including availability, affordability, due process and intergenerational equity. Heffron and McCauley (2017) add restorative justice, the repair of past harm. Walker and Day (2012) make a related argument in the context of fuel poverty: distribution, recognition and procedure are not separate dimensions to be traded off, because a failure of recognition is itself a form of distributive injustice.
[P116|Normal] The literature has two weaknesses for present purposes. The first is scale. Energy justice is usually applied to policy, siting conflicts and large infrastructure. It has relatively little to say about the design of a household or community-scale technology. Sovacool and Dworkin's principles do not tell an engineer which material to specify, how to organise maintenance, or how to decide who sits on a management committee. The second is operationalisation. Recognition is often reduced to "stakeholder engagement", and procedure to "consultation". Both reductions lose the critical content of the concepts. A consultation can be procedurally correct and still be a mechanism for legitimising a decision that was taken elsewhere.
[P117|Normal] What the study takes from this literature is a set of evaluative questions, not a ready-made design method. The questions are: who receives what, through which procedure, with whose knowledge recognised, and with what repair for past harm? The framework in Section 6 turns these questions into indicators. The theoretical framework in Section 5 explains why they cannot be answered by a purely technical assessment.
[P118|Heading 2] 4.4 Living Labs and systems thinking
[P119|Normal] A Living Lab is a real-world arrangement for innovation in which users, researchers, firms and public bodies collaborate over time. Definitions vary, but most agree on four elements: a real-life setting, multiple stakeholders, co-creation with users, and iterative experimentation (Bergvall-Kåreborn and Ståhlbröst, 2009; Leminen et al., 2012). Living Labs have been classified by who drives them (provider, utilizer, enabler or user), by their sector, and by the degree to which they are physically or virtually constituted. Hossain et al. (2019), in a systematic review, find that the literature is fragmented and that many studies describe individual labs without comparing them. Dell'Era and Landoni (2014) position the Living Lab between user-centred design and participatory design, which is useful because it makes clear that a Living Lab is not simply a consultation method. It is an organisational form.
[P120|Normal] The energy Living Lab literature is smaller. Mbatha and Musango (2022) review the application of the concept in the energy sector and find that it is used inconsistently: sometimes as a space, sometimes as a method, sometimes as a network. They propose a six-phase process for setting up an energy Living Lab in an urban informal settlement, from scoping and stakeholder identification to evaluation and scaling. Mukama et al. (2022) describe the development of Living Labs for gendered energy technology innovation in poor urban environments, and Petrulaityte et al. (2022) present the GENS co-design toolkit that supports multi-stakeholder collaboration. Von Wirth et al. (2019), writing on urban Living Labs in Europe, show that labs can influence sustainability transitions through mechanisms such as embedding, learning and network formation, but that their impact depends on how the lab is connected to the wider regime.
[P121|Normal] Systems thinking supplies a different set of resources. Checkland (1981) argues that systems are not objects in the world but ways of organising inquiry; the boundary of a system is a choice, and the choice has political consequences. Meadows (2008) shows how feedback, stocks and flows produce behaviour that cannot be inferred from the parts. Midgley (2000) develops a critical systems approach in which boundary critique is central: the analyst asks who is included and excluded by a given framing, and what would change if the boundary were drawn differently. Sterman (2000) provides the modelling vocabulary of causal loops and delays. Cilliers (1998) cautions that complex systems cannot be fully represented, so any model is a reduction that must be used with care.
[P122|Normal] The combination of Living Lab and systems thinking is often asserted rather than derived. A "systemic Living Lab" is usually a lab that uses systems tools, not a lab whose boundaries, stakeholder set, feedback architecture and evaluation criteria are derived from systems theory. That distinction is the subject of Section 5.8. It matters because a lab that is systemic only in its vocabulary will reproduce the conventional boundary of the project: funders, implementers and selected community representatives, with the wider settlement treated as context.
[P123|Normal] The empirical evidence on energy Living Labs in African informal settlements is thin. The South African work is recent and mostly descriptive. There are few longitudinal studies, few comparative studies and few evaluations that ask whether the lab produced outcomes that would not have occurred without it. This is not a criticism of the researchers, who are working in difficult conditions. It is a statement about the state of the evidence, and it shapes the claims that this study can make. The study can contribute a carefully documented case. It cannot claim to establish general effectiveness.
[P124|Heading 2] 4.5 End-user participation and acceptance
[P125|Normal] Participation has a long and sceptical literature. Arnstein's (1969) ladder distinguishes manipulation and therapy from consultation, partnership and citizen control. The ladder is more than fifty years old, but its central insight remains: participation is not a single activity, and the difference between consultation and control is the difference between legitimising a decision and sharing it. Sanders and Stappers (2008) describe the shift from designing for users to designing with them, and von Hippel (2005) shows that users can be important sources of innovation, particularly when their needs are unusual and their knowledge is tacit.
[P126|Normal] Energy research has adopted participation as a norm but has been slower to examine its conditions. Wüstenhagen et al. (2007) distinguish social acceptance at three levels: socio-political, community and market. The distinction is useful because it shows that a technology can be acceptable in principle and resisted in a particular place. But the model remains attitudinal. It asks what people think about a technology, not what the technology, the infrastructure and the household practice do together.
[P127|Normal] The dominant model of acceptance in engineering and information systems is the Technology Acceptance Model. TAM holds that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use predict intention to use, which in turn predicts use (Davis, 1989). UTAUT extends the model with social influence and facilitating conditions (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Both models have been enormously productive. They are also built on assumptions that do not fit the present problem. They assume an individual chooser, a finished artefact, a measurable intention and a context that can be treated as background. They have little to say about the cost of fuel, the reliability of supply, the division of labour in a household, the terms of tenure, or the material properties of a dwelling. A TAM study can tell us that residents find a biogas system useful. It cannot tell us why the system is abandoned when the water supply fails.
[P128|Normal] Practice theory and the energy cultures framework offer an alternative. Shove (2003) argues that consumption is shaped by conventions of comfort, cleanliness and convenience, which are collective and material rather than individual. Stephenson et al. (2010) propose that energy behaviour is shaped by the interaction of norms, practices and material culture. These approaches are closer to the present study because they treat technology and practice as co-constitutive. But they are stronger on explanation than on design. They describe how practices persist; they say less about how a design team might intervene.
[P129|Normal] Participation also has failure modes that the literature documents but projects often ignore. Consultation can be tokenistic. Meetings can be dominated by those with time, confidence and status. Expectations can be raised and then disappointed. Benefits can be captured by local elites. Participation can create fatigue, particularly when communities are asked to participate repeatedly in projects that do not endure. These are not arguments against participation. They are arguments for designing participation with the same care as the technology, and for evaluating it rather than assuming its virtue.
[P130|Heading 2] 4.6 Decentralised energy and biogas
[P131|Normal] Anaerobic digestion is a mature technology. Bacteria break down organic matter in the absence of oxygen, producing a gas that is roughly 50 to 70 per cent methane and a residue that can be used as a soil amendment. The process requires a consistent feedstock, water, a temperature range that supports microbial activity, and a retention time that allows digestion to complete. In practice, the design of a digester is a design of a supply chain and a maintenance arrangement as much as a design of a vessel.
[P132|Normal] Biogas has been promoted across Africa for decades, with mixed results. Successful installations tend to share several features: a reliable feedstock supply, a user who understands the system, a maintenance arrangement with access to spare parts, and a use for the gas that fits existing cooking practices. Failures tend to share the opposite features. Feedstock runs out, water is too far away, the digester is too small for the household, the gas pressure is too low, the stove does not fit the pot, or the person who was trained leaves. The technical literature often treats these as "non-technical barriers". A posthumanist reading would treat them as part of the technology: the digester is not the vessel alone, but the vessel plus the feedstock, the water, the labour, the stove, the spare parts and the agreement about who maintains it.
[P133|Normal] In South African informal settlements, the conditions for biogas are both promising and difficult. Organic waste is plentiful in some areas, particularly where there is a market or food trade. Water is often shared and sometimes costly. Space is limited, and tenure is insecure. Cooking practices vary, and gas may not be acceptable for all dishes. Municipal waste services are uneven. These conditions do not make biogas impossible. They make the configuration the central design problem, which is precisely why biogas is a useful case for this study.
[P134|Heading 2] 4.7 Posthumanism in energy and engineering
[P135|Normal] Posthumanism has been applied extensively in education, geography, feminist technoscience and design, but rarely in energy engineering. Forlano (2017) argues that design should take non-human agency seriously, and that this changes what counts as a design problem. Ulmer (2017) sets out posthumanism as a research methodology, with implications for how data are produced and interpreted. Aboalez (2025), writing in engineering education, makes the case for a posthumanist social approach to energy transition. That paper is the closest precedent for the present study and is discussed in Section 5.4.
[P136|Normal] The gap is not that posthumanism has never been mentioned in energy research. It is that there is no developed account of how distributed agency shapes the design, acceptance and use of energy technologies in informal settlements. The literature on energy justice tells us what justice requires. The literature on Living Labs tells us how to organise collaboration. The literature on posthumanism tells us how to think about human–technology relations. What is missing is a framework that connects the three at the scale of a settlement project.

[P145|Heading 1] 5. Theoretical Framework
[P146|Heading 2] 5.1 Why a theoretical framework matters in doctoral research
[P147|Normal] A theoretical framework is a set of assumptions and concepts, connected by an argument, that determines what a study observes, how it interprets what it observes, and what it will accept as an explanation. It is not a summary of theories that seem relevant. It is the structure that makes the study coherent, and it does three kinds of work at once. It specifies an ontology, a view of what exists and what kinds of things can act. It specifies an epistemology, a view of how knowledge about those things can be produced. And it specifies an axiology, a view of what the research is for and whose interests it serves.
[P148|Normal] The literature on doctoral supervision is consistent about the function of the framework, even when it disagrees about the detail. Grant and Osanloo (2014) describe the theoretical framework as the blueprint of a dissertation: it determines the rooms that can be built and the connections between them. Ravitch and Riggan (2017) go further, arguing that a conceptual framework is not a diagram added at the end of a literature review but the researcher's reasoned account of why the study is designed as it is. Sandberg and Alvesson (2011) show that the construction of a research question is itself a theoretical act, because the question already assumes a way of dividing the world. Alvesson and Kärreman (2007) make a related point about theory development: empirical material becomes interesting when it resists the theory that was used to generate it, not when it confirms it.
[P149|Normal] Four functions follow from this. First, a framework prevents description from masquerading as analysis. A study that reports what people said, without a theory of why they said it, produces data rather than knowledge. Second, it makes assumptions contestable. If a study assumes that households are rational choosers, that assumption can be examined and, if necessary, rejected. If the assumption is left implicit, it cannot be examined. Third, a framework connects a study to a tradition. It allows the reader to see which conversation the study is joining, and therefore what counts as a contribution. Fourth, it disciplines interpretation. A researcher who has committed to a theory can be held to its implications; a researcher who has not can explain any finding after the fact.
[P150|Normal] The relationship between theory and empirical work runs in both directions. Theory shapes the questions, the design and the interpretation. Empirical material, in turn, can revise the theory. This is the logic of abduction, which Alvesson and Kärreman (2007) describe as a movement between a theoretical frame and a surprising observation. The surprise is not a nuisance. It is the point at which the study has something to say.
[P151|Normal] A theoretical framework is not the same as a conceptual framework, and the distinction matters for the structure of this proposal. The theoretical framework is the general body of theory that the study adopts, together with the justification for adopting it. It is abstract and portable. The conceptual framework is the researcher's own model of the specific problem, derived from theory, literature and context. It is concrete and local. Section 5 establishes the theory. Section 6 constructs the model. A common weakness in applied research is to collapse the two, so that the model is presented as if it were a theory and the theory is never tested against anything.
[P152|Normal] The consequences of a weak or absent framework are predictable. Variables are selected ad hoc. Claims are made that cannot be falsified. Findings cannot be generalised beyond the case, because there is no theory to say what they are a case of. Recommendations follow from the researcher's preferences rather than from the analysis. In engineering management, where the pressure to be useful is strong, this failure is common. A framework is sometimes treated as an academic ornament that delays the practical work. The argument of this section is the opposite: without a framework, the practical work has no way of knowing what it is looking at.
[P153|Heading 2] 5.2 Theoretical frameworks in Engineering Management research
[P154|Normal] Engineering Management sits at the intersection of technical systems, organisational behaviour, economics, innovation studies and public policy. Its problems are rarely confined to one level. A decision about a technology is also a decision about skills, contracts, maintenance, regulation and trust. A project that is technically sound can fail because the organisation that was supposed to operate it did not have the capacity, or because the tariff structure made the service unaffordable, or because the community had learned from previous projects that promises are not kept.
[P155|Normal] The field has borrowed theories from several disciplines. From psychology and information systems it has taken the Technology Acceptance Model and its successors. From operations it has taken Lean Thinking and the resource-based view. From complexity science it has taken complexity theory and systems thinking. From sociology and innovation studies it has taken socio-technical systems theory and the multi-level perspective. Each of these has been productive in the settings for which it was developed. The difficulty arises when a theory developed for a firm, an individual or a market is applied to an infrastructural problem in a low-income settlement. The theory does not simply lose precision. It mis-specifies the object.
[P156|Normal] Three mis-specifications recur. The first is level confusion. A theory of individual intention is used to explain a collective outcome, or a theory of firm strategy is used to explain a household practice. The second is the black-boxing of technology. The artefact is treated as a fixed input whose properties are known, when in fact its properties are part of what has to be explained. The third is the treatment of context as background. Context is what the model controls for, rather than what the model is about. In a study of energy in an informal settlement, context is the substance: tenure, density, infrastructure, income, gender and municipal capacity are not variables to be held constant. They are the conditions that make the technology work or fail.
[P157|Normal] A framework adequate to this problem needs to be relational and multi-level. It needs to hold human and non-human elements in one analytical frame, without reducing one to the other. It needs to be normative, because the problem is about justice and not only about efficiency. And it needs to be empirically tractable, because the study has to produce findings that can be defended. These four requirements are the criteria against which the theory is selected in Section 5.11.
[P158|Normal] There is also a question of disciplinary fit. Engineering management values usefulness. A theory that cannot be used to make a decision will not survive in the field, however elegant it is. The framework developed here is therefore assessed not only for its philosophical coherence but for whether it changes what a design team would do on a Monday morning.
[P159|Heading 2] 5.3 Philosophical foundations of critical posthumanism
[P160|Heading 3] 5.3.1 Humanism and its limits
[P161|Normal] Humanism is less a single doctrine than a family of commitments. It treats the human being as the central subject of knowledge and value. It separates culture from nature, mind from body, and subject from object. It assumes that the human has a essence that is universal, and that this essence is the measure against which other beings are judged. These commitments made possible important achievements: universal rights, scientific inquiry, the critique of arbitrary authority. They also produced exclusions. The universal human was, in practice, a particular kind of human: male, European, propertied, rational and able-bodied. Those who did not fit were treated as less than fully human, or as nature rather than culture, or as objects of study rather than participants in knowledge.
[P162|Normal] Posthumanism begins from this diagnosis. It does not reject the achievements of humanism. It rejects the pretence that the human is a self-contained, universal subject, and it asks what becomes visible when that pretence is dropped. The question is not "what comes after the human?" but "how has the human been produced, and what else is involved in that production?"
[P163|Heading 3] 5.3.2 Three formations that must not be conflated
[P164|Normal] The term posthumanism covers several projects, and the differences matter.
[P165|Normal] The first is posthumanism as a historical condition. The human is increasingly entangled with technologies, data, infrastructures and ecological systems. The boundaries between body and machine, nature and culture, are becoming harder to draw. This condition is described by Hayles (1999) and by Braidotti (2013), although they evaluate it differently.
[P166|Normal] The second is critical posthumanism. This is a normative and philosophical project. It draws on anti-humanism, feminism, postcolonial thought and science and technology studies. It affirms difference rather than universalism, and it treats knowledge as situated. Braidotti (2013, 2019) is its most systematic contemporary exponent. Wolfe (2010) distinguishes it carefully from the third formation.
[P167|Normal] The third is transhumanism. Transhumanism seeks to enhance human capacities through technology: longer life, greater intelligence, control over the body. It is often described as posthuman, but its values remain humanist. It celebrates perfectibility, mastery and transcendence. It treats the body as a limitation to be overcome rather than as a condition of knowledge. This study rejects transhumanism, and the rejection is not a matter of taste. Transhumanism would direct attention to the enhancement of individual capacities and away from the collective, material and institutional arrangements that are the subject of this study.
[P168|Normal] A fourth position, technological determinism, is also rejected. Determinism holds that technology drives social change according to its own logic. The alternative adopted here is not that society is autonomous, but that technology and society are co-constituted. A prepaid meter does not cause a particular relation between landlord and tenant. It materialises a relation that is already being negotiated, and in doing so it changes the terms of the negotiation.
[P169|Heading 3] 5.3.3 Relational ontology
[P170|Normal] The central ontological claim of critical posthumanism is that entities do not precede their relations. This is stated most rigorously by Barad (2007). In agential realism, the primary unit is the phenomenon, not the object. A phenomenon is an entanglement of matter and meaning in which boundaries are enacted. The entities that appear within a phenomenon, which Barad calls relata, acquire their properties through intra-action. They do not exist first and then interact. Intra-action is the relation through which the relata emerge.
[P171|Normal] This is a difficult claim, and it is easy to repeat without understanding it. An example may help. A biogas digester is not a thing with fixed properties that is then introduced into a household. What the digester is, in practice, depends on the feedstock that is available, the water that can be spared, the space that is allocated, the stove that is used, the person who maintains it and the agreements that govern it. Change the water supply and the digester becomes a different object: it may become a burden rather than a service. The properties that matter are not intrinsic to the vessel. They are produced in the relation.
[P172|Normal] Haraway's (1988) epistemology is a companion to this ontology. Knowledge is always from somewhere. The idea of a "view from nowhere" is not a description of objectivity but a concealment of a particular position. Situated knowledge is not less objective; it is objective in a different way, because it can be located and therefore checked. For this study, the implication is direct: the knowledge of a resident about how a stove behaves in a small kitchen is not anecdote to be validated by an expert. It is knowledge produced at a particular site, and it is indispensable to a design that has to work at that site.
[P173|Heading 3] 5.3.4 Distributed agency
[P174|Normal] If entities emerge through relations, then agency cannot be a property that belongs to humans alone. Barad (2007) describes agency as an enactment, not an attribute. Agency is distributed across the apparatus through which a phenomenon is observed and through the materials that participate in it. The agential cut is the boundary that is drawn, within a phenomenon, between subject and object, or between what is observed and what observes. The cut is not arbitrary, but it is a cut: it includes some things and excludes others, and it has consequences.
[P175|Normal] Latour's (2005) actor-network theory is adjacent to this position but not identical. Latour treats humans and non-humans symmetrically as actants, and he traces the networks through which action is assembled. His work is empirically useful because it shows how a technology enrols allies and how it can be undone. It is less useful for normative questions, because it is deliberately indifferent to the justice of the network it describes. This study uses Latour's sensibility without adopting his ontology wholesale.
[P176|Normal] Bennett's (2010) notion of thing-power is also relevant. Bennett argues that matter has a vitality of its own, that things can resist, surprise and exceed human intention. A digester that produces too little gas, a pipe that clogs, a stove that will not sit flat on a pot: these are not merely technical failures. They are moments in which the material world asserts its own agency and changes what the household can do.
[P177|Normal] The consequence for research is significant. If agency is distributed, then a study that asks only what people think has already excluded most of what is going on. It has excluded the meter, the fuel, the dwelling, the water supply and the maintenance arrangement. Those are not context. They are participants.
[P178|Heading 3] 5.3.5 Materiality and technological agency
[P179|Normal] Posthumanism takes matter seriously. This is what distinguishes it from social constructionism, which can treat the material world as a product of discourse. Matter is not passive. It has properties that enable some actions and constrain others. A prepaid meter, for example, is not a neutral device for measuring consumption. It enacts a particular relation between the household, the utility and money. It requires a purchase before use, it can disconnect a household automatically, and it shifts the management of cash flow onto the household. Those are material effects that shape practice, and they are not captured by asking whether the meter is easy to use.
[P180|Normal] A biogas digester is another example. Its material requirements, which include water, feedstock, temperature and space, produce a set of social requirements: someone must collect the waste, someone must carry the water, someone must be available when the system needs attention, and someone must be able to buy a spare part. The technology distributes labour, and the distribution is usually gendered. This is not a social consequence that follows from a technical artefact. It is a property of the artefact-in-relation.
[P181|Heading 3] 5.3.6 Ecological interdependence
[P182|Normal] Posthumanism also insists on the interdependence of human and ecological systems. The nature–culture distinction is not a description of the world but a way of organising it, and that organisation has costs. The Anthropocene is the name for a condition in which human activity has become a geological force. It is also a condition in which the consequences of that activity are unevenly distributed: those who contributed least to emissions are often most exposed to their effects.
[P183|Normal] For this study, ecological interdependence matters in two ways. First, energy interventions have environmental effects that are not externalities to be added at the end. A biogas system that consumes water in a water-scarce settlement is not environmentally benign simply because it is renewable. Second, environmental justice and social justice are not separate agendas. The same arrangements that expose residents to smoke and fire also expose them to floods, heat and pollution. A framework that treats the social as one domain and the environmental as another will miss the connection.
[P184|Heading 3] 5.3.7 Ethics and power
[P185|Normal] Posthumanist ethics is relational and affirmative. Braidotti (2006, 2013) contrasts potestas, a power that negates and constrains, with potentia, a capacity that enables and creates. The distinction is useful because it avoids the assumption that power is always repressive. A community can have the capacity to organise, to learn and to build, even when it lacks formal authority. The task of a just intervention is to increase potentia without ignoring the potestas that constrains it.
[P186|Normal] Haraway (2016) offers the concept of response-ability: the capacity to respond to others, human and non-human, in a way that is accountable. Response-ability is not a rule. It is a practice of attention. Barad (2007) describes ethics as ethico-onto-epistemology, insisting that questions of being and knowing cannot be separated from questions of value. What we choose to observe, and how we choose to observe it, is already an ethical decision.
[P187|Normal] These ideas have practical implications for a Living Lab. Who is invited to the first meeting is an ethical decision. Which data are recorded and which are not is an ethical decision. Who is credited for an innovation, and who bears the risk of failure, are ethical decisions. A framework that treats ethics as a consent form to be signed at the start will miss most of them.
[P188|Heading 3] 5.3.8 What posthumanism is not
[P189|Normal] The term is used loosely in popular writing, and it is important to be precise. Table 3.2 distinguishes the position adopted here from several positions with which it is often confused.
[P190|Normal] Table 3.2: What posthumanism is not
[TABLE 7] 7 rows x 3 cols
  | Position | Core claim | Relation to this study
  | Transhumanism | Technology should enhance human capacities; the body is a limitation to be overcome | Rejected. It remains humanist in its values and directs attention away from collective material arrangements.
  | Technological determinism | Technology drives social change according to its own logic | Rejected. Technology and society are co-constituted; the arrangement is the unit of analysis.
  | Social constructionism | Reality, including nature, is produced through discourse | Partly rejected. Discourse matters, but matter has properties that resist and shape what can be said and done.

  | Systems thinking | Wholes can be understood through relations, feedback and emergence | Retained as a methodological complement. It is often functionalist and does not, by itself, address justice.
  | Human-centred design | Design should begin from users' needs and experience | Critiqued. It treats the user as the centre and the artefact as a tool, rather than treating the arrangement as the unit.
[P192|Heading 2] 5.4 The four thinkers and their contributions
[P193|Normal] Critical posthumanism is not a single system. The four thinkers used in this study disagree about ontology, method and politics, and the disagreements are productive. Table 3.1 summarises their contributions and limits.
[P194|Normal] Table 3.1: Comparison of the four posthumanist thinkers used in this study
[TABLE 8] 5 rows x 4 cols
  | Thinker | Core concepts | What the study takes | Where the study departs
  | Rosi Braidotti | Nomadic subject; posthuman knowledge; affirmative ethics; potestas and potentia; zoe and bios | A normative vocabulary for justice and difference; the idea of theory as a navigational tool; the distinction between constraining and enabling power | Her frame is abstract and European in reference. It says little about infrastructure, households or empirical design.
  | Donna Haraway | Cyborg; situated knowledges; partial perspective; response-ability; sympoiesis; making kin | An epistemology for situated, partial, community-generated knowledge; a basis for rejecting the technocratic view from nowhere | She deliberately refuses to prescribe method, and the cyborg imagery needs translation to an energy context.
  | Karen Barad | Agential realism; intra-action; agential cut; apparatus; diffraction; ethico-onto-epistemology | The most rigorous ontology of the human–technology relation; the phenomenon as the unit of analysis; diffraction as an analytical practice | The vocabulary is demanding and can become obscure. Applied mechanically, it can flatten human suffering into ontology.
  | N. [P196|Normal] Braidotti supplies the normative horizon. Her critique of humanist universalism is the reason the study cannot treat "the user" as a generic category. Her account of affirmative ethics is the reason the framework is oriented towards capacity-building rather than deficit-repair. Her distinction between potestas and potentia, developed in Transpositions (2006) and used by Aboalez (2025), gives the study a way of talking about power that is not only negative. But Braidotti's work is not a method. It does not tell the researcher what to observe in a settlement, and it can encourage a style of writing that is more evocative than analytical. The study uses her concepts and resists her prose.
[P197|Normal] Haraway supplies the epistemology. Her argument that knowledge is situated is the strongest available alternative to the assumption that the designer knows best. Her concept of response-ability gives the study a way of describing the ethical relation between researchers and residents that is more demanding than informed consent. Her later work on sympoiesis, or making-with, is directly relevant to a Living Lab, because it describes collaboration as a condition of life rather than as a technique. The limitation is that Haraway is deliberately unsystematic. She offers figures and provocations, not procedures.
[P198|Normal] Barad supplies the ontology and the analytical unit. The concept of intra-action is the reason the study treats the phenomenon, rather than the user or the artefact, as the unit of analysis. The concept of the apparatus is the reason the Living Lab can be understood as producing the phenomenon it studies, rather than observing it from outside. Diffraction, the practice of reading insights through one another rather than against one another, is used in the analysis to hold the interview, observational and workshop material together. The risk is obscurity. Barad's vocabulary is drawn from quantum physics, and it can be used to impress rather than to explain. Every technical term in this proposal is therefore defined in plain English and illustrated with an energy example.
[P199|Normal] Hayles supplies the corrective to abstraction. Her account of embodiment insists that information is always instantiated in a body, a device and a material arrangement. Her concept of technogenesis, the idea that humans and technologies co-evolve, supports the study's refusal to treat the technology as an external object. Her work is less useful for questions of justice and participation, and it is not asked to carry that weight.
[P200|Normal] The four thinkers converge on a rejection of the autonomous humanist subject, on relationality, and on the critique of the nature–culture divide. They diverge on almost everything else. Braidotti is a vitalist monist; Barad is a realist about relations; Haraway is a situated pragmatist; Hayles is a media theorist of embodiment. The study does not present them as a single theory. It assembles a small number of concepts from each, states why each is needed, and accepts that the assembly is a construction rather than a discovery.
[P201|Normal] This is also the point at which the study connects to the supervisor's work. Aboalez (2025) draws primarily on Braidotti and Barad to argue for a posthumanist social approach to energy transition. The present study extends that argument from engineering education to settlement-level design, and adds Haraway's epistemology and Hayles's materialism to address questions that the earlier paper did not have to answer.
[P202|Heading 2] 5.5 Posthumanism and socio-technical energy systems
[P203|Normal] An energy system is usually described as a chain: generation, transmission, distribution, metering, billing, appliances and end use. The description is accurate as far as it goes, but it separates the technical from the social and then treats the social as the place where the technical is received. A posthumanist account describes the same system as an assemblage: a configuration of people, materials, infrastructures, institutions, fuels, dwellings, meters, tariffs and practices whose relations produce the capacity to deliver an energy service.
[P204|Normal] The difference is not merely semantic. In the chain description, the household is the end point. In the assemblage description, the household is one element among many, and the service is produced by the relations between them. A cooking service, for example, is produced by the stove, the fuel, the pot, the kitchen, the person who cooks, the money that buys the fuel, the tariff that sets the price, the reliability of the supply and the norms that govern what counts as a proper meal. Change any one of these and the service changes.
[P205|Normal] This way of thinking has become more common in energy social science. Sovacool (2014), reviewing fifteen years of energy scholarship, calls for a social science agenda that takes infrastructure, practice and politics seriously. The multi-level perspective in transition studies describes how niches, regimes and landscapes interact over time (Geels, 2002, 2011). That literature is useful because it explains why transitions are slow and why a promising niche can fail to displace an incumbent regime. Its limitation, for present purposes, is that it operates at a high level of aggregation. It can explain the fate of a technology category. It is less good at explaining why one digester works and the one next door does not.
[P206|Normal] The value of posthumanism is that it operates at the scale of the configuration without losing the wider structures. It can hold the micro and the macro in one frame, because both are composed of relations. The tariff, the municipal by-law, the landlord's rule, the stove and the pot are all elements of the same assemblage, even though they operate at different scales.
[P207|Heading 3] 5.5.1 A worked illustration: the biogas digester
[P208|Normal] Consider a community-scale biogas digester proposed for an informal settlement. A conventional engineering assessment would specify the feedstock, the hydraulic retention time, the digester volume, the gas yield and the capital cost. A TAM study would add measures of perceived usefulness and ease of use, and perhaps a question about whether residents would recommend the system to a neighbour. Both would produce useful information. Neither would explain what happens next.
[P209|Normal] A posthumanist analysis asks a different set of questions.
[P210|Normal] Feedstock. Who produces the organic waste, who collects it, and what else do they do with their time? If the waste is currently sold or fed to animals, the digester is competing with an existing use. If collection is done by women or children, the labour cost falls on them. If the supply is seasonal, the digester's output will be seasonal. The feedstock is not an input to the technology. It is a relation between the technology, the household economy and the division of labour.
[P211|Normal] Water. Digestion requires water, and the ratio of water to feedstock matters. In a settlement where water is shared, purchased or carried, the digester is competing with cooking, cleaning and drinking. A design that assumes a tap in the yard has already excluded the households that are most in need.
[P212|Normal] Space. A digester needs a footprint, a slope, access for maintenance and a safe distance from dwellings. In a dense settlement, space is not empty. It is claimed, used and contested. The siting decision is a decision about whose space is taken and who is compensated.
[P213|Normal] Temperature. Microbial activity depends on temperature. A system that performs well in a warm climate may underperform in winter or at altitude. The material property is not a detail to be handled by a safety factor. It shapes the service that can be promised.
[P214|Normal] Cooking. Gas burns differently from wood or paraffin. The flame is less visible, the heat is more concentrated, and the pot may need to be different. A technology that ignores the pot and the dish will be judged unusable by the person who cooks, whatever the gas yield.
[P215|Normal] Maintenance. A digester needs someone to check the feed, remove the digestate, clear a blockage and replace a valve. That someone needs time, tools, training and a reason to keep going. If the arrangement depends on a volunteer who moves away, the technology has a design fault, not a social problem.
[P216|Normal] Gender. The labour of feeding, cleaning and maintaining a household energy system is usually assigned to women. A system that increases that labour while reducing smoke may be a good intervention on one dimension and a bad one on another. The posthumanist frame makes the distribution visible because it treats labour as part of the assemblage rather than as a preference.
[P217|Normal] Institutions. The digester may require a permit, a waste collection agreement, a connection to the municipal service or a credit arrangement. These are not "enabling environment" factors that sit outside the project. They are elements whose presence or absence changes what the technology is.
[P218|Normal] None of this is invisible to a good engineer. Experienced practitioners know that feedstock, water, space and maintenance decide whether a system survives. The contribution of the theory is not the list of factors. It is the claim that the factors are constitutive of the technology rather than external to it, and that the unit of design is therefore the configuration. If that claim is accepted, the design process changes. It must prototype the arrangement, not only the artefact. It must include the meter, the tariff, the dwelling, the maintenance contract and the waste supply in the prototype. It must evaluate the arrangement, not only the device.
[P219|Normal] The TAM study would tell us that residents find the idea of biogas useful. The posthumanist study would tell us that the system will fail in the households where water is carried, succeed in the households where a market provides a steady waste stream, and produce a new burden for the women who are expected to feed it. The second account is less tidy, but it is more useful for design.
[P220|Heading 2] 5.6 Posthumanism, social justice and just energy transition
[P221|Normal] The normative core of this study is not posthumanism alone. It is a conversation between posthumanism and energy justice. Each supplies something the other lacks.
[P222|Normal] Posthumanism adds four things to the justice literature. First, it extends the moral community beyond humans. Materials, ecologies and future generations are not merely resources to be allocated; they are participants in the arrangement whose flourishing matters. Second, it reframes justice as a property of composition rather than only of distribution. The question is not only how much of a benefit each household receives, but which actors and materials are admitted to the arrangement in the first place. Third, it makes visible forms of exclusion that a human-centred recognition analysis can miss. A dwelling that cannot accommodate a technology, or an infrastructure that excludes by design, is a form of injustice that does not appear in a survey of attitudes. Fourth, it supplies a critique of the technocratic view from nowhere. The designer who claims to be neutral is usually occupying a position that others do not have.
[P223|Normal] Posthumanism also has limits, and they should be stated plainly. It does not specify distributive criteria. It cannot tell a municipality how to allocate a budget between two settlements. It does not generate policy instruments. It has no theory of institutions, and it can be vague about how decisions are actually made. Applied without a human-centred justice vocabulary, it can obscure human suffering by dissolving it into ontology. A woman who cannot afford paraffin is not a "phenomenon". She is a person in a specific relation of deprivation, and the analysis must be able to say so.
[P224|Normal] Energy justice supplies the missing operational content. The four dimensions used in this study, distribution, procedure, recognition and restoration, provide criteria that can be assessed. Distribution asks who receives the service and who bears the cost. Procedure asks who participates and on what terms. Recognition asks whose knowledge and practices count. Restoration asks what past harm is repaired and what future capacity is built. These are not derived from posthumanism. They are derived from the justice literature (McCauley et al., 2013; Jenkins et al., 2016; Heffron and McCauley, 2017), and they are retained as a normative-operational complement.
[P225|Normal] Justice also has a scalar dimension. Fraser (2009) argues that the question of the frame, who is included in the "who" of justice, is itself a question of justice. In an energy project, the frame may be the household, the street, the settlement, the municipality or the planet. Each frame makes some claims visible and others invisible. A project that is just at the scale of the settlement may be unjust at the scale of the region, if it displaces pollution or extracts water. The study does not resolve this problem, but it names it and builds a scalar check into the evaluation.
[P226|Normal] The combination is therefore deliberate. Posthumanism provides the ontology and the critique of technocracy. Energy justice provides the criteria and the policy vocabulary. Systems thinking provides the tools for mapping relations and feedback. None of the three is sufficient alone, and the framework is designed so that each can be held accountable by the others.
[P227|Normal] One further point is necessary. The literature on just transition has been criticised for treating "the community" as a homogeneous beneficiary. The same criticism applies to a posthumanist framework if it is not careful. A settlement is stratified by tenure, income, gender, age, ethnicity and political affiliation. A project that benefits "the community" may benefit one group within it and burden another. The framework therefore requires disaggregated evidence. It does not assume that a community-level outcome is a household-level outcome.
[P228|Heading 2] 5.7 Posthumanism and end-user participation
[P229|Heading 3] 5.7.1 The user in humanist design
[P230|Normal] User-centred design begins from the needs and experience of the user. Participatory design goes further, involving users in the design process. Co-creation and co-research extend participation to the generation of ideas and the production of knowledge (Sanders and Stappers, 2008; von Hippel, 2005). These traditions have improved design practice, and they have given users a voice that they did not have before.
[P231|Normal] They also carry humanist assumptions. The user is treated as a centre of intention and experience. The artefact is treated as a tool that the user employs. Participation is treated as a process of extracting knowledge from users and transferring it into a design. The designer remains the one who decides what counts as knowledge and what counts as a good design. The user is consulted, but the frame of the consultation is set elsewhere.
[P232|Normal] These assumptions are not wrong in every context. They are inadequate for a technology whose performance depends on the arrangement around it. If the problem is the water supply, the tenure arrangement or the maintenance contract, then consulting users about their preferences for a stove will not solve it. Participation has to extend to the elements that the design tradition treats as context.
[P233|Heading 3] 5.7.2 The end-user as a participant in an assemblage
[P234|Normal] A posthumanist reconstruction treats the end-user as a position within an assemblage rather than as a centre of intention. The end-user is the person whose practices, body, dwelling and relationships are enrolled in the technology. Their agency is real, but it is not the only agency at work. The artefact, the fuel, the infrastructure and the institution also act, and the outcome is produced by the relation.
[P235|Normal] This does not diminish the user. It makes the user's knowledge more important, not less. If the outcome depends on the relation, then the person who lives in the relation has access to knowledge that the designer does not. That knowledge is situated, embodied and practical. It is not always articulate, and it is not always consistent. It is nevertheless indispensable.
[P236|Heading 3] 5.7.3 What kind of knowledge do end-users hold?
[P237|Normal] The literature often asserts that users hold valuable knowledge without saying what kind. Five types are relevant here.
[P238|Normal] Tacit knowledge is knowledge that is embedded in practice and difficult to articulate. A cook knows when a pot is at the right temperature by sound and feel, not by a thermometer. A household knows how much paraffin to buy in a given week without calculating it.
[P239|Normal] Situated knowledge is knowledge produced from a particular position (Haraway, 1988). A tenant knows the landlord's rules in a way that an owner does not. A woman knows the risks of cooking in a shared kitchen in a way that a male researcher may not.
[P240|Normal] Embodied knowledge is knowledge held in the body. It includes the physical effort of carrying water, the heat of a stove, the smoke in the eyes and the weight of a pot. Hayles (1999) insists that this knowledge cannot be reduced to information.
[P241|Normal] Practice-based knowledge is knowledge of how a task is actually done, as distinct from how it is supposed to be done. It includes workarounds, repairs, substitutions and the informal rules that make a system work.
[P242|Normal] Cultural knowledge is knowledge of what a technology means. It includes the appropriateness of a fuel for a dish, the symbolism of a flame, the social meaning of cooking for guests and the norms that govern who cooks and when.
[P243|Normal] A design process that recognises these types of knowledge will use different methods to elicit them. Interviews are good for articulate knowledge. Observation and transects are good for tacit and practice-based knowledge. Diaries and photovoice are good for embodied and situated knowledge. Workshops are good for cultural knowledge, if they are organised in a way that allows disagreement.
[P244|Heading 3] 5.7.4 Culture as constitutive, not as a barrier
[P245|Normal] The earlier draft of this proposal treated culture as a barrier to acceptance. That framing is misleading. A cultural practice is not an obstacle to a technology; it is part of the standard against which the technology is judged. If a dish requires a visible flame, a technology that provides an invisible flame is not culturally neutral and technically adequate. It is inadequate for that dish. The question is not how to overcome the culture but how to design a technology that can be enrolled in it, or how to negotiate a change in practice that the household considers acceptable.
[P246|Normal] This does not mean that all practices should be accepted uncritically. Some practices are harmful, and some are constrained by poverty rather than by preference. The point is that the analysis must distinguish between a preference, a constraint and a compromise. A household that uses paraffin because it cannot afford electricity is not expressing a cultural preference. It is managing a constraint. A household that uses wood because the food tastes better is expressing a preference. The design response is different in each case.
[P247|Heading 3] 5.7.5 Participation and its failure conditions
[P248|Normal] Participation is not automatically good. The literature on participation identifies several failure modes, and a framework that ignores them will romanticise the community it studies.
[P249|Normal] Tokenism. Residents are invited but not heard. Their presence is used to legitimise a decision that has already been made (Arnstein, 1969).
[P250|Normal] Elite capture. Those with status, education or political connections dominate the process and direct the benefits towards themselves.
[P251|Normal] Expectation inflation. The project promises more than it can deliver, and the disappointment damages trust in future projects.
[P252|Normal] Consultation fatigue. Communities are asked to participate repeatedly in projects that do not endure. The cost of participation falls on those with the least time.
[P253|Normal] Framing power. The agenda, the language and the technical vocabulary are set by the project. Residents can respond within the frame but cannot change it.
[P254|Normal] Invisibility of labour. Participation is counted in meetings attended, not in the time and effort that residents contribute. The labour of participation is often borne by women.
[P255|Normal] Exit. The project ends, the funding stops, and the arrangements that were built around it collapse. Participation without a sustainability plan is a temporary relationship.
[P256|Normal] A posthumanist analysis adds a further failure mode: material exclusion. A resident may be willing to participate but unable to attend because the meeting is held at a time when she is cooking, or in a place that she cannot reach, or in a language she does not speak comfortably. The barrier is not attitudinal. It is material and institutional, and it can be designed against.
[P257|Heading 3] 5.7.6 Acceptance re-theorised
[P258|Normal] The study replaces the attitudinal construct of acceptance with a relational one. Acceptance is a pattern of sustained use that emerges when technology, infrastructure, household practice, cost and social norms align. It is not a decision made once. It is an achievement that has to be reproduced every day.
[P259|Normal] This definition has four implications. First, acceptance is observable in practice, not only in stated intention. A household that says it likes the system but does not use it has not accepted it. Second, acceptance is provisional. It can be lost when a part fails, a price rises or a relationship changes. Third, acceptance is distributed. It depends on the maintenance arrangement, the fuel supply and the institution as much as on the household. Fourth, acceptance is not the same as justice. A technology can be accepted because the household has no alternative, and that acceptance can coexist with exploitation.
[P260|Normal] The definition also makes acceptance measurable in a way that is useful for design. The study can observe use over time, record the points at which use declines, and trace the relations that changed. That is more demanding than a satisfaction survey, but it produces evidence that can be acted on.
[P261|Heading 2] 5.8 Posthumanism and the Systems Living Lab
[P262|Normal] A Living Lab is a real-world arrangement for co-creation. It brings users, researchers, firms and public bodies together in a setting where the innovation will be used, and it organises iterative cycles of design, prototyping and testing (Bergvall-Kåreborn and Ståhlbröst, 2009; Leminen et al., 2012). The literature distinguishes several types, including provider-driven, utilizer-driven, enabler-driven and user-driven labs. It also distinguishes labs that are primarily physical spaces from those that are primarily methods or networks (Mbatha and Musango, 2022).
[P263|Normal] Systems thinking adds three resources. The first is boundary critique. Checkland (1981) argues that the boundary of a system is a choice, and Midgley (2000) shows that the choice has political consequences. Who is inside the system, and who is in the environment, determines whose interests are considered. The second is feedback. Meadows (2008) shows that the behaviour of a system is produced by loops, delays and stocks, not by the intentions of its members. The third is emergence. Cilliers (1998) argues that complex systems have properties that cannot be predicted from their components, and that any model is a reduction.
[P264|Normal] A Living Lab becomes systemic, in this study, when three things are true. First, its boundary is chosen through an explicit critique rather than by convention. The lab does not assume that the project, the settlement or the municipality is the relevant system. It asks which boundary would include the relations that matter, and it records what is excluded. Second, its feedback architecture is designed. The lab collects data on the effects of its own interventions and uses them to revise the intervention. Third, its evaluation criteria are derived from the system's purpose, not from the project's outputs. It asks whether the arrangement became more just and more secure, not only whether the prototype worked.
[P265|Normal] There is a further, distinctly posthumanist point. A Living Lab is an apparatus in Barad's sense. It does not observe a phenomenon from outside; it participates in producing the phenomenon. The questions it asks, the instruments it uses, the people it includes and the data it records all enact a cut. The cut makes some things visible and others invisible. Reflexivity about the cut is therefore a condition of validity, not an optional virtue. A lab that presents its findings as neutral observations has misunderstood its own role.
[P266|Normal] This has practical consequences. It means that the lab must document its own decisions: why this site, why these participants, why this technology, why these indicators. It means that dissenting views must be recorded, not smoothed into consensus. It means that the lab must be accountable to the people it studies, not only to the funder. And it means that the evaluation must ask what the lab itself changed, including changes that were not intended.
[P267|Normal] The study derives six design principles for a systemic Living Lab from this argument. They are stated here and operationalised in Section 6.
[P268|Normal] Boundary transparency. The lab states whose interests are included and excluded, and revisits the boundary as the project develops.
[P269|Normal] Distributed participation. Participation is designed around the material conditions of residents' lives, including time, location, language, care responsibilities and safety.
[P270|Normal] Configuration as prototype. The lab prototypes the arrangement, including the artefact, the fuel supply, the maintenance arrangement, the tariff and the governance rules.
[P271|Normal] Reflexive apparatus. The lab records its own interventions and treats them as part of the data.
[P272|Normal] Justice as an outcome and a process. The lab evaluates distribution, procedure, recognition and restoration, and it applies the same criteria to its own operation.
[P273|Normal] Exit and durability. The lab plans for its own withdrawal from the start, including who will own, maintain and finance the arrangement after the project ends.
[P274|Normal] These principles are not a recipe. They are commitments that shape how the lab is organised, and they can be assessed.

  | RQ5 | Systems Living Lab; posthumanism; justice criteria | Provides criteria for judging whether the lab reconfigured the assemblage justly | Evaluation reduces to user satisfaction
[P288|Normal] For RQ1, the theory does more than provide a definition of a Living Lab. It supplies an account of what a system is, who decides its boundary and how inequality is reproduced through the relations that the boundary includes or excludes. Without that account, the question can be answered by a taxonomy of Living Lab types, which is descriptive but not explanatory.
[P289|Normal] For RQ2, the theory explains why participation is not simply a matter of inviting people. Participation is an effect of the relations that make it possible: time, transport, language, care responsibilities, tenure, trust and the distribution of authority. The theory directs attention to those relations and to the material arrangements that sustain or undermine them.
[P290|Normal] For RQ3, the theory explains why acceptance cannot be measured by asking people whether they like a technology. Acceptance is produced in the relation between the artefact, the fuel, the dwelling, the practice and the institution. The theory directs the study to observe use over time and to trace the points at which it changes.
[P291|Normal] For RQ4, the theory explains why a mechanism that works in one configuration may fail in another. It directs the design towards leverage points: the maintenance arrangement, the fuel supply, the tariff, the governance rule and the siting decision. It also insists that a mechanism is not just unless it can be shown to distribute benefits and burdens fairly.
[P292|Normal] For RQ5, the theory provides the criteria for evaluation. The lab is assessed not only on whether it produced a working prototype but on whether it changed the configuration in a way that increased justice and security. That is a higher bar, and it is the bar that the study sets.
[P293|Heading 2] 5.11 Justification for selecting posthumanism
[P294|Normal] Six criteria are used to select the theory. They are explanatory power, ontological fit, capacity to handle human and non-human elements, normative adequacy, empirical tractability and institutional fit.
[P295|Normal] Explanatory power. Posthumanism explains why identical technologies produce different outcomes in different households and streets. It explains why participation can fail even when it is well organised. It explains why material and infrastructural conditions matter. It explains why justice is not an add-on but a property of the arrangement.
[P296|Normal] Ontological fit. The research questions are about relations, not about individuals or variables. Posthumanism is the only candidate that takes the relation as its primary unit. TAM takes the individual. Diffusion theory takes the population. Practice theory takes the practice. Each captures something, but none captures the full configuration.
[P297|Normal] Human and non-human elements. The study has to hold people, artefacts, infrastructures, fuels, institutions and ecologies in one frame. Posthumanism does this without reducing one to the other. Systems thinking can also do it, but it tends to treat the elements as components of a functional whole rather than as participants with unequal power.
[P298|Normal] Normative adequacy. The study is about justice. Posthumanism supplies a normative vocabulary that is not reducible to efficiency. It is complemented by energy justice, which supplies the criteria. The combination is stronger than either alone.
[P299|Normal] Empirical tractability. Posthumanism is often criticised for being abstract. This study addresses the criticism by operationalising the concepts as observable relations. Intra-action becomes the relation between the artefact, the household and the infrastructure. Distributed agency becomes the set of actors that must be enrolled for the system to work. Situated knowledge becomes the knowledge held by residents and elicited through specific methods. The test of the framework is whether it can be used in the field, not whether it is elegant.
[P300|Normal] Institutional fit. The theory was introduced by the supervisor and is supported by the research programme in which the study is located. That is a reason to take it seriously, but it is not a sufficient reason to adopt it. The argument of this section is that the theory is defensible on its merits. If it were not, the institutional fit would be a reason to negotiate rather than a reason to proceed.
[P301|Normal] Several alternatives were considered.
[P302|Normal] TAM and UTAUT were rejected for the reasons set out in Section 5.9. They are useful for measuring attitudes but not for explaining configurations.
[P303|Normal] Diffusion of innovations explains how an innovation spreads through a population. It is useful for understanding adoption curves and the role of early adopters. It is less useful for understanding why a technology that has spread can be abandoned, or why the benefits of diffusion are unevenly distributed.
[P304|Normal] Social practice theory is close to this study in its attention to routine, materiality and convention (Shove, 2003; Stephenson et al., 2010). It is a valuable complement, and it informs the analysis of cooking and fuel stacking. Its limitation is that it is stronger on reproduction than on intervention. It explains why practices persist; it says less about how a design team might change them.
[P305|Normal] Actor-network theory is the closest alternative. It shares the commitment to symmetry between humans and non-humans, and it provides a rich empirical vocabulary for tracing associations. It was not adopted as the primary theory because it is deliberately indifferent to normative questions. The study needs a theory that can say something about justice, not only about association.
[P306|Normal] The capability approach (Nussbaum, 2011) provides a normative framework for evaluating wellbeing and freedom. It is compatible with the justice dimension of this study and could be used to specify what people are able to do. It does not, by itself, provide an account of technology or of material agency. It is therefore a possible complement rather than a foundation.
[P307|Normal] Political ecology and institutional theory explain the political and organisational conditions of energy transitions. They are important, and they inform the analysis of municipal capacity and vested interests. They operate at a different level from the present study, which is concerned with the design of a local arrangement.
[P308|Normal] Systems thinking alone was rejected because it can describe a configuration without asking whether the configuration is just. It is retained as a methodological complement, not as the foundation.
[P309|Normal] The verdict is that posthumanism is retained as the primary foundation because it is the only candidate that supplies an ontology adequate to the human–technology–infrastructure relation at the heart of the study. It is not sufficient on its own. The supporting perspectives supply what it lacks: systems thinking supplies the tools, energy justice supplies the criteria, Living Lab theory supplies the method and socio-technical theory supplies the wider transition context.
[P310|Normal] The theory also has limits that should be stated. It explains individual motivation poorly. It does not produce measurable acceptance scores. It offers limited guidance on policy design. It can be difficult to write clearly. These limits are real, and the study manages them by using mixed qualitative methods and by keeping the empirical claims modest.
[P311|Heading 2] 5.12 Synthesis: theoretical propositions
[P312|Normal] The framework can be stated as six propositions. They are not hypotheses in the statistical sense. They are claims that the empirical work will examine, qualify and, where necessary, revise.
[P313|Normal] P1. Acceptance is an emergent property of a socio-material configuration. It follows that interventions which change only the artefact will produce unstable acceptance. The observable implication is that acceptance will vary with water, space, fuel supply and maintenance arrangements, not only with attitudes.
[P314|Normal] P2. End-user agency is distributed across people, artefacts and institutions. It follows that participation mechanisms which address only human voice will fail to shift configurations. The observable implication is that projects which consult residents but do not change the material arrangement will not produce durable change.
[P315|Normal] P3. Justice outcomes depend on which actors and materials are admitted to the intra-action. It follows that the boundary of the project, who is in the room, what counts as evidence and which infrastructure is treated as fixed, is a justice decision. The observable implication is that projects with narrow boundaries will produce benefits that are captured by those inside the boundary.
[P316|Normal] P4. A Living Lab is an apparatus that produces the phenomena it studies. It follows that reflexivity about the lab's own cuts is a condition of validity. The observable implication is that a lab which does not document its own interventions will misattribute its findings to the community.
[P317|Normal] P5. Acceptance and justice are related but not identical. It follows that high acceptance can coexist with injustice. The observable implication is that a technology can be widely used and still distribute costs unfairly, for example when tenants pay more than owners or when women bear additional labour.
[P318|Normal] P6. Participation has material conditions. It follows that the failure of participation is often a failure of design rather than a lack of community interest. The observable implication is that attendance and engagement will vary with the time, place, language and care arrangements of the meeting.

[P322|Heading 2] 5.13 Chapter summary
[P323|Normal] This section has argued that energy interventions in informal settlements fail for reasons that are theoretical as well as technical. The dominant models treat acceptance as an individual decision about a finished artefact, and in doing so they exclude the relations that determine whether a technology works. Critical posthumanism supplies an alternative ontology. It treats entities as emerging through relations, agency as distributed, matter as generative and knowledge as situated. It is complemented by energy justice, which supplies normative criteria, by systems thinking, which supplies tools for mapping relations and feedback, and by Living Lab theory, which supplies a method for organising collaboration.
[P324|Normal] The framework has limits. It does not specify distributive criteria, it does not produce policy instruments and it can be difficult to operationalise. The study manages these limits by treating posthumanism as the foundation rather than the whole, by using energy justice as an operational complement and by stating its empirical claims in a form that can be examined.
[P325|Normal] The framework yields six propositions about acceptance, agency, boundary, apparatus, justice and participation. These propositions are the basis for the conceptual framework in Section 6, which turns them into components, relationships and indicators. The distinction between the two sections is deliberate. Section 5 explains why the study thinks the way it does. Section 6 shows what the study will look at.
[P326|Heading 1] 6. Conceptual Framework
[P327|Heading 2] 6.1 From theory to model
[P328|Normal] Section 5 ended with six propositions about acceptance, agency, boundary, apparatus, justice and participation. Those propositions are abstract. They say that acceptance is emergent, that agency is distributed, that boundaries have justice consequences, that the lab produces what it studies, that acceptance and justice are not the same thing, and that participation has material conditions. None of them tells a researcher what to do on the first day of fieldwork.
[P329|Normal] This section converts the propositions into a working model. The model is called SETILL: a Systemic Living Lab for Just Energy Transition. It has ten components, each of which names something the lab has to organise, decide or measure. The components are not stages. They run in parallel and they feed one another. The numbering is a convenience for reference, not a sequence.
[P330|Normal] The distinction between Section 5 and this section is worth restating, because it is the distinction between a theoretical framework and a conceptual framework. Section 5 justifies the ontology. This section builds the model that the ontology implies, with enough specificity that another researcher could apply it and enough openness that the case can surprise it. A model that cannot be surprised is not a research instrument; it is a description of the author's expectations.
[P331|Heading 2] 6.2 What the framework is and is not
[P332|Normal] The framework is a structured account of the elements that have to be aligned for a community-scale energy innovation to be taken up, sustained and judged fair in an informal settlement. It is intended to be used in three ways: as a design heuristic for the project team, as an analytical frame for the data, and as an evaluation instrument.
[P333|Normal] It is not a theory of everything. It does not explain national energy politics, it does not model the electricity market, and it does not predict adoption rates. It is deliberately local. Its claim is that the local configuration is where the fate of a decentralised technology is decided, and that the configuration can be described systematically without pretending to capture the whole system.
[P334|Normal] It is also not a checklist to be completed mechanically. Each component raises a question that has to be answered in the specific case. The answers will differ between a settlement with a market and one without, between a site with yard space and one without, between a community with an organised women's group and one where the only organised structure is the ward committee. The framework's contribution is the set of questions, not a set of answers.
[P335|Normal] Three design commitments follow from Section 5 and shape every component. The first is that the unit of analysis is the configuration, not the artefact and not the individual. The second is that justice is assessed throughout, not added at the end. The third is that the lab is part of what it studies, so its own operation has to be documented.

[P350|Heading 3] 6.4.1 C1 Boundary critique and stakeholder mapping
[P351|Normal] Every project draws a boundary. The boundary decides who is consulted, whose costs are counted and which infrastructure is treated as fixed. In most projects the boundary is drawn by convention: the funder defines the project area, the implementing organisation lists its partners, and the settlement appears as a beneficiary group. The boundary is rarely stated, and because it is not stated it cannot be questioned.
[P352|Normal] This component makes the boundary an explicit decision. The lab produces a boundary statement that names who is included, who is excluded and why. It includes a note on the actors who could affect the outcome but are not in the room: the landlord who controls the meter, the ward councillor who controls access to the site, the municipal department that owns the waste collection route, the supplier who stocks the spare part. The statement is revisited at each cycle, because the boundary that made sense at the start may not make sense once the fieldwork begins.
[P353|Normal] The component is derived from Checkland's (1981) argument that the system is a way of organising inquiry rather than an object in the world, and from Midgley's (2000) boundary critique, which asks whose interests are served by a given framing. It is also informed by Arnstein (1969), because the question of who is inside the boundary is inseparable from the question of who has power within it.
[P354|Normal] The practical test is simple. Can the research team produce a dated statement of the boundary, with reasons, and show that it changed at least once in response to something learned in the field? If not, the boundary was probably inherited rather than chosen.
[P355|Heading 3] 6.4.2 C2 Situated diagnosis
[P356|Normal] A diagnosis is an account of what the problem is. In energy projects the diagnosis is usually technical: the settlement lacks a reliable cooking fuel, the waste is not collected, the grid connection is inadequate. These statements may be true, but they are made from a position. A woman who cooks for eight people, a tenant who pays a fixed rent that includes electricity, a landlord who collects the rent, an artisan who repairs stoves and a municipal official who manages a landfill will describe the same settlement differently.
[P357|Normal] This component treats those differences as data. The lab collects accounts from multiple positions and uses them together, rather than selecting one account as the accurate description and treating the rest as perceptions. The methods are those that can capture practice and embodiment as well as opinion: transect walks, household energy diaries, photovoice, observation in kitchens and key-informant interviews.
[P358|Normal] The component draws on Haraway's (1988) argument that knowledge is always situated and that the view from nowhere conceals a position. It also draws on the South African literature on gendered energy research, which has shown that household energy practices are organised by gender and that a diagnosis which ignores this will mis-specify the problem (Musango et al., 2020; Anditi et al., 2022; Musango, 2022).
[P359|Normal] The test is whether the diagnosis contains accounts that differ by position, and whether those differences changed something in the design. A diagnosis that produces a single coherent story is suspicious. Settlements are not coherent.
[P360|Heading 3] 6.4.3 C3 Participation architecture
[P361|Normal] Participation is often organised as an event: a meeting, a workshop, a feedback session. The event is announced, people are invited, some attend, and the attendance is recorded as evidence of participation. This approach measures presence, not influence, and it systematically excludes those whose time is not their own.
[P362|Normal] This component treats participation as a design problem. It asks what would have to be true for a tenant who works during the day, a woman with small children, a person with a disability or an elderly resident to take part on equal terms. The answers are practical: meetings at times that fit the working day, venues within walking distance, childcare, translation into the languages people actually use, separate sessions where mixed groups would silence some voices, and payment for time where the project can afford it.
[P363|Normal] The component is derived from Arnstein's (1969) ladder, which distinguishes consultation from partnership and citizen control, and from the co-design literature, which insists that users are participants in the generation of ideas rather than sources of requirements (Sanders and Stappers, 2008). It also reflects the Living Lab literature's attention to the organisational form of collaboration (Leminen et al., 2012).
[P364|Normal] The test is whether attendance is broad and stable across the cycles, and whether the people who attend are able to change decisions. A participation architecture that produces good attendance and no change has failed.
[P365|Heading 3] 6.4.4 C4 Configuration design
[P366|Normal] The fourth component is the one that most directly distinguishes SETILL from a conventional Living Lab. In a conventional lab the artefact is the object of design and the context is the setting in which it is tested. Here the configuration is the object of design. The artefact is one element, and the design process has to specify the others: the fuel supply, the water source, the space, the maintenance arrangement, the spare-parts route, the skills, the tariff and the rules that govern use.
[P367|Normal] For a biogas system this means that the design specification includes the feedstock supply chain, the water budget, the siting decision, the gas storage, the stove type, the digestate disposal route, the maintenance schedule and the person responsible for each task. A specification that covers only the digester volume and the gas yield is incomplete, however technically sound it is.
[P368|Normal] The component rests on Barad's (2007) argument that properties emerge through intra-action, and on Bennett's (2010) account of thing-power, which insists that materials resist and surprise. It is also informed by practice theory, which shows that technologies are enrolled in routines that already exist (Shove, 2003; Stephenson et al., 2010).
[P369|Normal] The test is whether the specification names the non-technical requirements explicitly, and whether the prototype that is built is a prototype of the arrangement or only of the vessel.
[P370|Heading 3] 6.4.5 C5 Governance and ownership
[P371|Normal] A community-scale system needs a decision-making structure. Who sits on the management committee? How are members chosen, and can they be removed? Who owns the asset if the funder withdraws? Who authorises a repair, and who pays for it? Who resolves a dispute between two households over access?
[P372|Normal] These questions are often left to the end of a project, when they are answered by default: the asset is registered to a community organisation that may or may not be functional, the committee is composed of whoever was present at the final meeting, and the dispute route is a phone number that stops working when the project officer leaves.
[P373|Normal] This component makes those decisions explicit and early. It produces a written agreement, in the local language, that names the owner, the managing body, the decision rules, the tariff-setting principle and the dispute route. The agreement is not a legal contract in most cases, but it is a document that residents can describe and hold each other to.
[P374|Normal] The component draws on the commons literature on institutional design, particularly the principles that boundaries, rules and conflict-resolution mechanisms should be defined by the users themselves. It is also informed by von Wirth et al. (2019), who show that the durability of an urban Living Lab depends on its embedding in local institutions.
[P375|Normal] The test is whether residents can describe, in their own words, who owns the system and what happens when it breaks. If the answer is "the project", the component has not been completed.
[P376|Heading 3] 6.4.6 C6 Cost incidence and financing
[P377|Normal] Every intervention distributes cost. Some of the cost is financial: capital, operating expenses, fuel, spare parts. Some is in labour: the time spent collecting feedstock, carrying water, cleaning the digester, attending meetings. Some is in risk: the possibility that the system fails and the household has to fall back on a more expensive option.
[P378|Normal] This component traces the distribution. It asks who pays, in what form, at which point in the service chain, and with what consequence for the household budget. It disaggregates by tenure, income and gender, because the same nominal cost can be trivial for an owner and decisive for a tenant.
[P379|Normal] The component draws on the energy justice literature's attention to affordability and to the distribution of burdens (Sovacool and Dworkin, 2015; Walker and Day, 2012), and on the argument that unpaid labour is a cost even when no money changes hands.
[P380|Normal] The test is a cost profile by household type that includes unpaid labour. A project that reports a low cost per household without disaggregating has not answered the question.
[P381|Heading 3] 6.4.7 C7 Capability and local enterprise
[P382|Normal] A Living Lab that leaves behind a working system but no capacity to maintain it has not succeeded. This component treats the development of local capability as an outcome in its own right: skills in construction, operation, maintenance and repair; a local supplier of spare parts; a small enterprise that can service other installations; and a set of people who can train others.
[P383|Normal] The component is informed by Braidotti's (2013) distinction between potestas and potentia, which frames capacity-building as an increase in the community's power to act rather than as compensation for a deficit. It is also compatible with the capability approach, which evaluates arrangements by what people are able to do and be (Nussbaum, 2011).
[P384|Normal] The test is whether the skills persist after the project team leaves. A training workshop that produces a certificate but no practice has not built capability.
[P385|Heading 3] 6.4.8 C8 Justice evaluation
[P386|Normal] This component is the operational heart of the framework's normative claim. It takes the four dimensions of energy justice and turns each into a set of indicators with a data source and a point of comparison. The indicators are set out in Table 4.4 and discussed in Section 6.6.
[P387|Normal] The component is not a scoring exercise. It produces a profile that reports where the arrangement performs well and where it does not. The profile is shared with participants before it is finalised, and disagreements about it are recorded.
[P388|Heading 3] 6.4.9 C9 Reflexive monitoring
[P389|Normal] A Living Lab is an apparatus in Barad's (2007) sense: it does not observe a phenomenon from outside but participates in producing it. The questions it asks, the people it includes and the data it records all enact a cut. This component makes the cut visible.
[P390|Normal] In practice the lab keeps three records. The first is a decision log: what was decided, by whom, on what basis, and what alternatives were rejected. The second is an intervention register: every action the project took that could have affected the outcome, from the installation of the digester to the choice of a meeting venue. The third is a reflection record: the research team's notes on its own assumptions, surprises and discomforts.
[P391|Normal] These records serve two purposes. They allow the analysis to distinguish project effects from other changes in the settlement, and they allow the reader to assess the lab's own role in what it found. Without them, a finding about residents' preferences may be a finding about the questions the project asked.
[P392|Heading 3] 6.4.10 C10 Exit, durability and scaling
[P393|Normal] The final component plans for the end from the beginning. It asks what will still be working two years after the project closes, who will be maintaining it, how it will be financed, and what will happen to the relationships that were built.
[P394|Normal] An exit plan is not an abandonment plan. It specifies the handover: the asset, the records, the spare parts, the training materials and the contacts. It specifies the finance: a maintenance fund, a tariff, a municipal budget line or a combination. And it specifies the review: a date at which the arrangement will be assessed by the people who depend on it.
[P395|Normal] The component draws on von Wirth et al. (2019), who show that the impact of an urban Living Lab depends on how it is embedded in wider structures, and on Mbatha and Musango's (2022) observation that many energy Living Labs in Africa are not designed for continuation.
[P396|Normal] The test is a named owner, a budget line and a maintenance route that do not depend on the research team.
[P397|Heading 2] 6.5 The relationships between the components
[P398|Normal] The components are not independent. Four relationships matter for the analysis.
[P399|Normal] The first is a dependency: C4 depends on C2. A configuration cannot be designed without a diagnosis of what the configuration currently is. This seems obvious, but projects routinely design a technology before diagnosing the arrangement, and then discover during implementation that the water, space or maintenance conditions were never assessed.
[P400|Normal] The second is a tension: C3 and C10 pull against each other. Broad participation takes time, and the later cycles of a project are under pressure to close. The framework does not resolve the tension, but it requires the lab to record how it was managed.
[P401|Normal] The third is a feedback: C8 feeds C4. The justice evaluation is not only a final assessment; it produces findings that should change the design. If the distributional analysis shows that tenants bear a disproportionate cost, the design of the tariff or the siting has to respond.
[P402|Normal] The fourth is a precondition: C1 and C5 make the other components possible. A lab without a boundary statement does not know what it is assessing, and a lab without a governance agreement cannot hand anything over.
[P403|Normal] Table 4.3 maps the research questions onto the components and the evidence that will answer them. The mapping shows that no single question is answered by a single component. RQ2, for example, is answered mainly through C2 and C3, but also through C6, because cost is a condition of participation.

[P410|Normal] Two features of Table 4.4 deserve comment.
[P411|Normal] First, the indicators are mostly about relations, not about perceptions. "Decisions changed as a result of resident input" is a relational indicator: it requires a record of a decision and a record of the input. "Satisfaction with participation" is a perception indicator, and it can be high in a process that changed nothing. The framework prefers the former, although it collects the latter as well.
[P412|Normal] Second, the indicators can produce a mixed profile, and that is the point. A project might show good distributional outcomes, weak procedural influence and strong recognition. The report would say so. A framework that produces a single number invites the reader to ignore the dimensions that the number conceals.
[P413|Normal] The framework also applies the justice criteria to the lab itself. The lab consumes time, attention and space. It makes decisions about who is included. It distributes the benefits of participation unevenly, because those who attend more often get more of the project's attention. C9 exists to make this visible, and the justice profile in Chapter 8 includes a short assessment of the lab's own performance.
[P414|Heading 2] 6.7 How the framework will be used in the analysis
[P415|Normal] The framework does three jobs in the analysis, and it is worth being explicit about them because the risk of using a framework loosely is that it becomes a set of headings under which anything can be filed.
[P416|Normal] The first job is deductive. The ten components provide an initial coding frame for the interview and observational data. A passage about the timing of meetings is coded to C3. A passage about who owns the digester is coded to C5. This gives the analysis a starting structure and makes it comparable across data sources.
[P417|Normal] The second job is abductive. The framework is expected to be incomplete. Where the data do not fit a component, or where a component turns out to be unimportant in this case, that is a finding. The analysis records the misfits and uses them to revise the model. This is the moment at which the study can contribute to theory rather than merely apply it. Alvesson and Kärreman (2007) describe this as the construction of mystery: the empirical material becomes interesting when it resists the framework used to generate it.
[P418|Normal] The third job is evaluative. The justice criteria in Table 4.4 are applied at baseline and at follow-up, and the resulting profile is one of the study's main outputs. The profile is not a verdict on the community or on the project team. It is a description of how a particular arrangement distributed benefits, voice, recognition and repair at a particular time.
[P419|Normal] There is a risk in the third job that should be named. An evaluation conducted by the project team, about the project team's work, is vulnerable to self-serving interpretation. The study manages this in three ways: the criteria are specified in advance, the data include records that the team did not generate (diaries, observation by a research assistant not involved in delivery), and the draft profile is discussed with participants before it is finalised.
[P420|Heading 2] 6.8 Boundaries of the framework
[P421|Normal] The framework has four limits that should be stated.
[P422|Normal] First, it is designed for a community-scale, decentralised technology in a low-income urban settlement. It may not transfer to household-scale devices, to networked infrastructure or to rural settings without modification. The components would remain relevant, but their relative weight would change.
[P423|Normal] Second, it assumes a research team with a presence in the field over an extended period. A three-month consultancy could use the framework as a checklist, but it could not complete the cycles or observe the durability of the arrangement.
[P424|Normal] Third, it depends on access. A lab that cannot reach tenants, or that is confined to the households the municipality is willing to identify, will produce a partial picture. The boundary statement should record this.
[P425|Normal] Fourth, it is a framework for design and evaluation, not for causal inference. It can show that an arrangement changed and describe how, but it cannot isolate the contribution of the lab from other changes in the settlement. The design in Section 7 addresses this through the case study logic and through the reflexive record, not through statistical control.
[P426|Heading 2] 6.9 Chapter summary
[P427|Normal] This section has converted the theoretical propositions of Section 5 into a ten-component model. The components cover the frame (boundary, diagnosis, participation), the intervention (configuration, governance, cost, capability) and the assessment (justice, reflexivity, exit). Each component has a core question, a set of activities and a test of whether it is working. The justice dimension is operationalised in twelve indicators with data sources.
[P428|Normal] The framework is presented as an instrument that can be used, criticised and revised. Its value will be judged by whether it produces findings that a conventional technology-transfer approach would have missed, and by whether those findings are useful to the people who live in the settlement. That judgement cannot be made in a proposal. It can only be made after the fieldwork, and the design in Section 7 is intended to make it possible.
[P429|Heading 1] 7. Research Design and Methodology
[P430|Heading 2] 7.1 Research paradigm
[P431|Normal] A paradigm is a set of assumptions about what can be known and how. The study works within a critical posthumanist paradigm, and this has four consequences for the design.
[P432|Normal] The first is ontological. Reality is treated as relational: entities, including technologies and households, acquire their properties through the relations in which they are enrolled. This does not mean that the digester is not real. It means that what the digester is, for the purpose of the study, includes the water, the feedstock, the space, the labour and the rules that sustain it.
[P433|Normal] The second is epistemological. Knowledge is treated as situated and partial. The researcher does not stand outside the settlement and observe it. The researcher is part of the arrangement, and the knowledge produced is knowledge from a particular position. This is not a weakness to be corrected by more rigorous detachment. It is a condition to be documented.
[P434|Normal] The third is methodological. If the object of study is a configuration, then the methods have to capture relations rather than variables. That points toward qualitative methods that can follow actors, materials and practices, and toward analytical tools that can represent feedback and interdependence.
[P435|Normal] The fourth is axiological. The study is not neutral about justice. It asks whether the arrangement improved or worsened the position of the people who live in it, and it reports the answer even when the answer is unflattering.
[P436|Normal] These commitments place the study within the interpretive and critical traditions rather than the positivist one. That placement has implications for how quality is judged, which are addressed in Section 7.7. It does not imply a rejection of technical evidence. The study records gas yields, water use, cost and maintenance events, and it treats them as data about the configuration rather than as background.
[P437|Heading 2] 7.2 Research design
[P438|Normal] The study uses a single embedded case study, organised through the four stages of Design Research Methodology (DRM). The two are complementary rather than competing. DRM provides the internal logic of a design-oriented project: it specifies what has to be clarified, described, prescribed and evaluated. The case study provides the empirical logic: it specifies how evidence is collected from a bounded instance and how claims are generalised.
[P439|Normal] Figure 1.1 shows the design. The top band carries the two lenses that shape every stage. The four stage blocks are the DRM sequence, with the activities that belong to each. The orange line is the iterative return from evaluation to description and prescription, which is the point of a design study: the evaluation is not a final verdict but an input to the next revision. The bottom band locates the whole sequence within the case.

[P442|Heading 3] 7.2.1 Design Research Methodology
[P443|Normal] DRM was developed by Blessing and Chakrabarti (2009) for research in engineering design, and it has been widely used in engineering management. It distinguishes four stages.
[P444|Normal] Stage 1: Clarification of requirements. The researcher establishes what the problem is, what the current situation looks like, what the desired situation would be, and what criteria would show that an intervention had succeeded. In this study, Stage 1 covers the literature review, the theoretical framework, the conceptual framework, site scoping and the ethics application. The output is a set of requirements for the SETILL framework and for the biogas configuration, together with the criteria against which the pilot will be evaluated.
[P445|Normal] Stage 2: Descriptive study I. The researcher examines the current situation in depth, to understand why it is as it is. In this study, Stage 2 covers the key-informant interviews, the resident focus groups, the transect walks, the household energy diaries and the photovoice work. The output is an evidence-based account of how energy services are currently produced in the settlement, who does the work, what fails and why.
[P446|Normal] Stage 3: Prescriptive study. The researcher develops and specifies an intervention that is intended to move the situation toward the desired state. In this study, Stage 3 covers the co-design workshops, the specification of the SETILL model in its site-specific form, the specification of the biogas configuration and the construction of the prototype. The output is a documented design with explicit assumptions and explicit requirements.
[P447|Normal] Stage 4: Descriptive study II. The researcher evaluates the intervention against the criteria established in Stage 1, and revises the understanding of the problem in the light of the results. In this study, Stage 4 covers the pilot evaluation, the justice assessment, the revision of the framework and the feedback of findings to the community.
[P448|Normal] The stages are not a waterfall. Stage 4 feeds back to Stage 2 and Stage 3, and a finding in Stage 2 can send the researcher back to Stage 1. The design allows for two full cycles and a partial third, subject to time and access.
[P449|Heading 3] 7.2.2 Why a case study
[P450|Normal] A case study is appropriate when the research asks how or why a phenomenon works in its real-life context, when the researcher cannot manipulate behaviour, and when the boundaries between the phenomenon and its context are not clear (Yin, 2009). All three conditions hold here. The study asks how a configuration produces or undermines acceptance, it cannot control the conditions under which households use energy, and the context is not separable from the technology.
[P451|Normal] The case is embedded rather than holistic. Within the settlement there are four units of analysis: households, women's groups, the municipal service (waste, energy and planning functions) and the technology team (developers, artisans and researchers). The units are analysed separately and then brought together, because the configuration is produced at the intersection between them.
[P452|Normal] Flyvbjerg (2006) argues that the case study is undervalued in social science because of a mistaken belief that it cannot contribute to knowledge. His argument is that a well-chosen case can produce knowledge that is not available from statistical samples, particularly where the aim is to understand mechanisms rather than to estimate frequencies. That is the aim here. The study does not claim that its findings describe all South African informal settlements. It claims that the findings describe a configuration of a type that recurs, and that the framework can be tested elsewhere.
[P453|Normal] Analytical generalisation, in this sense, works through theory rather than through sampling. The reader asks whether the mechanism identified here would operate in another setting with similar conditions, and the study provides enough detail about the conditions for that judgement to be made.

[P470|Heading 3] 7.5.1 Interviews
[P471|Normal] Semi-structured interviews will be used with key informants and with a subset of households. The interview guide is organised around the ten components of the framework but does not follow them mechanically. It begins with the person's own account of how energy is obtained and used, then moves to the points at which it fails, then to the arrangements that surround it, and finally to questions of decision-making and fairness.
[P472|Normal] Interviews will be conducted in the language the participant prefers, with interpretation where the researcher is not fluent. Interviews will be audio-recorded with consent, transcribed, and where necessary translated with the translation checked by a second speaker. Translation is an analytical act and will be documented as such.
[P473|Heading 3] 7.5.2 Focus groups
[P474|Normal] Focus groups will be held separately for women, men, youth and tenants. The separation is deliberate. Mixed groups in this context tend to reproduce existing authority, with older men speaking first and at length. Separate groups allow other accounts to emerge. A final mixed session will be held to test whether the separate accounts survive when the groups are brought together.
[P475|Normal] The groups will be facilitated by a trained facilitator of the same gender where possible, with a note-taker. Discussions will be recorded with consent and transcribed.
[P476|Heading 3] 7.5.3 Transect walks
[P477|Normal] A transect walk is a structured walk through the settlement with a resident guide, recording what is observed and what the guide says about it. The method produces spatial evidence that interviews miss: where the water points are, how far the wood is, which plots have space for a digester, where the waste accumulates, which paths flood, and how the settlement's edges are policed.
[P478|Normal] Walks will be conducted at different times of day, because a settlement at six in the morning is not the same place as at six in the evening. Notes and photographs will be recorded with consent, and locations will be described in a way that does not identify individuals.
[P479|Heading 3] 7.5.4 Household energy diaries
[P480|Normal] Diaries capture what interviews miss: the ordinary sequence of a day. Participating households will be given a simple diary, in the local language, with pictograms for fuel types and a page for each day. A fieldworker will visit twice a week to support completion, because written diaries exclude participants with limited literacy.
[P481|Normal] The diary records what was cooked, on what, for how long, at what cost; when the electricity was off; what was used instead; who did the work; and what went wrong. The record is imperfect, and the analysis will treat it as an account produced with the researcher rather than as an objective log.
[P482|Heading 3] 7.5.5 Photovoice
[P483|Normal] Photovoice asks participants to photograph what matters to them and to explain the images in a group discussion. It shifts the framing from the researcher's questions to the participant's attention. The method is used here for two reasons: it produces evidence about material conditions that residents consider important rather than what the project considers important, and it creates a record that can be shown to the municipality.
[P484|Normal] Ethical care is required. Photographs can identify people and dwellings. Participants will be trained in consent for photography, will be asked not to photograph identifiable individuals without permission, and will retain control over which images are used in publications.
[P485|Heading 3] 7.5.6 Co-design workshops
[P486|Normal] Four workshops will be held in Stage 3. The first frames the problem and tests whether the diagnosis produced in Stage 2 is recognised by residents. The second develops the service model: who operates the system, who pays, who decides, what happens when it fails. The third specifies the technical configuration: size, siting, feedstock, water, stove, storage and spare parts. The fourth reviews the prototype and agrees the evaluation criteria.
[P487|Normal] The workshops will use visual and physical materials rather than text, because text-based workshops exclude participants who are not confident readers. Disagreement will be recorded rather than resolved by the facilitator, and minority positions will be carried into the analysis.
[P488|Heading 3] 7.5.7 Documents and secondary data
[P489|Normal] The study will also collect documents: municipal Integrated Development Plans, waste and energy policies, project records from previous interventions, equipment specifications and maintenance logs. These are used to establish the institutional conditions and to check accounts given in interviews.

[P494|Heading 3] 7.6.1 Reflexive thematic analysis
[P495|Normal] Thematic analysis is used in its reflexive form (Braun and Clarke, 2006, 2021). The approach treats themes as produced by the analyst's engagement with the data rather than as latent objects waiting to be found. Coding is both deductive, using the ten components, and inductive, allowing codes that the framework did not anticipate.
[P496|Normal] The six phases will be followed: familiarisation, initial coding, generating themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and writing. Coding will be conducted on the transcripts in the original language where possible, with translated extracts used for reporting. A second coder will code a subset of ten transcripts independently, and disagreements will be discussed and recorded. The purpose of the second coder is not to establish inter-rater reliability in a positivist sense but to test whether the coding frame is being applied consistently and to surface the analyst's assumptions.
[P497|Normal] Braun and Clarke (2021) insist that reflexivity is not a separate section appended to the method but a practice that runs through the analysis. The study will keep a reflexive journal, and extracts from it will be used in the methodology chapter to show how interpretations changed.
[P498|Heading 3] 7.6.2 Situational mapping
[P499|Normal] Situational mapping, developed within grounded theory by Clarke (2005), produces three kinds of map: situational maps of the major human and non-human elements and their relations; social worlds and arena maps of collective actors; and positional maps of the discursive positions taken on contested issues.
[P500|Normal] The method suits the study because it can represent non-human elements without reducing them to context. A map can show the digester, the water point, the meter, the landfill and the ward committee in the same frame, with the relations between them. Positional maps are particularly useful for the disagreements that the study expects: on whether the settlement should be formalised, on whether women should manage the system, on whether the municipality can be trusted.
[P501|Heading 3] 7.6.3 Causal loop diagramming
[P502|Normal] Causal loop diagrams represent feedback: how a change in one variable affects another, and how the effect returns. They will be used for two purposes. First, to represent the participants' own accounts of why systems succeed or fail, built with them in workshops. Second, to represent the researcher's synthesis of the evidence.
[P503|Normal] A diagram will be built for the settlement's current energy arrangements, and a second for the proposed configuration. The comparison is itself a finding: it shows which loops the intervention is intended to change and which it is likely to reinforce. A loop that reinforces exclusion, for example, may survive a technically successful installation.
[P504|Normal] The diagrams are not simulation models. They are qualitative representations, and they will be reported as such.
[P505|Heading 3] 7.6.4 Diffractive reading
[P506|Normal] Diffraction, in Barad's (2007) sense, is a practice of reading insights through one another rather than against one another. In this study it is used to compare data sets that describe the same phenomenon from different positions: a household diary and an official's account of the same service; a woman's description of cooking and a designer's specification of the stove.
[P507|Normal] The procedure is simple in principle and demanding in practice. The two accounts are set side by side, and the analysis asks what each makes visible, what each obscures, and what difference the difference makes. The output is not a synthesis that resolves the disagreement but an account of how the phenomenon is produced differently in different apparatuses. That account is directly relevant to RQ3, because it shows that acceptance is not a single fact about a single object.
[P508|Heading 3] 7.6.5 From analysis to framework revision
[P509|Normal] The analysis is not only a description of the case. It is a test of the framework. Three outcomes are possible for each component. The component may be confirmed as important and adequately specified. It may be confirmed as important but under-specified, in which case the specification is revised. Or it may turn out to be unimportant in this case, in which case the framework is revised to say so.
[P510|Normal] The revision will be documented in a table that shows the original component, the evidence, and the revised version. That table is one of the study's contributions, because it shows what a framework looks like after it has been used.

[P515|Normal] Three strategies deserve emphasis. The first is negative case analysis: the study will actively seek cases that contradict the emerging argument, and will report them. A household that uses the system successfully for reasons the framework did not anticipate is as important as one that abandons it.
[P516|Normal] The second is the audit trail. All coding decisions, sampling decisions and analytical memos will be retained and indexed, so that an examiner can trace how a claim was produced.
[P517|Normal] The third is the treatment of the researcher's position. The researcher is a doctoral candidate at a South African university, working with a supervisor whose work informs the framework, in a settlement where the researcher is an outsider. That position shapes access, interpretation and what participants are willing to say. It will be described in the thesis rather than concealed.

The account will describe poverty, failure and conflict. Participants have a legitimate interest in how they are represented, and the study will share draft findings with them and record their responses.
[P526|Normal] The third is the ethics of the framework itself. A posthumanist framework that treats people as elements of an assemblage risks describing them in a way that diminishes their agency. The writing will guard against this by keeping human voices in the text and by avoiding a vocabulary that turns suffering into ontology.

[P532|Heading 2] 7.10 Limitations of the design
[P533|Normal] The design has four limitations that should be stated in advance.
[P534|Normal] First, a single case cannot support claims about the prevalence of a configuration. The study will produce an account of how a mechanism worked in one place. Readers may judge whether it applies elsewhere, but the study will not make that judgement for them.
[P535|Normal] Second, the researcher's presence changes the configuration. A settlement that is being studied is not the same as one that is not. The reflexive record addresses this but does not eliminate it.
[P536|Normal] Third, the study depends on a technology that may not perform as intended. Biogas is sensitive to feedstock, water and temperature. If the system underperforms, the study will report that, and the analysis will treat the underperformance as evidence about the configuration rather than as a failed experiment.
[P537|Normal] Fourth, the timeframe is three years, and the durability of the arrangement can only be assessed within that window. A follow-up beyond the doctoral period would be valuable, and the exit plan will include arrangements for one.
[P538|Heading 2] 7.11 Summary of the methodology
[P539|Normal] The study is a single embedded case study conducted through four DRM stages in an informal settlement in Greater Tzaneen. Data are collected through interviews, focus groups, transect walks, diaries, photovoice, co-design workshops and documents, and analysed through reflexive thematic analysis, situational mapping, causal loop diagramming and diffractive reading. Quality is addressed through the eight big-tent criteria and a documented audit trail. Ethics are addressed through procedural clearance and through a relational practice that includes an exit plan and shared findings. The next section describes the case site.
[P540|Heading 1] 8. Context and Case Study
[P541|Heading 2] 8.1 Why Tzaneen
[P542|Normal] The choice of Greater Tzaneen is not arbitrary, and it is not a convenience sample in the ordinary sense. The municipality combines the conditions that the study needs: a large population with high levels of service connection and low levels of service quality; an economy built on agriculture and agro-processing, with the waste streams that follow; a climate that is warm enough for anaerobic digestion to be feasible; and a municipal administration that is under capacity but not absent.
[P543|Normal] It is also a place where the contradictions of the South African transition are visible in a small area. The town has a formal economy, a hospital, schools and a retail sector. Around it are settlements with different degrees of formality, different tenure arrangements and different access to services. The distance between them is a few kilometres and several decades of investment.
[P544|Normal] Finally, the municipality is in Limpopo, a province that receives less attention in the energy transition literature than Gauteng or the Western Cape, despite having a large population and significant renewable potential. A study located there adds to a thin evidence base.
[P545|Heading 2] 8.2 The municipality
[P546|Normal] Greater Tzaneen Local Municipality (LIM333) is one of four local municipalities in the Mopani District. It covers approximately 2,896 square kilometres and had a population of 478,254 at the 2022 census, up from 389,623 in 2011, a growth rate of about two per cent a year (Statistics South Africa, 2023). There are roughly 129,600 households, with an average size of 3.7 people. About half are headed by women. Just over 16 per cent of adults have no schooling, and under nine per cent have a higher education qualification.
[P547|Normal] Table 6.1 sets out the indicators that matter for this study.

[P550|Normal] The contrast between the electricity figure and the water, sanitation and refuse figures is the point of the table. Almost every household is connected for lighting. Fewer than a third have water inside the dwelling, and about a quarter have refuse collected. A household in this municipality is likely to be connected to the grid and to manage its own water, waste and cooking fuel. That combination is exactly the configuration the study is about.
[P551|Normal] Two caveats are necessary. The first concerns the dwelling classification. A figure of 97.1 per cent formal dwellings is difficult to reconcile with the visible presence of informal settlements in and around Tzaneen. The census category counts a structure as formal if it is a house or a flat on a formal erf, and backyard rooms and rented shacks on a formal plot may be counted within the formal dwelling. The statistic therefore understates the number of households living in informal or insecure arrangements. The study will not rely on it, and will establish tenure arrangements through its own scoping.
[P552|Normal] The second concerns the electricity figure. "Electricity for lighting" records whether a household uses electricity for lighting, not whether it has a legal connection, a reliable supply or enough capacity to cook. A household with an informal connection from a neighbour's meter is counted as using electricity. The indicator measures presence, not service.
[P553|Heading 2] 8.3 Energy in the municipality
[P554|Normal] The municipality's energy profile is shaped by three factors.
[P555|Normal] The first is the national grid. Distribution in the area is carried out by Eskom rather than by a municipal distributor, which means that outages, tariff decisions and connection processes are administered at a distance. Municipal officials can advocate but cannot decide.
[P556|Normal] The second is the local economy. Tzaneen is an agricultural service town. Agro-processing generates organic waste: fruit waste, sawdust, poultry litter, abattoir waste and market waste. Some of it is already used, for animal feed or composting. Some is dumped. A digester would compete for a fraction of this stream, and the competition is part of what the study has to understand.
[P557|Normal] The third is the pattern of household fuel use. As in much of Limpopo, households combine electricity with wood, paraffin and LPG. Wood is often collected rather than bought, particularly in peri-urban areas where there is access to trees. Paraffin is bought in small quantities and is expensive per unit of energy. LPG is used where it is available and affordable. The combination changes with the season, the price and the reliability of supply.
[P558|Normal] The study will not attempt to quantify the municipal energy balance. It will document the household-level configuration and the institutional conditions that shape it.
[P559|Heading 2] 8.4 Waste and water
[P560|Normal] Biogas depends on a feedstock stream and a water supply, and both are constrained in the municipality.
[P561|Normal] Refuse removal reaches about a quarter of households. The rest manage waste on site, burn it, bury it or dump it. Organic waste is therefore dispersed rather than concentrated, which is a problem for a digester that needs a steady supply. The design response is either to aggregate waste from a small number of sources (a market, a poultry operation, a group of households) or to size the digester to the supply that can be guaranteed. The choice between these is a design decision that the co-design workshops will address.
[P562|Normal] Water is supplied through a network that reaches most households, but inside connections are limited and supply is intermittent in some areas. A digester needs water in a ratio to feedstock, and the ratio competes with cooking, cleaning and drinking. In a settlement where water is carried, a technology that needs several hundred litres a week is a different proposition from one that needs fifty. The study will measure water use and will treat it as a design constraint rather than an operating detail.
[P563|Heading 2] 8.5 The settlement
[P564|Normal] The specific settlement has not been selected. This is deliberate, and it should be read as a statement about the state of the work rather than as an omission.
[P565|Normal] The scoping phase in months one to six will assess candidate settlements against the criteria in Table 5.1. The assessment will include a site visit, conversations with community structures and a meeting with the municipality. The outcome will be documented in a short site selection memorandum, appended to the thesis, that states which criteria each candidate met and why the chosen site was preferred.
[P566|Normal] What can be said now is what the study expects to find, based on the literature and on preliminary conversations. The settlement is likely to be characterised by a mix of owner-occupied and rented dwellings, an electricity supply that is connected but unreliable and costly, a reliance on multiple cooking fuels, organic waste that is not collected, and water that is available at a shared point. It is likely to have at least one organised structure, whether a residents' committee, a women's group or a church-based organisation, and it is likely to have experience of previous projects that shapes how a new one is received. That last point matters. A settlement that has been promised services before will not take a new promise at face value, and the study should not expect it to.
[P567|Heading 2] 8.6 Relevance to Engineering Management
[P568|Normal] The case is an Engineering Management case, not only an energy case, and the connection should be made explicit.
[P569|Normal] Engineering Management is concerned with the design, operation and improvement of systems that combine technical and organisational elements. The Tzaneen case is exactly such a system. A community-scale digester is a technical system with a supply chain, a maintenance regime, a cost structure, a governance arrangement and a set of users whose practices determine whether it works. The decisions that determine its fate are management decisions: siting, sizing, contracting, staffing, pricing, training, scheduling and risk allocation.
[P570|Normal] The study contributes to the field in three ways. It provides a method for specifying the non-technical requirements of a technical system in a low-income context. It provides an evaluation approach that treats justice as a system performance criterion rather than as an external constraint. And it provides evidence about the organisational conditions under which a decentralised energy system is sustained.
[P571|Normal] These contributions are relevant beyond biogas. The same questions arise for solar water heating, for container-based sanitation, for water ATMs and for small-scale PV. The framework is intended to be portable across these technologies, and the case is intended to show how.
[P572|Heading 2] 8.7 Access, permissions and relationships
[P573|Normal] Access will be negotiated in three steps.
[P574|Normal] The first is institutional. The study will seek written permission from the municipality to conduct research in the area, and will engage the relevant departments (waste management, electricity, planning and local economic development) as participants rather than as gatekeepers. The engagement will include a commitment to share findings.
[P575|Normal] The second is community-level. The study will approach the recognised community structures, explain the purpose and the limits of the project, and ask for permission to work in the area. It will not offer benefits in exchange for access. Where a structure has concerns about the project, those concerns will be recorded and addressed before fieldwork begins.
[P576|Normal] The third is household-level. Participation will be sought individually, with a clear statement that refusal has no consequence for service entitlement. Participants will be able to withdraw at any point, and data already collected will be removed on request where that is practicable.
[P577|Normal] The study will also establish a small advisory group of residents, municipal officials and a technical expert, which will meet twice a year. The group is not a decision-making body, but it provides a channel for critique that does not depend on the researcher's judgement.
[P578|Heading 2] 8.8 Summary of the case
[P579|Normal] Greater Tzaneen offers a setting in which nearly every household is connected to the grid and fewer than a third have water inside the dwelling, where refuse collection reaches a quarter of households and organic waste is dispersed, and where a large share of households are headed by women and depend on multiple fuels for cooking. Those conditions make the configuration, rather than the artefact, the central problem.
[P580|Normal] The settlement will be selected during scoping. The study will document the selection, describe the site in detail, and treat the description as part of the evidence rather than as background. Section 9 states what the study expects to contribute.

[P587|Heading 2] 9.2 Theoretical contribution
[P588|Normal] The theoretical contribution is the argument that acceptance is an emergent property of a socio-material configuration, and that this claim has consequences for how energy innovation should be designed and evaluated.
[P589|Normal] The claim is not new in its general form. Practice theory, socio-technical systems theory and actor-network theory have all argued that technology and social order are co-produced. What is new is the specific combination: a posthumanist ontology applied to the design of a community-scale energy system in a South African informal settlement, with the justice literature supplying the normative criteria. The combination produces a set of propositions that can be examined and, importantly, can fail.
[P590|Normal] The test of the contribution is whether the framework explains something that a TAM study would have missed. The study expects to find at least three such things. First, cases in which a household reports a positive attitude and stops using the system, because a material condition changed: the water supply failed, a part could not be sourced, a landlord raised the rent. Second, cases in which a household reports a negative attitude and continues to use the system, because there is no alternative. Third, cases in which the same technology produces different outcomes in adjacent households with similar incomes, because the configuration differs in a way that an attitude survey cannot detect.
[P591|Normal] If none of these is found, the contribution is weakened. The study will report that.
[P592|Heading 2] 9.3 Methodological contribution
[P593|Normal] The methodological contribution is the combination of four analytical practices and the documentation of what each contributes.
[P594|Normal] Reflexive thematic analysis is well established, but it is usually used alone. Situational mapping is used in grounded theory but rarely in energy research. Causal loop diagramming is used in systems research but rarely with qualitative community data. Diffractive reading is used in posthumanist research but rarely in engineering. The study will report how the four were combined, what each produced, and where they produced incompatible accounts.
[P595|Normal] The combination is not offered as a template to be copied. It is offered as a documented experiment in method, with an honest account of its costs. Diffractive reading, in particular, is slow and difficult to explain to a reader who is not familiar with the vocabulary. The study will show what it added and what it cost.
[P596|Normal] The second methodological contribution is the operationalisation of justice. The energy justice literature is rich in principles and thin in indicators. The twelve indicators in Table 4.4 are an attempt to close that gap for a project-scale intervention. They are not the only possible set, and the study will report which ones proved usable and which did not.
[P597|Heading 2] 9.4 Practical contribution
[P598|Normal] For practitioners, the study offers a design protocol and a body of evidence.
[P599|Normal] The protocol treats the configuration as the unit of intervention. In practice this means that a project team working on a biogas system would specify the feedstock supply, the water budget, the siting, the maintenance arrangement, the spare-parts route and the governance rules at the same time as the digester volume and the gas yield. The study will produce a template for that specification, derived from the case and tested against it.
[P600|Normal] The evidence concerns the conditions under which a community-scale biogas system is sustained. The literature contains many accounts of why systems fail and few longitudinal accounts of how a system was maintained, by whom, at what cost and with what problems. The study will produce such an account for one case, with the maintenance record and the cost data.
[P601|Normal] These outputs are modest, but they are the kind that a project manager can use. A framework that is elegant but unusable is of limited value in engineering management.
[P602|Heading 2] 9.5 Policy contribution
[P603|Normal] At the policy level, the study speaks to two questions that municipalities currently answer by default.
[P604|Normal] The first is how to evaluate an energy programme against justice criteria. Municipal Integrated Development Plans routinely state commitments to redress and inclusion, and they rarely specify how those commitments will be assessed. The indicator set in Table 4.4 offers a way of doing so at project scale, with data that a municipality could collect.
[P605|Normal] The second is how to organise participation so that it is more than consultation. The study will document the arrangements that made participation possible for tenants, women and youth, and the arrangements that did not. That evidence is directly relevant to the design of municipal engagement processes, and to the conditions attached to Just Energy Transition Partnership funding.
[P606|Normal] The study does not claim that its findings will change national policy. It claims that they provide evidence that is currently missing from a policy conversation that is conducted mostly at national scale.
[P607|Heading 2] 9.6 Contribution to the supervisor's research programme
[P608|Normal] The study is located within a research programme on gendered energy innovation in poor urban environments, led by the supervisor and colleagues. That programme has produced a gender analysis framework (Anditi et al., 2022), a systems model of energy service security (Batinge et al., 2022), a co-design toolkit (Petrulaityte et al., 2022) and a process for developing energy Living Labs (Mukama et al., 2022). It has also produced a posthumanist argument for a social approach to energy transition in engineering education (Aboalez, 2025).
[P609|Normal] This study extends that programme in three ways. It moves the posthumanist argument from engineering education to settlement-level design. It adds a justice evaluation that the earlier work identified as necessary but did not operationalise. And it provides a detailed case that can be used to test the programme's frameworks in a new site.
[P610|Normal] The relationship is a strength and a risk. It is a strength because the study can build on established concepts and access. It is a risk because a study that is too closely aligned with its supervisor's framework may not test it hard enough. The design addresses this through negative case analysis, through the inclusion of a critical friend on the advisory group, and through the explicit statement of what would count as disconfirming evidence.
[P611|Heading 2] 9.7 Contribution to the researcher's development
[P612|Normal] A doctoral study is also a process of formation, and it is reasonable to state what the researcher expects to learn. The proposal is written by a candidate who is developing competence in three areas: qualitative research in a context of poverty and inequality; the application of posthumanist theory to engineering problems; and the management of a multi-stakeholder design process.
[P613|Normal] The study is designed to build all three. The fieldwork requires the researcher to work in a language and a setting that are not their own, with the humility that entails. The theoretical work requires a facility with concepts that are not native to engineering. The design work requires the management skills that the study is, in part, about.
[P614|Normal] None of this belongs in a claim about contribution to knowledge. It belongs in the statement of what the study is for, and it is stated here because it shapes the choices in Section 10.

[P625|Heading 3] 10.2.1 Phase 1: Foundation and ethics (months 1–6)

[P630|Heading 3] 10.2.2 Phase 2: Descriptive study I (months 7–14)
[P631|Normal] The second phase produces the diagnostic account. It begins with key-informant interviews, which establish the institutional context and identify the households and groups to approach. Focus groups follow, then transect walks, then diaries, then photovoice. The sequence is not rigid, but it is deliberate: the earlier methods inform the later ones.
[P632|Normal] Twelve to fifteen households will keep diaries for four weeks. Ten to twelve residents will take part in photovoice. Twenty to twenty-five household interviews and eight to ten interviews with non-users will be conducted. The exact numbers will be adjusted as saturation is assessed.

[P635|Heading 3] 10.2.3 Phase 3: Analysis and modelling (months 15–21)
[P636|Normal] The third phase consolidates the analysis. It produces the situational maps, the causal loop diagrams and the first revision of the framework. It also produces a draft of the findings chapter, which is submitted to the supervisor for review.
[P637|Normal] This phase includes a member-checking session with participants, in which the emerging findings are presented and discussed. The session is not a validation exercise in the positivist sense; it is an opportunity for participants to disagree, and the disagreements are recorded.
[P638|Normal] The deliverables are a findings chapter draft, a set of maps and diagrams, and a revised framework.
[P639|Heading 3] 10.2.4 Phase 4: Co-design and prototyping (months 22–27)
[P640|Normal] The fourth phase is the design intervention. Four workshops produce the service model, the governance agreement, the technical specification and the evaluation plan. Construction begins after the specification is agreed, and is undertaken by a local contractor with the technology team, using local labour where possible.
[P641|Normal] Commissioning includes operator training and a maintenance schedule. The phase ends with a working prototype, a signed governance agreement and a trained operator group.
[P642|Normal] This phase carries the highest risk in the plan. Construction can be delayed by weather, by supply, by permission and by disagreement. A two-month contingency is built into the schedule.
[P643|Normal] The deliverables are an agreed specification, a working prototype, a governance agreement and a trained operator group.
[P644|Heading 3] 10.2.5 Phase 5: Evaluation and drafting (months 28–33)
[P645|Normal] The fifth phase assesses the intervention and writes the thesis. Monitoring data are collected over six months: gas output, water use, feedstock input, maintenance events, cost and use by household. Follow-up interviews are conducted with the original participants and with a sample of households that did not take part.
[P646|Normal] The justice profile is constructed and discussed with participants before it is finalised. The thesis is drafted, and a paper is prepared for a conference or journal.
[P647|Normal] The deliverables are a justice profile, a full thesis draft and a conference paper.
[P648|Heading 3] 10.2.6 Phase 6: Finalisation (months 34–36)
[P649|Normal] The final phase completes the thesis, prepares the examination copy and submits. It also includes the exit handover: a community feedback session, the transfer of records and contacts, and the establishment of the maintenance arrangement agreed in Phase 4.
[P650|Normal] The deliverables are a submitted thesis and a completed handover.
[P651|Heading 2] 10.3 Milestones and decision points
[P652|Normal] Five decision points are built into the plan. Each is a point at which the study could be adjusted.
[P653|Normal] Month 6: site confirmation. If no site meets the criteria, the study will either relax a criterion with documented reasons or delay the fieldwork by three months while a new site is scoped.
[P654|Normal] Month 14: framework revision. If the diagnostic evidence does not support the framework, the framework is revised before the co-design phase. This is the point at which a major revision is cheapest.
[P655|Normal] Month 22: go/no-go on construction. Construction proceeds only if a governance agreement and a maintenance arrangement are in place. If they are not, the study will run a design-only intervention and report the reasons, which is itself a finding about the conditions for community-scale technology.
[P656|Normal] Month 27: prototype acceptance. If the system does not perform to specification, the study will document the shortfall and analyse it as evidence about the configuration.
[P657|Normal] Month 33: submission readiness. If the draft is incomplete, a three-month extension will be requested.

[P674|Heading 2] 11.2 Notes on the chapters
[P675|Normal] Three chapters carry the weight of the argument and deserve comment.
[P676|Normal] Chapter 6 is the diagnostic chapter, and it does the work that the literature review cannot: it establishes what the configuration actually is in this place. It will be organised around the ten components, but it will not follow them mechanically. It will begin with a day in the settlement, then move to the systems that produce the services, then to the points of failure, then to the relations of power that shape who bears the cost.
[P677|Normal] Chapter 8 is the evaluation chapter, and it is the one most likely to produce uncomfortable findings. It will report the justice profile in full, including the dimensions on which the intervention performed poorly. It will also report the lab's own performance against the same criteria.
[P678|Normal] Chapter 9 is the discussion, and it is where the theoretical contribution is made. It will return to the six propositions and state which were supported, which were qualified and which were not supported. It will also discuss what the case shows about the limits of the framework.

[P815|Heading 2] Appendix E: Workshop protocol
[P816|Normal] Each workshop runs for three hours and follows the same structure.
[P817|Normal] Welcome and purpose (15 minutes). Restate what the workshop can and cannot decide.
[P818|Normal] Ground rules (10 minutes). Agreed by the group, including how disagreement will be handled.
[P819|Normal] Evidence presentation (30 minutes). Findings from the diagnostic phase, presented visually, with time for challenge.
[P820|Normal] Small-group task (60 minutes). Groups of five to seven, mixed by gender and tenure where possible, each with a facilitator and a visual template.
[P821|Normal] Plenary (45 minutes). Each group presents; disagreements are recorded on a flip chart rather than resolved.
[P822|Normal] Decision record (20 minutes). What was decided, what was deferred, who will do what.
[P823|Normal] A decision log is completed after each workshop. It records the decision, the alternatives considered, who supported and who opposed, and the reasons given. The log is a primary data source for C9 and for the procedural justice indicators.
