## 2. D-01 — Aboalez (2025), *A Posthumanist Social Approach to Energy Transition*

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| Author | Khaled Aboalez (Postgraduate School of Engineering Management, University of Johannesburg, APK Bunting Road Campus) |
| Venue | Procedia CIRP 135 (2025) 8–13 — 32nd CIRP Conference on Life Cycle Engineering (LCE 2025) |
| DOI | `10.1016/j.procir.2025.01.042` (printed on p. 8) |
| Licence | CC BY-NC-ND 4.0, open access |
| Keywords | Energy Transition; Renewable Energy; Engineering Education; Social Justice; Critical Post-Humanism |

**Why this document is decisive.** It is the only place in the workspace where the supervisor's theoretical position is stated explicitly, and it anticipates most of the architecture the client has now been asked to build. It is therefore treated as the primary interpretive key for Chapter 3.

**Content actually in the paper:**

1. **Problem framing.** Engineering is argued to have been constituted "largely in opposition to the values and practices of social justice" (citing Schneider), and engineering practice is described as adopting an "ideology of depoliticisation of technology" in which cultural and social values are treated as political and therefore irrelevant to technical work.
2. **Definitional work.** Social justice in engineering is defined through Leydens, Lucena and Schneider (2012, p. 63) and Leydens, Lucena and Nieusma (2014, p. 1).
3. **The posthumanist turn.** Critical posthumanism is presented as an alternative to humanism (Braidotti 2013), with a specific conceptual shift from "interaction" between pre-existing entities to Barad's "intra-action" (Barad 2007, p. 139).
4. **Key theoretical principles of critical posthumanism (§5.1).** Overcoming humanist anthropocentrism; attending to nonhuman others; affirming difference; rejecting false universalisms; anti-racism; openness to others; ethical accountability, sustainability and intergenerational solidarity.
5. **Justice.** Two dimensions are borrowed from Gewirtz (1998) — *distributional* and *relational* justice — and then extended: "social justice, as seen through the lens of posthumanism, will consider human and nonhuman actors, where the human/nonhuman hierarchy flattens" (citing Rose & Walton 2018). Ulmer (2017, p. 2) is used to argue that justice is "also material, ecological, geographical, geological, geopolitical, and geo-philosophical".
6. **Methodological instrument.** *Posthumanist cartography* (after Braidotti 2006) is proposed as an analytical and normative tool for mapping power relations in energy project assemblages, distinguishing *potestas* (hindering power) from *potentia* (enabling power).
7. **Pedagogy.** A "critical posthuman pedagogy" / "cyborg pedagogy" (Gough 2004) is proposed for engineering education.

**Reference base actually cited in D-01 (44 numbered references).** Braidotti (2006, 2011, 2013, 2019); Barad (2007); Forlano (2017); Ulmer (2017); Ellis (2018); Gewirtz (1998); Rawls (1999); Bozalek & Carolissen (2012); Radomska (2010); Van der Tuin & Dolphijn (2012); Åsberg, Koobak & Johnson (2011); Postma (2016, *SAJHE*); Motala (2017); Gough (2004); Bell & Russell (2000); Razack (2009); Leydens & Lucena (2018); Riley (2008, 2012); Lucena (2013); Herkert (2005); Nieusma & Riley (2010); Wüstenhagen, Wolsink & Bürer (2007); Zárate-Toledo, Patiño & Fraga (2019); Köhl?/other energy sources.

**Limitations to be aware of when building Chapter 3 on it:**
- It is a six-page conference paper, not a theoretical treatise; it states principles but does not develop an ontology, epistemology or axiology at the depth a doctoral chapter requires.
- Haraway and Hayles are **absent**. The supervisor's paper rests on Braidotti and Barad only. The client's brief requires all four thinkers, so Chapter 3 must extend beyond the supervisor's text — which is legitimate, but the extension must stay compatible with the Braidottian/Baradian frame the supervisor actually uses.
- The paper's own use of "posthumanism" is prescriptive/normative (what engineers *ought* to do) more than analytical (what the socio-material world *is*). Chapter 3 will need to supply the missing ontological and epistemological argument.
- It is engineering-education focused; the transfer to informal-settlement energy innovation is asserted, not demonstrated.

### 3.4 Substantive content of each block

**Block B — Chapter 1 (pp. 1–20).** Establishes energy poverty in African informal settlements as "gendered and persistently understudied" (citing Musango et al. 2020; Anditi et al. 2022). Introduces three borrowed frameworks that the whole thesis depends on:

- **SRM** — Systems Reinforcing Model for Energy Access Sustainability (Okoye 2020), with four system interconnections; only rows 1 and 2 are used.
- **EC** — Energy Cultures framework (Stephenson et al. 2010), material culture / cognitive norms / energy practices.
- **SEA** — Sustainable Energy Access framework (Shrestha & Acharya 2015).

It then defines the novel contribution as **SETILL** — Systems Energy Technology Innovation Living Lab — "with gender design". The problem statement is explicitly gendered: "the lack of greater gender representativity in the design of energy innovations/systems which could reduce the issue of justice, reduce ... the agency for women as economic actors, thereby leading to lack of security of energy services".

**Block C — Chapter 2 (pp. 21–116).** A three-level review (WHY / HOW / WHAT, after Onyeagoziri 2020):
- *Level 1 — parent discipline:* gendered energy technology innovation and energy security; historical context; innovation diffusion (Bass model); energy insecurity; gender-focused case studies; energy poverty indicators (MEPI, MuSIASEM, IEA/AGECC tiers); energy access sustainability; women's empowerment.
- *Level 2 — research focus:* Living Lab definitions, typology, international case studies (including Maputo, Siyakhula, RLabs, GENS), systems Living Labs, real-world testing, scaling and replication, gender-centric integration.
- *Level 3 — core variables:* systems thinking and systems engineering (SsT/SE), system dynamics, biogas technology, poor informal settlements.

**Block D — Conceptual framework (pp. 117–140).** Uses Miles & Huberman's (1984) antecedents/attributes/consequences structure; defines the Living Lab; presents Table 3.1 (SETILL structure), Figures 3.1–3.5 (conceptual framework, basic LL concept, stakeholder roadmap, rich picture, SETILL model); specifies five stakeholder groups; defines SETILL discursively.

**Block E — Methodology (pp. 141–186).** Constructionist-interpretivist paradigm; DRM (Blessing & Chakrabarti 2009) in four stages; grounded theory; community-based system dynamics and causal loop diagrams; two case studies — **Tzaneen (Limpopo, South Africa)** and **Umuahia (Abia State, Nigeria)**; trustworthiness (credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability); ethics; limitations.

**Block F — Proposal tail (pp. 187–193).** Expected contributions, limitations, a Gantt chart for **2027–2029**, ethics, a proposed seven-chapter TOC, a conclusion, and an **empty References heading**.
